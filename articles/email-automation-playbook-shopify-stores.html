The prerequisite nobody mentions: capture the address

Every automation below is a triggered email, and a triggered email needs an address. This sounds obvious until you look at a real checkout: a shopper who adds to cart as a guest and abandons before the email field is unrecoverable by email — there is nobody to send to. Two capture points fix most of it: the email field early in checkout (Shopify asks for it at the contact step for exactly this reason), and a popup or embedded form offering the first-order incentive. The honest mechanics: your recovery ceiling is your capture rate. A store capturing 30% of abandoners can only ever recover from that 30%. Fix capture before you optimize copy — no flow, however well-written, fires at an address you never collected. And one consent line, stated plainly: the popup must actually get permission. A pre-checked box is not consent, and a cart-recovery email to someone who never opted in is the fastest way to train spam filters against your domain.

The 7 flows, wired

Build order follows revenue per recipient, not the customer journey — the money flows first. Each flow: the trigger, the sequence, the branching gates.

1. The abandoned-cart flow (trigger: product added, checkout NOT started)

Trigger: a product enters the cart and no checkout starts within the window. Sequence: email one at +1 hour — a plain reminder, no discount, cart contents shown, one checkout link. Email two at +24 hours — the objection-handler: name the actual reasons people leave (Baymard 2025: extra costs 40%, slow delivery 20%, trust 19%, forced account 18%, long checkout 17%) and answer them — shipping cost stated up front, delivery time, reviews, guest checkout. Email three at +48 hours — the final nudge. The branching gates: anyone who purchases at any point exits immediately (the filter, not the email, does the work — exclude “placed order since started checkout”). Anyone who starts checkout mid-flow moves to flow #3 and stops receiving this one — running both flows at the same person is the double-send that teaches customers to ignore you. The discount rule, stated plainly: do not lead with a discount. Discounting in email one trains shoppers to abandon on purpose and wait for the code — operators who sequence discount-last consistently recover more. The incentive, if you use one, belongs in email three, and only there. The honest mechanics: Klaviyo’s 2025 benchmarks put this flow at $3.07 per recipient on average and $27.12 for the top 10% — a nearly 9× spread that is almost entirely execution quality (timing, filters, the no-discount-first discipline), not list size.

2. The abandoned-checkout flow (trigger: checkout started, no order)

Trigger: the shopper reaches checkout, enters at least the contact info, and leaves without ordering. Why it is a separate flow: this is a higher-intent audience than flow #1 — they typed their details, which means the objection is narrower (payment options, delivery time, total cost, trust) and the recovery math is better: one 14-brand per-flow cut puts entry-email conversion at 2.30% for checkout vs 1.82% for cart, at essentially identical revenue per recipient ($3.56 vs $3.55) — running only one of the two leaves the other’s revenue on the table. Sequence: email one at +1 hour — shorter than flow #1’s, because the shopper was seconds from buying: restate the total (no surprise costs — the 40% reason), the delivery date, the payment options. Email two at +24 hours — trust and urgency: reviews of the exact product, the returns policy in one line. The branching gates: SMS belongs here, not in flow #1 — you have the phone number from checkout, and the +1-hour window is where a one-line text nudge earns its keep (Brevo’s native SMS/WhatsApp is the bench reason this row exists). Purchasers exit instantly; cart-flow recipients who start checkout transfer here. The honest mechanics: the first email’s timing is the whole game — flow architects report recovery roughly halving when the first touch waits 24 hours instead of 1 (labeled as operator claim, directionally consistent with the benchmarks). Set the +1h email live before you wordsmith anything.

3. The welcome series, wired for commerce (trigger: signup via popup, footer, or checkout)

Trigger: a new subscriber who has not purchased. Sequence: email one at day 0 — the welcome: who you are, what you sell, the first-order incentive with the gate stated (the code, the minimum, the expiry — vague “10% off” with no terms trains the wait-for-code behavior from flow #1). Email two at day 2 — bestsellers and social proof: the products other customers buy, the reviews, the “start here” curation. Email three at day 5 — the last call on the incentive, then the series ends. The branching gates: anyone who purchases exits to flow #5 immediately — a welcome series that keeps selling to a new customer is how you look automated in the bad way. Source-branch the incentive: popup signups came for the discount (give it), checkout signups already buy (skip the discount, lead with curation). The one-automation ceiling, stated plainly: GetResponse Starter and Kit’s free plan each allow limited automation — Starter exactly one workflow, Kit free none — so on those tiers this is the flow that gets the slot and the cart flows wait for the upgrade. The honest mechanics: Klaviyo 2025 puts the welcome flow at $2.35 per recipient average, $20.92 top 10% — second only to cart recovery, because a new subscriber is the warmest non-buyer you will ever have.

4. Post-purchase: thank-you + shipping expectations + review engine (trigger: order fulfilled)

Trigger: the order ships. Sequence: email one at day 0 — the thank-you plus what-happens-next: when it ships, how to track it, what to do if something is wrong. This email has the highest open rate of anything you will send (it is about their package) — spend that attention on the second-order seed, not just logistics: the category cross-sell (“goes well with”), the care guide, the brand story in two sentences. Email two at day 7–14 after delivery — the review request: one product, one link, no friction. The branching gates: reviewers enter the referral/UGC track (review with photo earns the next incentive); non-reviewers get one reminder, not a drip. Support tickets suppress the cross-sell — selling to someone with an open complaint is the fastest way to turn a solvable problem into a one-star review. The honest mechanics: benchmarks put post-purchase at $0.38 per recipient and the review request at $0.50–2 — modest per-send, but the review is inventory: every collected review raises the conversion rate of the product page that flows #1–3 are trying to sell. The review request is the only flow here that pays you twice.

5. Browse abandonment (trigger: viewed product, no add-to-cart)

Trigger: a known subscriber views a product page and leaves without adding to cart. Sequence: one email at +24 hours — the product, social proof, the category’s bestsellers. That is the whole flow: one email, not a sequence. Why it is built fifth: the intent is the weakest of the three abandonment signals (no cart, no checkout), and the benchmarks agree — $0.95 per recipient average, $7.05 top 10%, the lowest of the abandonment family. It earns its place because it is nearly free once built, not because it is a money flow. The branching gates: anyone who adds to cart or starts checkout exits to flows #1/#2 immediately — this flow must never email someone who is already in a hotter flow. Frequency-cap it hard: a subscriber who browses ten products should get one email about the most-viewed, not ten emails. The tier note: browse abandonment is a behavioral trigger — on MailerLite it needs the Power tier ($25–39/mo), not Comfort. If you are on Comfort, this is the flow that justifies the upgrade — or the flow you skip until the first four are printing.

6. Win-back + replenishment (trigger: days since last order vs the product’s cycle)

Trigger: a past customer goes quiet past the product’s natural reorder point. The replenishment half: consumables get a time-based estimate — the skincare serum bought 60 days ago is running out now; the email says so, with the one-click reorder. Set the timing per SKU category, not one global “90 days” — a 30-day supplement and a 180-day filter on the same cadence is how replenishment emails feel like spam. The win-back half: non-consumable customers get the re-engagement at 120–180 days of silence: what is new, what changed, one honest reason to come back. The branching gates: the VIP branch — top 10% by lifetime value get the human touch (early access, the founder email, the phone number) instead of the discount; discounting your best customers is paying people who would have bought anyway. The sunset branch: 365 days with no order and no opens moves them off the active list — not deleted, but out of the weekly sends and into the quarterly + holiday-only cadence. The honest mechanics: benchmarks rank win-back low per-recipient ($1–4 range in operator cuts) because the audience is cold — but the list is already paid for, so the marginal cost is near zero. The sunset is list hygiene that pays for itself: several tools bill contacts, not engaged subscribers, and a dead tail costs real money every month.

7. Back-in-stock + price-drop alerts (trigger: product events, not behavior)

Trigger: a product event — the item is back, the price dropped — matched against the subscribers who asked to be told. Sequence: one email, fast: the product, the new status, the buy link. Speed matters more than copy — a back-in-stock email sent three days late sells to whoever is left. Why it is last and why it matters: this is the only flow triggered by your inventory rather than the customer’s behavior, which makes it the highest intent per-send in the playbook — every recipient raised their hand for exactly this product. Most stores never build it because it needs the product-event trigger wired to the email tool (the Shopify integration’s actual sync depth — the thing the vertical guide audits per tool — is what makes or breaks this flow). The branching gates: cap alerts per subscriber (three back-in-stock emails in a week is a restock firehose, not a service); price-drop alerts go only to viewers/abandoners of that product, never the whole list — a site-wide “prices dropped” blast trains the wait-for-sale behavior from flow #1. The honest mechanics: this flow has no Klaviyo benchmark row because it is too store-specific to average — judge it on its own placed-order rate, and expect it to be the highest on the account.

The build order, with the money math

Build in this order: the two abandonment flows (flows #1 and #2), the welcome series, post-purchase, browse abandonment, win-back + replenishment, back-in-stock. The operator ranking by revenue per recipient — cart $8–15, welcome $3–8, post-purchase $2–5, browse $1–4, review $0.50–2, sunset $0.10–0.50 (Ultra Marketing Group, mature accounts) — is the priority list; journey order is not. One line of judgment, labeled: most stores build the welcome series first because it is the easiest to understand, and leave the cart flows — the two highest-RPR flows on the account — for “later,” which is the most expensive procrastination in e-commerce email.

Worked example — a 2,500-contact store on MailerLite Comfort

2,500 contacts, ~800 checkout starts a month, roughly 70% abandoning (Baymard’s average, directional for any single store). The two abandonment flows fire at the captured subset — say ~400 cart/checkout-flow recipients a month after capture losses. At Klaviyo’s $3.07 average revenue per recipient, that is ~$1,228 a month in recovered revenue against a $19 Comfort bill — benchmark math, not a promise, and your capture rate is the multiplier that matters most. At top-10% execution ($27.12 RPR) the same volume is ~$10,848 — the 9× spread is the price of the timing, filter, and no-discount-first discipline in flows #1 and #2. Now the send math that decides the tier: 2,500 contacts × ~12 emails a month (weekly broadcasts + flow volume) = ~30,000 sends — against Comfort’s 10× cap of 25,000. The cap binds. This store either trims cadence or steps to Power ($25–39/mo) — the single most expensive surprise in the MailerLite pricing page, and exactly why the table above says to run the math first. On Brevo’s send-based billing the same volume prices by sends, not contacts — model it before you assume Comfort is cheaper.

Five mistakes that cost more than the tool

  • One cart flow instead of two. Cart abandonment (added product, never started checkout) and checkout abandonment (entered info, left) are different intents, different objections, different timing — and per the 14-brand cut, nearly identical revenue per recipient. Merging them into one sequence leaves half the recovery on the table.
  • Leading with the discount. The code in email one trains shoppers to abandon deliberately. Discount-last is not a tactic; it is the difference between recovering carts and manufacturing them.
  • Judging flows on open rates. Apple’s Mail Privacy Protection has inflated every open rate since 2021 — Klaviyo says so on page 7 of its own benchmark report. Judge flows on clicks, placed orders, and revenue per recipient, or you will optimize for a number that stopped meaning anything.
  • Building flows before fixing capture. The best-written cart flow in the world fires at zero people if the email field sits behind the abandonment point. Capture rate is the ceiling; everything else is interior design.
  • Skipping the sunset. The 365-day dead tail costs money on contact-billed plans and drags down deliverability everywhere. Cadence, not deletion — quarterly + holiday-only — but the weekly sends stop.
