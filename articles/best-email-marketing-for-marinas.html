A marina does not sell dock space — it sells the renewal. The economics say it plainly: annual slip rentals run $2,500 to $15,000 a year depending on the boat and the water (one 2026 roundup; covered slips carry a 20-30% premium), published boards show a 50-footer at $800/month month-to-month or $700 annual — $8,400 a year for one slip — and transient rates hit $4.00 per foot per night in season. The slip-holder is the recurring-revenue customer, and the renewal is the whole game: on Long Island, town marina slips are $120-$130 per foot per year and the waitlists for larger boats run five to six years — when a slip-holder leaves, the waitlist fills the hole, but only if the marina is still running the list. The second machine is the off-season service lane: basic winterization runs $180 to $800 depending on the engine, shrink wrap $16-$30 per foot, indoor storage $34 per foot — and northern shops fill up right after Labor Day. Email is the machine that runs all of it: the renewal runway that locks in the annual contracts, the fall winterization rush that books the shop, the transient capture that turns a one-night Dockwa guest into an annual slip-holder, and the fuel-and-service upsell that monetizes every visit. Here is how the best tool runs it.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The slip-renewal runway. The renewal is not a bill — it is a 90-day campaign. The machinery: the 90-day reminder (the contract renewal date is on the calendar, not in someone's head — the email lands while the boat is still in the water), the 60-day value email ("what your slip fee bought this year" — the dock work, the dredging, the events; the customer who sees the value renews), the 30-day deadline (the invoice, the payment link, the waitlist pressure — on competitive water, "your slip goes to the waitlist on December 1" is honest scarcity, because the waitlists really are five years deep in places), the lapsed-holder rescue (the customer who doesn't renew gets the "what would bring you back" sequence — and the waitlist gets the "a slip opened" email in the same week). Scored on the tools: date-triggered sequences, payment-reminder templates, and waitlist segmentation.
  2. The winterization service lane. The fall rush is the marina's second business: winterization, shrink wrap, storage, spring commissioning — the service lane books the shop while the slips are still paying. The machinery: the Labor Day announcement (northern shops fill up right after Labor Day — the email that says "booking now open" on September 1 is the one that fills October), the pricing menu (published boards run $550 for an inboard winterization, $20-$22/ft for shrink wrap, $34/ft for indoor storage, $375 pickup within 25 miles — the email with the real menu books; the email that says "call for prices" doesn't), the scheduling engine (October slots are the product — the email with the calendar link sells itself), the commissioning reversal (spring: the "launch slots are open" email, same list, same machine), and the storage-upsell ladder (outdoor at $27/ft vs indoor at $34/ft — the email that explains the $7/ft difference in one photo sells the upgrade). Scored on the tools: seasonal launch templates, deadline-driven sequences, service-menu rich templates.
  3. The transient-boater capture. Transients are the trial users of the marina business: they book through Dockwa, stay a night or two, and leave — unless the marina runs the capture. The machinery: the pre-arrival email (one boater's review calls a marina's morning-of-departure email — slip number, hailing channel, dock code, WiFi password — the best email from a marina he's ever read; that level of pre-arrival care is the standard), the during-stay touch (the fuel-dock offer, the restaurant coupon, the "extend a night" prompt — the $6/ft/day transient rate makes the extend-a-night math easy), the post-stay upgrade offer (the transient who stayed three nights this season gets the annual offer before the season closes — and the email knows their boat's length, because the reservation did), the cruiser lane (the boats passing through on the loop or the migration — they come back the same week next year, and the email arrives two weeks before they do). Scored on the tools: transactional + marketing in one place, boat-length segmentation, and location/route segmentation.
  4. The fuel-and-service upsell. The fuel dock is the visit engine: every boat that buys fuel walks past the store, the restaurant, and the service desk. The machinery: the slip-holder fuel price (the published norm at one club marina — transients pay one rate, slip-holders a better one — and the email that says so is the retention email), the service-reminder lane (100-hour service, bottom paint season, impeller age — the marina knows the boat's service history if it keeps the records; the email is the reminder), the ship's-store seasons (the line that sells out every June — the email sells it before the shelf empties), the restaurant lane (the transient dining room, the slip-holder taco night — the events email is the foot-traffic machine). Scored on the tools: segmentation (slip-holder vs transient vs boatyard), and promo-template speed (the Friday "weekend special" email goes out Thursday).
  5. The community engine (the waitlist moat). The marina's moat is not the docks — it is the waiting list and the community that refuses to leave. The machinery: the waitlist nurture (the applicant paying $100 to sit on a 5-year list is the most patient buyer in boating — the annual "you're #14, here's what's moving" email keeps them warm and honest), the events calendar (dock parties, fishing tournaments, the commodore's cruise — the emails that make the marina a club, not a parking lot), the referral engine (slip-holders know boaters — the "refer a boater, get a month of slip fees" email is the cheapest acquisition in the business), and the off-season check-in (the winter email with the yard update — the slip-holder who sees the winter work done renews in the spring). Scored on the tools: event templates, referral flows, and long-horizon nurtures.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first — in this order:

  1. The renewal runway. Trigger: renewal date minus 90 days. Sequence: the 90-day value email ("what your slip fee bought this year" — the dock work, the events, the dredging), the 60-day reminder with the renewal terms, the 30-day invoice with the payment link and the waitlist note ("your slip goes to the waitlist on the deadline"), the 7-day final call, the lapsed-rescue sequence for non-renewers. The rule: the renewal is won in the 90 days BEFORE the invoice, not in the 7 days after.
  2. The winterization rush. Trigger: Labor Day. Sequence: the "booking now open" blast on September 1 (the boards agree northern shops fill right after Labor Day), the pricing-menu email (the real numbers — winterization $450-$550 by engine, shrink wrap $20-$25/ft, storage $27-$34/ft, pickup $375 — the menu books, the "call for prices" doesn't), the October-slot-scarcity email (the calendar screenshot is the creative), the commissioning reversal in spring ("launch slots are open" — same list, same machine).
  3. The transient capture. Trigger: the Dockwa reservation. Sequence: the pre-arrival email (slip number, hailing channel, dock code, WiFi password — the email one boater called the best from any marina he's read), the arrival-day welcome with the extend-a-night offer (transient math: $4/ft/night in season makes the extra night an easy yes), the post-stay annual offer (the 3-night guest gets the annual math before the season closes — the email knows the boat length because the reservation did), the cruiser-lane rebook (the migrating boat gets the email two weeks before it's back).
  4. The service-reminder lane. Trigger: the boat's service record (100 hours, impeller age, bottom-paint date). Sequence: the "your boat is due" email (the marina that keeps the records owns the service work), the bottom-paint season blast, the haul-out day confirmation, the spring splash email. The rule: the service lane is the margin lane — the slip is the rent, the service is the profit.
  5. The waitlist nurture. Trigger: the waitlist application. Sequence: the annual position email ("you're #14 — here's what's moved"), the opened-slip flash (the lapsed holder's slip goes to the list first — the email that fills a hole in a week), the events invitations (the waitlisted boater who comes to the dock party renews their application), the referral ask (slip-holders know boaters — the referral email is the cheapest acquisition in the business).

The honest catches

  • Email is not a booking system. Reservations live in Dockwa (or your marina-management software); the invoice lives in accounting. The email's job is the communication around the booking — the pre-arrival, the reminder, the rebook — not the booking itself. Don't shop for an email tool by asking "does it take reservations"; it shouldn't.
  • Storm-alert SMS needs real consent. Brevo's pocket superpower only works if every slip-holder opted in. "We have your number on file" is not consent for marketing texts — but a storm warning to a slip-holder's boat is exactly the kind of message they want. Collect the consent at renewal (a checkbox on the renewal form), and keep the SMS for the things that matter: storms, haul-out days, slot openings.
  • Your list churns when boats sell. A slip-holder who sells their boat doesn't unsubscribe — they just stop opening. Prune annually: the customer who hasn't opened in 12 months AND whose slip renewed to someone else is not a subscriber, they're a ghost. A marina list that never prunes rots from the inside.
  • Seasonal volume breaks per-email math. Brevo looks cheap until the September-to-April season has you sending weekly events + the winterization launches + the renewal runway to 2,000 contacts. Model the whole season, not the quiet month.

FAQ

How big does a marina's email list need to be?
Bigger than you think, smaller than a retailer: a few hundred slip-holders, a few hundred waitlisted applicants, the season's transients, and the service customers. The list is worth more per subscriber than almost any business — each slip-holder is an $8,000-a-year customer.

Do transient boaters join the email list?
If you ask at the right moment — yes. The pre-arrival email is the ask: "get the marina updates and the annual offer." The transient who refuses is still a review and a referral. The one who joins is next year's slip-holder.

What's a good slip renewal rate?
Marinas don't publish this the way SaaS publishes churn, so treat it as your own benchmark: healthy marinas renew most of their slips most years, and the waitlist covers the gaps. If your renewals are falling and the waitlist isn't backfilling, the problem is the 90 days before the invoice — not the invoice.

Should the marina email transients the same things as slip-holders?
No — and this is where the segments pay. Slip-holders get the renewal runway, the community calendar, and the service reminders. Transients get the pre-arrival, the extend-a-night, and the annual offer. One list, two lanes, zero confusion.
