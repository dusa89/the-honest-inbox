The 2 foundational flows (build these first)

The welcome series converts signups into relationships; the impact loop converts first gifts into second ones. First-time donor retention sits at 19.4% (Fundraising Effectiveness Project, 2024 report, via keela.co’s vendor-compiled benchmark — directional) — four out of five first-time donors never give again. These two flows are the only honest answer to that number. Build them before anything clever.

1. The new-subscriber welcome series, wired (day 0 / day 3 / day 7, branching on source)

Trigger: a form submit — connect-card signup, website form, event signup. Sequence: email one (day 0): the welcome — mission story, what the organization does with money, what the subscriber should expect. Email two (day 3): the impact email — the story of the money at work, told with one person, not statistics. Email three (day 7): the soft first ask — only to the branch that engaged email two. The branching gates (the wiring the guide never does): the trigger tags the source at signup — Sunday connect-card signups are the congregant lane, website signups the community lane, petition or advocacy signups the issue lane. The day-7 ask goes only to subscribers who opened email two; non-openers get a second impact email instead of an ask — asking the unengaged is how you train new subscribers to ignore you. The honest mechanics: the sequence only works because the ask is conditional. The guide’s tool-agnostic version names the mission → money → ask progression; the playbook adds the conditional gate that makes it convert. The one-automation ceiling, stated plainly: GetResponse Starter and Kit’s free plan each allow exactly one automation workflow — this is the one it should be. If the welcome series is your only automation, it has to do double duty: include the volunteer-team signup link in email three, and the impact-loop will have to wait for the paid tier.

2. The first-gift thank-you + impact loop (day 0 receipt-thanks → day 30 impact update → day 90 second ask)

Trigger: a donation event — from the donation platform, into the email tool via Zapier or a native integration (Donorbox syncs via Zapier per Donorbox’s own nonprofit roundup). Sequence: email one (immediately): the receipt-thanks — receipt first, gratitude second, one job, one email. Email two (day 30): the impact update — “here’s what your gift did,” one story, one number. Email three (day 90): the second ask — the honest fundraising ask, referencing the impact they already funded. The gift-size branching (where this becomes a playbook, not a drip): small gifts (“$25 and under” is a reasonable band — set yours, then adjust) stay on the loop and become eligible for the monthly-conversion engine in flow #6. Mid-range gifts get the full impact loop plus the annual-report send. Large gifts trigger a human handoff — the automation tags the contact and assigns the thank-you call to a board member or staffer, then stops. Automating a thank-you to a $5,000 donor is how you keep the $5,000 donor for exactly one year. The honest mechanics: overall donor retention is 42.9% (FEP 2024); first-time is 19.4%. The gap between those numbers is the thank-you — the guide said the second gift is won in the thank-you, not the ask; the playbook wires the loop that actually wins it. Every day past day 90 without a second ask is a donor drifting toward the 80.6% who don’t return.

The 3 retention flows

3. The RSVP-triggered event chain (not the blast)

Trigger: a registration — not the announcement email. Sequence: email one (14 days out): the confirmation — date, time, parking, what to bring. Email two (3 days out): the logistics email — the schedule, the ask (“bring a friend” for churches, “share the auction link” for galas). Email three (day of): the doors-open message. Email four (day 1 after): the thank-you + share-your-experience ask. The wiring the guide never does: this fires on registrants, not the whole list — most nonprofits blast the sequence to everyone, and the people who never registered get three reminders for an event they ignored. The registration form is the trigger; the broadcast list gets one announcement, not the chain. The attendee vs volunteer branches: volunteers who registered for the event get a different day-of message (the shift briefing, the point person, the check-in location) — sending volunteers the “doors open at 7!” message is how you announce that nobody thought about them. Branch on the volunteer tag at registration; the chain splits and both halves stay personal. The post-event email is the intake funnel: for nonprofits, post-event follow-up is what review automation is for salons — one flow, every event, forever, and the share-your-experience ask is the cheapest list growth in the vertical.

4. The lapsed-donor annual re-engagement (one honest email, then the nonprofit sunset)

Trigger: 12 months since the last gift. Sequence: one email. Not a drip campaign — the guide said this explicitly, and the playbook agrees: one honest email, one ask, one clean list after. The email is personal in tone and specific in content: what changed this year, what the donor funded last time, one ask. The branch after (the wiring): a re-gift drops the donor back into the impact loop (flow #2, day 30). No gift moves them to the annual-only cadence — Giving Tuesday and the year-end series only, four emails a year instead of twelve. Another 12 months with no gift, and they enter the nonprofit version of a sunset: they stay on the list (donors are relationships, not email addresses), but they move to the quarterly mission update + year-end only. The honest mechanics: the sunset for nonprofits is cadence, not deletion — unlike e-commerce, where the unengaged get suppressed, a donor who gave three years ago is one good year away from returning. The annual cycle is the automation: every donor, once a year, one honest email, one branch. And the list hygiene that pays for it: before this flow goes live, suppress bounced and unsubscribed addresses — several tools bill contacts, not active subscribers, and a 15-year-old member list with thousands of dead addresses costs real money on every plan here.

5. The volunteer onboarding drip (the rotating-cast continuity)

Trigger: a volunteer form signup. Sequence: email one (day 0): the orientation email — the point person, the schedule, what to expect at the first shift, who to ask for. Email two (day 7, after the first shift): the check-in — “how did the first shift go?” with the feedback reply prompt. Email three (day 30): the belonging email — the story of what volunteers built this month, the invitation to the next role. Email four (day 90): the milestone thank-you — the hour count if you track it, the public acknowledgment path (church bulletin, donor newsletter), the next step. The branching: branch by ministry or team at signup — the food-pantry volunteer and the worship-team volunteer have nothing in common operationally, and one generic “thanks for volunteering” drip serves neither. The honest mechanics: this is the playbook’s answer to the guide’s “volunteer problem,” but on the other side of it. The guide solves the admin side — the org-owned account, the 5-minute handoff doc, the discount renewal on the calendar. This flow solves the volunteer side: the rotating cast is the problem, and the automation is the continuity. Volunteers who feel briefed and acknowledged at 90 days don’t rotate; volunteers who got a clipboard and silence do. The Blackbaud donor-tree observation holds double for volunteers — their own compiled research notes that supporters who volunteer are significantly more likely to donate (their data, directional) — so the volunteer drip is also a donor pipeline. Tag every volunteer; flow #6 watches them.

The 2 growth flows

6. The one-time-to-monthly conversion engine (the highest-leverage automation in nonprofit email)

Trigger: the second gift, or day 60–90 after the first gift for engaged non-givers. Sequence: email one: the upgrade ask — not a discount, a frame: “$15/mo is the food pantry’s Tuesday budget.” The monthly ask works on translation, not urgency — the donor has to see the month in the mission. Email two (non-converters, day 14): the proof email — what the monthly program funded last quarter, one story, one number. Then the evergreen half: the monthly giver enters a different universe — the annual impact report send, the upgrade path (the $15/mo donor at 12 months gets the “would $25 reach twice as far” ask), and the annual gratitude email that isn’t an ask at all. The failed-payment dunning (the boring automation that saves the program): expired and declined cards are the #1 silent killer of monthly giving programs — the donors didn’t leave, their cards did. Three emails: the soft notice (day 1, “your card needs a quick update” with the one-click update link), the direct ask (day 7), the last call (day 14, before the subscription lapses). One line of judgment, labeled: recover the donor, not the card — the dunning emails thank first and ask second, or the update feels like collections. The honest mechanics: this flow exists because the numbers are the strongest in the vertical. Monthly recurring donors give 42% more per year than one-time donors (Network for Good data, via GiveSmart — vendor-tracked, directional), retain at roughly 90% (Blackbaud’s donor tree, their data), and generated 2.75X the annual value of nonrecurring donors in RKD Group’s 2025 GivingTuesday analysis (their data — directional). The monthly-conversion engine is why flow #2 branches small gifts here — the second gift is the trigger, and the monthly ask is the destination.

7. The Giving Tuesday + year-end appeal sequence (the annual calendar automation)

Trigger: the calendar — this is the only flow in the playbook that runs on dates rather than behavior, which is why it’s built last: it compounds on everything else, it doesn’t replace it. Sequence: Giving Tuesday: one warm-up email the day before (“tomorrow’s the day — here’s the one thing your gift funds”), the day-of appeal (morning: the launch; the guide’s one-automation-ceiling note applies — on the cheapest tiers this is a manual broadcast, and that’s fine, it’s one day a year). The year-end series: December 26 (“the year in review — what your giving built”), December 29 (the direct appeal), December 31 (the deadline email — the tax-year deadline is the honest urgency; use it plainly, don’t manufacture more). Then January: the thank-you + impact tease — the December givers enter flow #2’s impact loop, and the loop now has a story to tell. The honest mechanics: December money is deadline money, and the calendar doesn’t negotiate — the flow exists because the organization that improvises in December raises less than the one that built this in October. Giving Tuesday 2024 pulled $3.6B in the US (+16% year over year, 36.1M participants, 18.5M financial donors — GivingTuesday Data Commons estimates, via The NonProfit Times); 2025 hit a record $4B (Data Commons, via funraise.org). That money went to the nonprofits whose appeal was already written. One line of judgment, labeled: Giving Tuesday is a campaign, not a strategy — the warm-up and the day-of raise the year’s biggest one-day total, but flow #6 is what keeps the December donors giving in March.

The send math: the 2,500-contact nonprofit worked example

Worked example — a nonprofit with 2,500 contacts on MailerLite Comfort with the 30% nonprofit discount (~$13.30/mo at 1k — check your band at checkout; Comfort’s paid tier runs real automations): the weekly bulletin to 2,500 = ~10,750 emails/mo; the welcome series on ~40 new signups (120 emails); the impact loop on ~25 gifts a month (75); the event chain on ~3 events a quarter (a few hundred); the volunteer drip on ~15 signups (60); the lapsed-donor annual flow and the year-end series only in their months. Total: roughly 11,500–12,500 emails a month — comfortably inside any paid tier here. On GetResponse Starter (~$9.50/mo discounted, unlimited sends), the send math never binds — but Starter allows exactly one automation, so the tier question is workflows, not sends: budget Marketer (~$29.50/mo discounted) if you want all seven flows live at once. Your volume will differ; add your broadcasts plus drip sends plus event volume before you pick the tier. And the discount-first rule from the vertical guide: start with the discount application, not the trial — every discount on this page is claimed before or during signup, and building on the full-price plan then applying later is the mistake that costs the first quarter’s savings.

The discount-renewal calendar: the automation everyone forgets to build

Before you buy anything, open a calendar and write three entries. GetResponse’s 50% discount runs on a fixed validity term (independent coverage reports 12–24 months — confirm the current duration at signup, because it isn’t forever) — the renewal date goes on the calendar with a 30-day lead. MailerLite’s 30% doesn’t expire, but the proof of status must stay current — the re-verification date goes on the calendar. Constant Contact’s 30% is a 12-month prepay — the prepay anniversary goes on the calendar, because the budget conversation happens before the charge, not after. A lapsed discount is an unbudgeted price increase, and the organization that discovers it on the invoice has already paid it. This is the cheapest automation in the playbook and it runs on a calendar, not an email tool. The 4 mistakes that kill nonprofit email

  • Calling one automation “automated.” GetResponse Starter and Kit’s free plan each allow exactly one automation workflow — that’s the welcome series, and the other six flows are manual broadcasts. Building the welcome series and declaring the playbook done is the ceiling, not the floor. Budget the upgrade from day one.
  • Blasting the event sequence to the whole list. The event chain fires on registrations, not broadcasts. The people who never registered get one announcement, not three reminders. Registration is the trigger — the email tool needs the registration data (form or integration), or the chain is a blast wearing an automation costume.
  • Treating the volunteer list like the donor list. Volunteers need the orientation drip (flow #5), not the fundraising asks. The rotating-cast problem is solved by continuity automation on the volunteer side and the handoff doc on the admin side — one without the other is half a system.
  • Skipping the failed-payment dunning. Monthly programs die silently on expired cards — the donors didn’t leave, their cards did. Three recovery emails (day 1, day 7, day 14) is the cheapest revenue in the vertical, and it’s the flow everyone forgets because it isn’t glamorous.
