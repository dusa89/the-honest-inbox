Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. The post-job warranty drip. The repaired refrigerator has a 90-day parts-and-labor promise behind it — and the customer who trusted you with a $200–$300 fridge repair is your warmest audience for the next 90 days. The sequence: the thank-you the morning after (with the warranty terms restated — the paper that prevents the "you said this was covered" argument), the day-90 check-in ("warranty window closing — everything still running quiet?"), and the review ask attached to the happy silence. Estimates die on counters; warranty drips turn a $179 ticket into the household's forever appliance guy.
  2. The repair-vs-replace decision machine. Every appliance customer asks one question — "should I fix it or buy a new one?" The trade's honest answer is the 50% rule: repair when the fix costs under 50–60% of replacement and the unit is under roughly 10–15 years old (property-manager portfolio data, 2026). The email version: an age-graded series (the 7-year-old dishwasher, the 12-year-old dryer) that does the math out loud — a $400 quote on a $1,100 fridge versus the haul-away, delivery, and install chaos. The shop that answers this question honestly wins the job it recommends AND the replacement referral when the math fails.
  3. The maintenance-membership engine. The trade's recurring-revenue playbook is real and priced: $12–$20 a month or $150–$250 a year buys the annual visit — condenser coils, dryer-vent safety check, filter and gasket run-through (appliancemarketingpros 2026 pricing breakdown). At 45 minutes and roughly $85 all-in cost per visit, the membership prints margin and, better, it plants your tech in the kitchen once a year to see the aging compressor before it dies. The email version: the member calendar (annual visit scheduling), the non-member conversion sequence (the $179-average-repair-vs-membership math email), and the renewal drip.
  4. The seasonal load calendar. Appliances have a calendar and it is honest demand: May is fridge-coil prep before the summer heat wave (the compressor that dies in July was begging in May), March is dryer-vent fire-safety season (the vent that hasn't been cleaned in two years is a fire hazard and a slow-dry complaint), and September–October is the pre-Thanksgiving oven-and-dishwasher tune-up (the oven that limps through the holiday dinner was a warning all fall). Three seasonal blasts a year catch the appliances before they die — and the repair you prevent sells the membership.
  5. The property-manager drip. The commercial buyer is worth twenty homeowners: the manager with 40 units doesn't care about your oven gallery — she cares about the unit-turn appliance readiness email, the documented diagnostic invoice, and the repair-vs-replace math at portfolio scale (California rental managers run the same 50–60% rule; the replacement carries $200–$600 in haul-away/install coordination plus lost rent). The engine: the monthly B2B touch — current capacity, one useful line, segmented away from consumers entirely. The drip is the relationship; the standing account is the payoff.

The honest catches

  • The 4-hour arrival window wins the job, not the drip. Email keeps the customer for life, but same-day service with a tight arrival window wins the repair call — the shop that answers and arrives beats the shop with the prettier follow-up. Automate the after-care, not the dispatch.
  • Your list import is the real setup cost. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting the customer list and tagging it properly — segment (residential/landlord/commercial), appliance (fridge/washer/dryer/dishwasher/oven), membership status (member/non-member) (estimate — most dispatch exports won't have all of these out of the box). Budget an afternoon, not ten minutes. A list without appliance tags can't run the decision machine or the seasonal calendar — the tags are the product. Our deliverability audit checklist covers the list-hygiene half.
  • Email can't fix a 1.8-star reputation. The warranty drip multiplies what the vans deliver — a company with 1.8 stars will automate its way to more detailed complaints, and the tech who scratched the floor gets photographed. Fix the service first, then automate the ask.
  • Never relay a recall you haven't verified. Manufacturer recalls are a trust goldmine for the shop that relays them correctly — and a trust landmine for the shop that blasts one that doesn't apply. Verify the model numbers against the manufacturer's recall notice before the email leaves, every time.

How often should an appliance repair company email past customers?

Three seasonal blasts a year — the March dryer-vent safety campaign, the May fridge-coil prep, the September pre-Thanksgiving tune-up — plus the warranty gratitude drip after every repair and the membership renewal calendar. Members get one useful line a month; past customers don't need a newsletter — they need the warranty terms, the number on the fridge, and the email that arrives before the appliance dies. The property-manager segment is different: monthly, short, useful.

Should maintenance-plan members and one-time customers be on the same list?

Same tool, different segments — not different accounts. The warranty drip branches by membership status (the member gets the "already covered" version; the non-member gets the membership math email), and the campaigns run on separate calendars. Tagging handles it; one account keeps the list, the automations, and the membership engine in one place.
