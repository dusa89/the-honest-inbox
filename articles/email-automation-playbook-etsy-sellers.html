Etsy sellers don’t have a traffic problem — they have a one-time-buyer problem. Etsy brings the first sale; nothing brings the second except you. That job runs on 7 automations: the four flows every shop should build first — the new-buyer welcome, the abandoned-cart rescue, the post-purchase care email, and the review request (three of which our Etsy vertical guide names but never wires up) — plus three that separate a shop from a storefront: lapsed-buyer win-back, new-drop announcements to past buyers, and the VIP repeat-buyer flow. The 4 baseline flows (build these first)

These are the flows the second sale comes from. Build them before anything clever. One rule governs all four, and it’s the Etsy rule: a buyer’s email address is not your marketing list. Etsy requires express consent before you market to a buyer — buying a thing from your shop is not consent, and unsolicited advertising through Etsy Messages is prohibited outright. Your list is built off the platform (package inserts with QR codes to a landing page are the engine — see the capture system in the vertical guide). Every flow below fires from that list, not from your order history.

1. The new-buyer welcome (3 emails, 7 days)

Trigger: opt-in — the package-insert QR, the shop-banner link, the social bio landing page. Sequence: three emails over 7 days: the maker story (one paragraph — Etsy buyers buy the maker, not the marketplace; a faceless “thanks for subscribing” converts nothing), the care or how-to-use guide for what they bought (this email gets forwarded to friends more than any other in the playbook — our estimate, and it’s the one that teaches a buyer to use what they own), and the first-repeat coupon (10% off the next order, 30-day expiry). The honest mechanics: this is not Etsy’s order-confirmation email — Etsy sends that. Yours adds the story and the reason to come back.

2. The abandoned-cart rescue (2 emails: 30 minutes, 24 hours)

Trigger: cart abandoned by a known contact. Sequence: email one within 30 minutes — the gentle “still thinking it over?” with the product image; email two at 24 hours with the nudge that addresses the #1 abandonment reason (unexpected extra costs, cited by 48% of abandoners — Baymard, via ringly.io 2026) — state the shipping cost plainly in this email. The numbers: abandoned-cart email flows average a 50.5% open rate and a 3.33% placed-order rate, with the top tenth reaching 7.69% (Klaviyo benchmark of 143,000 flows, via flowium.com, 2026); the average cart abandonment rate is 70.19% (Baymard, via ringly.io 2026); and across 1,000 brands, the first message sent within 30 minutes converts best (Rejoiner data, via periscale.app). Two honest catches. First, you can only email the carts you have addresses for — Etsy favoriters who never opted in are unreachable by you, so this flow’s power scales with your capture system. Second, Etsy sends its own cart nudges — yours is the customizable layer (your voice, your shipping-cost honesty, your coupon), not a duplicate.

3. The post-purchase thank-you + care email (day of delivery)

Trigger: order marked delivered. Action: one email — the genuine thank-you, the care instructions for handmade goods, and the “here’s what to do if anything’s wrong” line with a direct reply path. This flow’s real job is defensive: a buyer who knows how to care for the item and where to write with a problem doesn’t open a case — they email you. It also quietly teaches the buyer that your emails are worth opening, which is what makes flow #4 work. Keep it short; the care guide can live on your site with a link.

4. The review request (delivery + 5–7 days, no incentive, ever)

Trigger: delivery plus enough time to use the item — 5 to 7 days for most goods. Action: one email asking for a review, with the direct link. The hard rule, stated plainly: do not offer anything in exchange for a review. Etsy’s house rules classify a seller offering a buyer “additional goods, services, or compensation” in exchange for a positive review as extortion — it is prohibited (etsy.com house rules). The review window opens when you mark the order shipped and runs 100 days from the delivery date; one review per order; buyers only; reviews are editable within the window (help.etsy.com). So the ask must be naked: no discount for a review, no free gift for a review, no entry into a drawing for a review. The honest consolation: a clean, well-timed ask after a good unboxing converts better than a bribed one, and it can’t get your shop flagged.

The 3 advanced automations

5. Lapsed-buyer win-back (90 days, category-segmented)

Trigger: past buyer with no purchase in 90 days. Action: one email — “we miss you” plus the incentive, segmented by what they bought (the printables buyer gets the new printable bundle; the jewelry buyer gets the new collection). Etsy’s repeat-purchase cycle is the whole business for most shops — a buyer who bought once already trusts you with money, which is the hardest trust to earn. This is the only automation in the playbook that benefits from purchase-category tagging — tag at import (or by hand for small shops) and the segmentation writes itself.

6. New-drop announcements to past buyers (per drop)

Trigger: your calendar — the new product line, the seasonal collection, the restock of the thing that sells out. Action: past buyers get 48-hour early access before the listing goes public, then the remainder of the list gets the announcement. Etsy’s own marketing promotes the marketplace; nobody promotes your drop except you. The early-access window is the mechanic that makes past buyers feel like insiders instead of an audience — and insiders buy on day one, which is what pushes a new listing into Etsy’s own rankings.

7. The VIP repeat-buyer flow (3rd purchase)

Trigger: third purchase. Action: a personal-feeling thank-you (write it like you mean it, because you should), plus early access to drops and a standing perk — free shipping on the next order, or first refusal on one-of-a-kind pieces. The 3-time buyer is your cheapest acquisition channel: they need no convincing, only a reason to come back first to you instead of browsing. The honest mechanics: this flow needs purchase counts in your email tool — tag buyers at import or keep a simple spreadsheet-to-segment routine monthly. For shops under a few hundred buyers, the manual version takes 20 minutes a month.

The send math: the small-list worked example

Worked example — an Etsy shop with 800 contacts on MailerLite Comfort ($19/mo at the 1,000-contact band, send cap roughly 10× the list = ~10,000 emails/mo): two campaigns a month = 1,600 emails. The drip volume (welcomes, cart rescues, post-purchase, review requests) = ~1,000–1,500. Total: roughly 2,600–3,100 emails a month — under a third of the cap, with room to double the list before the next price band. Flodesk Everything ($54/mo flat, unlimited) is overkill at 800 contacts but the math flips past ~5,000. Kit’s free plan stores the whole list at $0 and sends unlimited broadcasts — but the moment you want the automations, it’s Creator at $39/mo, which is why the vertical guide calls it the digital-product seller’s pick and the playbook prices it honestly. Your volume will differ; add your campaigns plus drip sends before you pick the tier.

The Etsy-platform question: what Etsy sends vs what only you can send

Before you build a single flow, look at what Etsy already sends: order confirmations, shipping updates, its own abandoned-cart nudges, and marketplace marketing emails. The honest division of labor: Etsy owns the transactional layer because it holds the checkout; your email tool owns the relationship layer because Etsy will never tell your maker story, ask for your reviews on your timing, or announce your drops to your past buyers first. The seam is purchase data: flows #5 and #7 need to know who bought what and how often, and that data lives in Etsy — tag at import (CSV) or by hand for small shops. Sellers who duplicate Etsy’s confirmations in their own tool send double emails and train buyers to ignore both. Wire the seam once, and the two systems stop fighting.

The 4 mistakes that kill Etsy-seller email

  • Building on the wrong tier. Flodesk Lite gives you one workflow for a seven-flow playbook — and even Pro doesn’t unlock the abandoned-cart automations; that’s Everything money. Kit’s free plan covers your whole list and none of your automations. GetResponse Starter gives you one workflow. Brevo free gives you zero automations and a 300-a-day cap. Every flow built on a tier that can’t run it is a rebuild. Pick the tier from the table above, then build.
  • Incentivizing reviews. The discount-for-a-review trick is extortion under Etsy’s house rules — prohibited, full stop. The ask must be naked: no compensation, no drawing, no free gift. A clean ask 5–7 days after delivery, with the direct link, is the whole technique.
  • Mailing your order history like it’s a list. Etsy requires express consent before marketing to a buyer — the purchase is not consent, and unsolicited advertising through Etsy Messages is prohibited. The package-insert QR to a landing page is the engine; the order CSV is not a list. One consent complaint costs more than the insert cards ever will.
  • The generic welcome. Etsy buyers bought the maker, not the marketplace — a “thanks for subscribing” email converts nothing. The welcome’s first email is the maker story in one paragraph, the second is the care guide they’ll forward to a friend, the third is the reason to come back. Personality is the product here; the tool is just the envelope.
