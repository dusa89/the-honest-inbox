A liquor store is not a wine club and not a taproom — it is a hunter's market with a cash register. The winery owns its product and ships its own club boxes; the taproom pours its own beer. The liquor store gets someone else's bottle in limited cases, sells it in days, and the only machine that decides who gets it is the list. This is real, documented store machinery: Tony's Fresh Market runs a formal "Bourbon Allocation Email" list that notifies subscribers of every allocation and gives them a chance to sign up and buy; a working store program ("Sip & Save") prices bottles in points — E.H. Taylor Single Barrel at 700 points, Blanton's at 500, Buffalo Trace at 250 — and if you miss the pickup week, that month's opportunity passes. The stores that win run the allocation as a machine with five engines: the allocated-bottle ladder (the monthly drop — per-bottle interest lists, the fair-play rules, the pickup window), the tasting-event calendar (in-store tastings and bottle signings that turn regulars into evangelists), the holiday-gift machine (November and December are roughly a quarter of the whole year's sales — the season the store makes or misses its year), the new-arrival rhythm (the weekday "we got it" alert for shelf goods), and the January playbook (the liquor category's lowest month — the list stays warm or the store starts from zero in February). Here is how the best tool for the store runs them.

What this guide scores

The liquor store is a drop business wearing a retail store's clothes: the product is scarcity, the revenue is the season, and everything between is a list. So this guide scores tools on the things a store actually does: the allocation-drop engine (the announce email, the per-bottle interest lists — the Connecticut hunter's rule is that blanket lists don't work, you ask for each bottle by name — the fair-play rules, the pickup-week reminder, and the segmentation that keeps the hunters separate from the gift buyers), the tasting-event calendar (invites, 48-hour reminders, the post-event "what we tasted" follow-up with the shelf link), the holiday-gift machine (gift-set guides, the corporate lane, the mix-and-match bundle ladder — the M&R playbook: any 6 for 10% off, 12 bottles for 15% off — running on the list, not on hope), new-arrival alerts (the weekday rhythm that keeps the list opening), and the January playbook (education and mixology content for the slowest month, so the list is warm when spring drops land). Everything else — the funnel builders, the AI gimmicks, the 200-template galleries — is decoration. A liquor store needs the drop, the event, and the gift season. Nothing else pays.

The economics of the season: liquor category sales climb up to 25% in December, and for real operators November-plus-December is about 25% of the entire year's revenue — one Colorado retailer measured it on the books. A store that treats the holiday email calendar as an afterthought is spending its highest-intent weeks silent. And 36% of 2026 holiday shoppers plan to give beer, wine, or spirits — ahead of toys. The gift season is the store's Super Bowl, and the list is how it fills the seats.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

1. The allocated-bottle ladder. The drop is the store's blockbuster — the monthly (or quarterly) allocation: the limited cases, the hunted bottles, the waitlist that lives in email. The ladder runs: the signup engineering (per-BOTTLE interest lists, not one blanket "allocated" list — the hunter asks for Eagle Rare by name, and the store's machinery honors the specificity), the drop announce (the bottles, the quantities, the rules), the fair-play layer (one per household, pickup window clearly stated, missed-pickup consequences in writing — the Sip & Save rule: miss the week, miss the month), the pickup-week reminder (the SMS ping that empties the holding shelf), and the follow-up to the no-yet-sayers (the allocation that doesn't move goes to the next list, and the store that runs this cleanly never sits on allocated inventory). The quiet finding from the bourbon-hunting world: the stores that mark up allocations to resale levels poison their own lists — the fair-price store keeps the list, and the list is the asset that compounds.

2. The tasting-event calendar. The store's pipeline machine. In-store tastings, bottle signings, distributor pour nights — the calendar that turns foot traffic into list subscribers and subscribers into regulars. The ladder: the invite (three weeks out, then the 48-hour reminder), the event itself, the post-event "what we tasted" email with the shelf link while the palate memory is fresh. The documented shape: Holiday Wine Cellar runs a brisk December tasting schedule — a bottle signing with the artist, a Crown Royal gift-pack tasting, a wine-club social — and every one of those is a list-building event disguised as a party. The regulars' VIP lane (first dibs on tasting seats) is the same machinery as the allocation VIP — the list, segmented, rewards loyalty twice.

3. The holiday-gift machine. November and December are ~25% of the year, and the machine has three gears. Gear one: the gift-set guide — the curated picks by price band, the bottles that photograph well on the holiday table, the "for the bourbon lover" / "for the host" segmentation. Gear two: the bundle ladder — the proven 12-Wines-of-Christmas shape: mix-and-match any 6 for 10% off, 12 for 15% off, the discount stacking with the case logic the store already runs. Gear three: the corporate lane — the office gift orders, the client bottles, the December bulk runs that move cases in single transactions. And the white space the holiday-email teardown exposed: almost nobody promotes gift cards or club memberships in holiday email — the easiest gift the store sells. The gift buyer is a different customer from the hunter: they buy by price band and occasion, not by taste profile. Separate the segments or lose both.

4. The new-arrival rhythm. The weekday machine that keeps the list opening between drops. The "we got it" alert: new shelf goods, restocks of the good stuff, the distributor's odd case that won't last the week. The rhythm is the moat — the store that emails every Friday with what's new trains its list to open every Friday, and the list that opens every Friday is the list that shows up for the December drop. The documented cadence from the retailers who run this well: direct emails alongside the other channels, the e-commerce shelf visible from home ("people peruse the whole catalogue from their own home"), and the average online order running LARGER than in-store. The new-arrival email is the feeder for the online cart.

5. The January playbook. January is the liquor category's lowest month — the documented slump after the holiday spike. The stores that go quiet in January start February from zero; the stores that run the playbook keep the list warm for the spring drops. The playbook: mixology content (the cocktail education the holiday gift buyers now want to use), the "what we drank in December" recap with the restock links, the loyalty-and-points housekeeping (the new year's allocation-list refresh — prune the dead addresses, reconfirm the interest lists), and the event seeding for spring (tasting calendar announced early, seats filling while competitors are silent). January is education month. The store that teaches in January sells in March.

The honest catches

The allocation list is power — running it unfairly kills it. The bourbon-hunting forums are full of the same complaint: the bottle is "spoken for" before the list ever hears about it. The store that runs the allocation by manager's-friendship owns a shrinking asset — the list learns it never wins and stops opening. Fairness rules in writing, applied visibly: one per household, the pickup window, the waitlist order honored. The list is the asset that compounds — and trust is the interest rate.

The hunters and the gift buyers are different customers — never send one the other's email. The single most expensive segmentation mistake in the business. The hunter who got the drop email learns the list is his insider channel; the gift buyer who gets the drop email learns the store is confusing. Two lists, two cadences, zero cross-sends — the same discipline as the bridal shop's full-price vs. sample-sale lists. The winery's club shipment and the store's allocation drop both live on this same separation.

Compliance is not optional theater — age-gate the list. Alcohol email marketing runs inside real rules: the list must be age-gated (21+ in the U.S.), and the store should confirm before any email leaves that its vendor's acceptable-use policy covers alcohol. The cheap move — collecting emails at the register with no age check and blasting promos — is the one that gets the account shut down. The store that builds the list cleanly owns it for years; the store that cuts the corner re-rents the list from scratch after the ban.

Don't spend the list's attention on every email — the drop is sacred. The allocation email is the highest-open message the store will ever send. Every off-topic blast to the hunter list ("we restocked vodka!") trains the hunters to treat the list as noise — and then the drop email lands in a inbox that stopped opening. The hunter segment gets drops, pickup pings, and tasting seats. Everything else goes to the general list. Scarcity applies to attention too.

December hides January — plan the slump before it arrives. The store that spends November and December emailing perfectly and then goes dark in January teaches its list a lesson: the list is only worth opening when there's a sale. January is the lowest month on the calendar — the documented slump — which makes it the cheapest month to win attention. Education content, the allocation-list refresh, the spring tasting calendar: the store that runs January as a machine doesn't just survive the slump — it arrives in February with a warmer list than every competitor that went quiet.

FAQ

How do we run an allocation list fairly? In writing: per-bottle interest lists (blanket "allocated" lists don't work — the hunter names the bottle), published rules (one per household, the pickup window, missed-pickup consequences), and the waitlist order honored visibly. The fairness is the marketing: a list that believes it can win stays a list. A list that learns the bottles are spoken for becomes a ghost town with open rates to match.

Do we need a loyalty or points program for allocations? Not to start. The per-bottle list plus pickup discipline is the engine; points (the Sip & Save shape — points deducted per allocated bottle, rewarding ongoing spend) are the upgrade for stores with enough volume to tier customers without starving new ones. The caution from the bourbon world: some states restrict or prohibit coupon and loyalty systems for alcohol, so the store should check its state's rules before building a points program. Fair rules first, points later.

Is email better than Instagram for drops? Yes for the close — and it isn't close. Social shows the drop to whoever the algorithm picks; email puts the drop in every hunter's inbox at the same minute. The stores that run real allocation machinery (Tony's Fresh Market's allocation list, the Sip & Save points program) run it on email, with SMS as the pickup ping. Instagram is the billboard; email is the register.

What about the holiday season — when do we start? The holiday-email teardown shows the pattern: gift sets, special release calendars, and event invitations dominate the winning holiday sends, and almost nobody promotes gift cards or club memberships — the white space is sitting there. Start the gift guide in early November (shoppers start early), run the bundle ladder through December, and have the corporate lane built before Thanksgiving — the bulk orders don't wait for the last-minute calendar.
