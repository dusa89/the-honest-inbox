A bakery is two businesses under one roof, and they run on opposite clocks. The daily-case track is a sell-through race: product that must sell before closing, every single day, with the waste bin as the alternative. The custom-order track is a slow, high-ticket pipeline: a wedding cake averages around $350 in the US — $125 on the low end, past $1,000 at the top, $7-$12 a slice for the high-end work — booked months ahead through an inquiry, a tasting, and a decision that takes weeks (industry and wedding-budget sources, October 2026). The global wedding-cake market is valued around $20.3 billion in 2026, heading toward $29 billion by 2034 at roughly 4.6% annual growth. Email is the only channel that runs both clocks at once: the daily-case list sells today's product before it goes stale, and the wedding-tier engine nurtures a $700 decision over 90 days. Six engines no other vertical has in this form: the wedding-tier engine — inquiry to tasting to booking; the holiday pre-order engine — the Thanksgiving and Christmas production calendar sold through the list; the daily-case list — what's in the case today, gone by 6pm; the corporate-catering drip — the recurring office order; the review engine — because the custom-order pipeline starts on your star rating; and the reorder/subscription ladder — subscription loaves, cake-of-the-month, gift cards as prepaid revenue. Scored below are the tools that survive contact with a shop whose product goes stale at midnight and whose biggest orders take three months to close.

What a bakery actually needs from email

Forget "open rates." The six engines that decide whether the case sells out and the wedding book fills:

  1. The wedding-tier engine. The inquiry arrives and the clock starts: the follow-up within the hour, the tasting booking sequence, the post-tasting close (the decision takes weeks, and the shop that follows up wins), the vendor-referral network (planners, florists, photographers — the bakery trade's version of a referral pipeline), and the anniversary reactivation (first-anniversary cake, the baby's first birthday). The unit economics justify the machinery: at $350-$1,000+ per cake, one booked wedding from the nurture sequence pays for years of email tooling. Scored on the tools: can it run a 60-90 day multi-step pipeline with tasting-booking triggers and referral-source segmentation?
  2. The holiday pre-order engine. Thanksgiving and Christmas are the production ceiling — the bakery physically cannot bake more than the ovens hold, and the list decides who gets the slots. The trade's own playbook (BakeStreet, updated October 2026) is a four-send sequence: menu live three to four weeks out (Thanksgiving lands Thu Nov 26, 2026; Christmas Fri Dec 25 — Constant Contact's 2026 calendar), the bundle-led send at ten days, real scarcity at five days, last call the morning of the cutoff. And the sequencing rule the playbook insists on: last year's holiday buyers get each send first — that list is, in the playbook's words, the most valuable list a bakery owns, and most shops don't have it segmented. The trade press agrees (Bakers Journal): the bulk of holiday sales comes from regulars, and the move is to get in touch with all of last year's holiday and catering buyers and get more active via email during the season. Scored on the tools: can you segment last-year buyers, stagger the four sends, and fire the last-call SMS on cutoff morning?
  3. The daily-case list. "What's in the case today" — the sell-through engine for product that goes stale at midnight. The fresh-bake announcement, the limited-run sell-through notice, the day-end save (the loyalty-only "what's left at 5pm" alert that turns waste into revenue), the seasonal-first-batch send. This is the high-frequency list: daily or near-daily, and it trains the habit the holiday engine depends on — subscribers learn the bakery's email means "act now." The scarcity here is honest by nature: the case is genuinely finite, gone by 6pm or it's trash.
  4. The corporate-catering drip. The recurring B2B track: weekly office bread orders, meeting catering, the quarterly all-hands, the office-party season in Q4. This is recurring revenue on a contract-free handshake — the account that orders the Friday croissants every week is the steadiest customer the bakery has, and it lands through a separate, quieter list. Scored on the tools: can it run a second list with its own cadence without contaminating the retail voice?
  5. The review engine. The custom-order pipeline starts on your star rating — the bride who Googles "wedding cake near me" filters by stars before she ever reads a word. The engine is the post-pickup review request (the wedding client's review is worth ten daily-case reviews — it books the next $700 cake), the response cadence, and the review-to-list pipeline. The bakery that captures the reviewer's email owns the reviewer twice.
  6. The reorder/subscription ladder. The compounding machinery: the subscription loaf (weekly bread, prepaid monthly — cash in hand before the wheat is bought), the cake-of-the-month club, the gift-card pushes at Christmas (prepaid revenue, redemption in January's dead weeks), the birthday-reminder program (the cake that reorders itself). Scored on the tools: can it run subscription reminders, gift-card campaigns, and birthday automations without a developer?

Notice what's missing: no generic newsletters, no "we're a local bakery!" filler. The wedding-tier engine does the high-ticket closing work, the holiday engine does the production-ceiling work, the daily-case list does the sell-through work, the corporate drip does the recurring-revenue work, the review engine does the trust work, and the subscription ladder does the cash-flow work. A bakery whose only email is the "happy holidays!" blast is running both clocks wrong — announcing to the list instead of the pipeline, and to everyone instead of last year's buyers first.

The honest catches

Your cake-order software already owns part of the flow. Don't double-buy it. Cake-shop platforms (BakeOnyx is the trade name — online ordering, quotes, deposits, production lists, customer messages) already send the transactional layer: the quote, the deposit confirmation, the production message. Those are not this tool's job. The email tool owns the nurture and the pre-order calendar — the inquiry follow-up, the tasting pipeline, the four-send holiday engine, the daily-case sell-through. Audit what your stack already sends before you pay for an email tool to send it twice.

Never fake scarcity — the holiday list is the asset you're borrowing against. The daily case is honestly scarce every day (gone by 6pm or it's waste), and the holiday production ceiling is honestly scarce every season (the ovens hold what the ovens hold). That's the entire edge. Invent a fake "selling out fast" once, and every later email — including the real ones — gets quietly discounted by the reader. The trade playbook's rule is blunt because it has to be: scarcity you fake destroys the urgency you need for the scarcity that's real.

This is not a newsletter business — it's a two-clock business. The daily-case list and the wedding pipeline are different lists with different voices, different cadences, and different buyers. The retail list wants the morning photo; the wedding list wants the tasting invitation. One list, two voices, and the wedding nurture reads like spam to the croissant regular. Segment them from day one — this is the mistake the "we have one newsletter" bakery makes every year.

Last year's buyers are the whole holiday strategy. The playbook's sequencing rule exists because it works: the bulk of holiday sales comes from regulars, and the people who bought a holiday item off you last year already like your pie, already know where to park, and are vaguely intending to order again and haven't got round to it. If you can't pull "last year's holiday buyers" as a list, fix that before the season starts — it is the most valuable list a bakery owns, and most shops don't have it. Thanksgiving 2026 is Thu Nov 26: the menu-live send fires roughly four weeks out. The clock is already running.
