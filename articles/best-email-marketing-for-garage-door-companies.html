A garage door business is two businesses wearing one uniform: the 7am emergency (the torsion spring that snapped with both cars trapped inside, the customer calling from their phone while the first company quotes $650 for a $285 job) and the considered purchase (the 18-year-old door patched for the fourth time, the opener that predates smartphones, the homeowner who finally wants the curb appeal). The emergency is won on speed and reviews — email will never close a 7am spring break. Everything after the truck leaves is email's territory: the spring you replaced today has a known lifespan, the door you repaired is either worth keeping or worth replacing, and every customer you have ever serviced is one email away from the tune-up plan, the opener upgrade, or the full door. What a garage door company actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. The post-service reactivation drip. The spring job you did today is a timer: 10,000–20,000 cycles before it breaks again (7–14 years average use), and you replaced both springs even though only one snapped — that is honest practice, and the email says so. The drip: day 3 (the thank-you + what was replaced + the maintenance truth: lubrication once a year, watch the rollers), month 6 (the tune-up nudge: a 20-minute tune-up at $95–$180 is cheaper than the next emergency call), the review ask. Most emergency customers never hear from the shop again — and then call a competitor the next time the door acts up. One drip fixes that.
  2. The aging-door replacement ladder. Doors live 15–30 years (Overhead Door, 2026), and the homeowner with the 19-year-old door patched twice in 18 months is buying a replacement — they just don't know it yet. The honest math the email teaches: repair averages $200–$600; replace runs $1,000–$3,500+; if you have called a technician multiple times in the last 12–18 months, the door has reached the end of its reliable life (Overhead Door's own repair-vs-replace guidance). Then the curb-appeal truth: contractor roundups cite ~100%+ cost recovery on a door replacement at resale — it is one of the highest-ROI projects in the home (label: published contractor roundups, not an appraisal of your house). Segment the list by install year and the ladder sells itself.
  3. The smart-opener upgrade sequence. Openers older than ~10 years get repaired; older than that, they get replaced — and the upgrade has a real pitch now: belt drives quiet enough for a bedroom over the garage, app control and delivery-day access, battery backup for outages. Installed $350–$950 (2026 surveys); the premium lines (wall-mount jackshaft, $650–$1,250) free the ceiling. The sequence targets the installed base: anyone whose opener you touched in the last 3 years but didn't replace gets the upgrade education. It is the trade's easiest upsell because the trigger (the opener that wakes the baby) is a daily annoyance, not an abstract.
  4. The tune-up plan that builds the annuity. The annual tune-up — balance, lubrication, travel-limit checks, safety-reversal test — runs $95–$180 and takes a tech under an hour. As a plan it is the trade's cheapest recurring revenue and its cheapest review machine: the tech is in the garage, the door is working, the ask is natural. The email job is enrollment (sell the plan at every service visit), renewal (the plan that lapses in month 11 gets the renewal drip in month 10), and the slow-season filler: tune-ups are the February and August work when emergency calls cool off, and the email book is how you fill them.
  5. Review moat with proof. The 7am spring-break customer chose you on reviews and speed — they picked the Stripe-verified tech with 47 completed jobs over the $650 quote, and they did it from their phone in the driveway. The review ask goes out when the door works and the panic is gone (day 3, not the day the truck leaves), with the direct Google review link, two emails max. In a trade where the emergency call is an impulse buy from a panicked stranger, the review count is the entire moat — and every emergency review reads as a promise kept.

Notice what's missing: no lead scoring, no landing-page builder Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. Your dispatch software already handles the service board; the email tool only has to hold the customer list, send the campaigns, and run the post-service machine. Paying for a marketing department's toolkit is the trap.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a garage door shop leaves on the table:

  1. The broken-spring drip. Trigger: spring job complete. Email 1 (day 3): the thank-you + what was replaced — name it: both springs (honest practice: replace the pair, since the second one has the same cycles on it), the cycle rating (10,000–20,000), and the one maintenance truth: lubrication once a year. Email 2 (week 2): the tune-up nudge — the $95–$180 tune-up that catches the worn rollers and loose hardware before the next 7am surprise. Email 3 (month 1): the review ask with the direct link — the panic is gone, the door works, this is the moment. Three emails, zero begging. The spring job is your most expensive acquisition and your warmest lead; the drip treats it that way.
  2. The aging-door replacement ladder. Trigger: install year on file, door 15+ years old. Email 1: the honest education — doors live 15–30 years (Overhead Door, 2026); the signal that it's replacement time is multiple service calls in 12–18 months, not one bad day. Email 2: the math — repair averages $200–$600 a visit; a new door installed runs $900–$3,500+; the fourth repair is the down payment on a door. Email 3: the curb-appeal case — the door is the biggest visible surface on most houses, and contractor roundups cite ~100%+ cost recovery at resale (label: published roundups, not a promise about your home). Email 4: the offer — the free assessment with the honest verdict (keep it or replace it). The ladder never lies about the door's condition; a shop that tells a customer their door is fine buys the next ten years of trust.
  3. The smart-opener upgrade sequence. Trigger: opener age on file (10+ years) or any opener repair in the last 3 years. Email 1: the daily-annoyance hook — the opener that wakes the baby, the one with no app, the one that dies in every outage. Email 2: the upgrade case — belt-drive quiet, app control and delivery-day access, battery backup; installed $350–$950, wall-mount jackshaft $650–$1,250 for the ceiling-space crowd (2026 contractor pricing data). Email 3: the safety note — openers from before the mid-2010s lack current safety-reversal standards; the upgrade is a safety fix, not a luxury. It is the trade's easiest upsell because the trigger is a daily annoyance.
  4. The tune-up plan enrollment + renewal. Trigger: any service visit (enrollment) and month 10 of the plan (renewal). The enrollment email goes out the week after the job: the $95–$180 annual visit, what it covers (balance, lubrication, travel limits, safety reversal), the slow-season scheduling pitch (you book first, before the emergency calls fill February). The renewal drip starts at month 10: month 10 the reminder, month 11 the last call, month 12 the lapse-and-welcome-back. The plan is the trade's cheapest annuity and its cheapest review machine — and it is what keeps your trucks full in the months the emergencies don't.
  5. The neighborhood referral machine. Trigger: any completed job. Email 1: the thank-you + the referral ask — the honest version: a fixed credit per signed neighbor (set your own number; the solar world standardizes around $500 on a $30k sale — your ticket is smaller, so size it to your margins). Email 2 (to the street, later): the visible-product campaign — garage doors are street-visible; the "new door on Oak Street" email names no customer, just the street and the curb-appeal story. Route-density wins in every trade: two doors on one street beat two doors in two towns, and the crew scheduling gets cheaper too.

The honest catches

Email will never close a 7am emergency. The spring-break customer picks on reviews, speed, and the honest price — from their phone, in the driveway, in minutes. Email's job is everything after the truck leaves: the reactivation, the tune-up, the ladder, the upgrades, the reviews that win the next 7am. A strategy that claims email books emergency calls is selling you a fantasy; a strategy that owns the post-service list is selling you the second half of the business.

Never sell a repair the door doesn't need. The ladder works because it is honest — the moment a customer suspects the "your door is dying" email is a script, every email after it reads as a lie. Segment by install year and service history, send the ladder to doors that actually show the age signals, and be willing to tell a customer their 9-year-old door is fine. The shop that tells the truth about a good door owns the day that door finally dies.

Get written consent — buy a list and you're the spam. Marketing email needs opt-in consent; buy a contractor list and blast it and you are the robocall in inbox form, and the complaint ceiling (Google wants under 0.3%, aims under 0.1%) will end your sending career before it starts. The consent checkbox goes on every service form and every online booking, and the unsubscribe link actually works. Every name on the list should be a door you have touched.

Cold snaps break springs — plan the season. Practitioner wisdom (label: shop lore, not a physics paper): spring failures cluster after cold snaps, when older metal is under maximum stress. The seasonal campaign rides the weather: the first cold week of fall is the pre-season tune-up book; the week after the deep freeze is the "is your door slower than usual?" check. You don't control the weather; you control the calendar around it.

Your dispatch software already sends some of this — don't double-send. Dispatch boards handle appointment confirmations, tech-on-the-way, and invoice delivery; many run their own customer messaging. Let dispatch handle job communication; let the email tool handle marketing campaigns, post-service drips, the ladder, upgrade sequences, and review asks. Check what your dispatch software already does before you pay for an email tool to do it twice.

Your list import is the real setup cost. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting the customer list and tagging it properly — install year, door type, opener age, spring replaced (and when), plan member, neighborhood (estimate — most dispatch exports won't have all of these out of the box). Budget an afternoon, not ten minutes. A list without install-year tags can't run the aging-door ladder — the tags are the product. Our deliverability audit checklist covers the list-hygiene half.

How do I keep my tune-up plan from lapsing?

Start the renewal drip at month 10, not month 12. Month 10: the reminder (your annual visit is due, here's the booking link, plan members book first). Month 11: the last call (the slow-season window closes, rates reset). Month 12: the lapse email with the welcome-back offer — lapses are cheaper to win back than cold customers are to acquire, because they already trust your trucks. Tag every plan member with a plan-start month so the drip fires on schedule.

Can I import my customer list from my dispatch software?

Yes — every tool here takes a CSV export, and the dispatch boards export one. The integration depth varies: direct or Zapier-path connections exist, but the honest workflow for most shops is a scheduled CSV export and import, with install year, door type, opener age, spring replaced (and when), plan member, and neighborhood added as custom fields. Are Mailchimp or Constant Contact fine for garage door companies?

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and both are built for generic small business rather than this niche. Should I email the customer the day of the service visit?

Not the marketing kind. The day of the visit belongs to dispatch: confirmation, tech-on-the-way, invoice. The marketing emails start after the panic has passed — the thank-you on day 3, the tune-up nudge at week 2, the review ask at month 1. Sending the review ask while the tech is still in the driveway reads as a script; sending it when the door has worked for a month reads as earned.
