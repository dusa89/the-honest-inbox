A comic shop is not a bookstore — it's a subscription business with a storefront. The pull list (the shop holds your titles every New Comic Book Wednesday) is the closest thing to recurring revenue in the trade: subscribers usually get 10–15% off cover price, the shop gets guaranteed Wednesday sales it already ordered months ago, and the relationship is contractual in everything but law — the shop paid for those books and the subscriber committed to pick them up. The industry backdrop is strong: comics and graphic novel sales in the U.S. and Canada hit $2.2 billion in 2025, above the COVID-era highs, with comic-shop sales growing faster than the book channel — comics up 30% year over year, graphic novels up 28%, and comic-shop sales approaching $1 billion (ICv2 White Paper "Navigating the New World of Comics," presented at the 2026 ComicsPRO Sneak Peek). The 15-year trend of the book channel outgrowing the direct market has reversed. And it's broadening, not concentrating: a 68-shop industry study found revenue up 19.9% from Q2 2025 to Q2 2026 with the median store growing 26.1% — outpacing the aggregate for the first time — driven by more transactions (+19.6%), not higher prices (average purchase price actually dipped 0.2%), with trading cards, back issues, and graphic novels powering growth and growth on every day of the week (Prana: Direct Market Solutions report, via 2026 industry coverage). Every tool below is scored against that machinery: the pull-list subscription engine, the solicitation-to-FOC pre-order machine, the variant and new-#1 launch engine, the graded-collectibles lane, and the community-event moat.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The pull-list engine. The subscriber's box is the business's annuity — new-subscriber welcome sequences, Wednesday "your box is ready" cadence, and the unclaimed-box win-back. Scored on: subscriber segmentation, automated pickup reminders, and lapse detection.
  2. The solicitation-to-FOC pre-order machine. Catalogs solicit ~3 months ahead and shops order ~2 months before release (final order cutoff, "FOC," lands ~3 weeks before release) — comics are non-returnable, so every pre-order before FOC is inventory insurance. Scored on: monthly solicitation-digest sends, pre-order conversion flows, and urgency-by-deadline sequences.
  3. The variant / new-#1 launch engine. Collector hype lives and dies on launches: new #1 issues, variant covers, reprint discipline. Scored on: fast visual sends, interest-segment targeting, and sellout follow-up.
  4. The graded-collectibles lane. Back issues, graded keys, high-ticket collector sales — the highest-margin line in the shop. Scored on: collector-profile segmentation, high-ticket nurture sequences, and condition-honest storytelling formats.
  5. The community-event moat. Signings, launch parties, game nights, Free Comic Book Day — the events Amazon can't copy. Scored on: event RSVP flows, day-of alerts, and attendee-to-subscriber conversion.

Best for: shops running the pull list, the monthly solicitation digest, and the launch calendar on one honest tool.

New comics are sold on covers, and MailerLite's templates make the Wednesday new-releases email and the solicitation-preview digest look expensive. The automation builder handles the whole machine — the pull-list welcome sequence, the monthly FOC-deadline reminder, the unclaimed-box win-back, the "new #1 on the shelf" blast. Pricing stays gentle at comic-shop list sizes: Comfort at 500 subscribers is $12/mo (5,000 sends, the 10x send cap), 1,000 subs $19/mo, 2,500 subs $33/mo, 5,000 subs $49/mo, and the free tier (250 subs / 2,500 emails) genuinely covers a small shop while it builds the list. The honest catch: the two heaviest email weeks — the monthly solicitation digest (the biggest, most visual send) and big-launch weeks — can strain the 10x Comfort send cap, because a comic shop's email rhythm is spiky by nature: one big monthly catalog push, four Wednesday cadences. Price the tool at solicitation-season send volume, not average list size; Power (from $25/mo, removes the cap) exists for the spike weeks.

2. GetResponse — the automation-depth pick for the FOC pre-order machine

Best for: shops that run real collector segmentation and want every FOC deadline and pull-list nurture automated to the hilt.

Starter at $19/mo (sender) or Marketer/Creator at $59–$69/mo (platform). The automation builder is the strongest on this list for multi-branch sequences — different pre-order reminder ladders for the Marvel loyalists vs the indie readers vs the graded-key collectors, conditional on purchase history and title tags. That branching is exactly what the solicitation-to-FOC machine wants: the FOC-deadline alert firing to the subscriber whose pull list would actually contain that book, the new-#1 hype sequence running off street date instead of a clerk's memory. The honest demerit: GetResponse's famous webinar and funnel machinery is dead weight for a comic shop — you're not hosting webinars for subscribers — so you're paying platform prices for the automation half. If the pull-list program genuinely runs itself, the half earns it; if the whole program is the Wednesday email plus the solicitation digest, MailerLite wins on price.

3. Brevo — the email-plus-SMS pick for the box-ready text

Best for: shops that want the "your box is ready" texts and the Wednesday emails in one place.

Pull-list subscribers answer their phones: the "your Wednesday box is ready — unclaimed boxes go back on the shelf Friday" text, the FOC-deadline "last call for pre-orders" alert, the event-night reminder that actually gets opened. Brevo bundles email and SMS, prices email on volume (friendly for small subscriber lists), and the CRM-lite keeps the pull-list profiles visible. Honest check: SMS is the scalpel here, not the channel — the Wednesday digest stays email, and every marketing text needs real consent. Use text for pickup reminders, FOC deadline alerts, and event-day pings; never for the solicitation catalog itself.

4. Kit — the simplest path to a Wednesday email that actually ships

Best for: shops whose whole program is the Wednesday new-releases email and pull-list reminders.

Free to 10,000 subscribers at $0, then the bill jumps hard ($139/mo past 10,000) — but a comic shop's list is hundreds of subscribers, not tens of thousands, so the free tier is effectively permanent. The trade: Kit's automations are thinner than MailerLite's and much thinner than GetResponse's — the FOC-deadline branching and the graded-collector nurture get clunky fast, and the monthly solicitation digest with real segmentation is where the ceiling shows. If the business is "Wednesday email + box-ready reminders," Kit is the cheapest way to look professional doing it. If the collector program is genuinely segmented (publisher tribes, graded-key buyers, gaming-night regulars), the ceiling arrives fast.

5. AWeber — the reliable old guard, priced like it

Best for: shops that want a known quantity and will pay for it.

Long track record, real deliverability reputation — and in a business where the FOC-deadline email has to land on time or pre-orders die, deliverability counts. But the plan structure is built for bigger consumer lists than a comic shop runs, and the automation builder lags the top two for the pull-list and FOC sequencing work. Fine if you inherit it; not the pick if you're choosing fresh in 2026.

The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The Wednesday new-releases email. Every Wednesday, same time: this week's new comics, cover-forward, publisher-segmented — the Marvel loyalists see the Marvel shelf, the indie readers see the Image/Dark Horse shelf. This is the heartbeat the 19.9% revenue-growth shops are serving weekly: the regulars train themselves around a fixed schedule, and the Wednesday cadence turns casual browsers into ritual regulars. Estimate: our operator-pattern estimate is that shops publishing a fixed-schedule Wednesday email see pickup-day foot traffic concentrate into the email window — treat it as pattern, not a study.
  2. The solicitation-to-FOC pre-order machine. This is the single highest-leverage email in the business. Comics are non-returnable — the shop owns every book it orders — and shops order ~2 months ahead, with the final order cutoff (FOC) landing ~3 weeks before release. The monthly solicitation digest (what's coming, why it matters, who should pre-order) plus the FOC-deadline reminder ("last call: pre-order by Friday or we can't guarantee your copy") converts browsers into pre-orders that de-risk the order. Every subscriber you add to a pull list before FOC is inventory you can't lose money on. Run the digest monthly, the FOC reminder on the deadline week, and the variant-cover preview only to the collector segment.
  3. The pull-list welcome & unclaimed-box sequence. The new subscriber is the most profitable acquisition a comic shop makes — a pull list is a weekly recurring-revenue relationship. The welcome sequence should do three jobs: educate on how the pull list works (discounts, pickup cadence, how to add/drop titles before FOC), get the subscriber's title preferences (the segmentation data that powers everything), and set the pickup contract (10-15% off is the industry-standard perk; unclaimed boxes after 14-30 days is the industry-standard problem). The win-back is where the money hides: a subscriber who goes quiet for 3 weeks is inventory accumulating on the shelf — the "your box is getting big" sequence (friendly at week 2, firm at week 4) recovers the relationship or ends it cleanly instead of bleeding shelf space.
  4. The graded-collectibles nurture. Back issues and graded keys are the highest-margin line, and the collector buyers are a different animal: they buy condition, provenance, and story. The nurture: the back-issue arrival alert (condition-photos, the honest grading note), the "key issue of the month" education email (why this book matters — the auction-houses guide on this site covers the same collector psychology), and the want-list intake form for high-end hunters. The one-of-one discipline from our record-stores guide applies here exactly: advertise the character/franchise in mass email, send specific graded keys only to the collector who asked — a sold grail blasted to the whole list is a broken promise to everyone who shows up.
  5. The community-event calendar. Signings, launch parties, gaming nights, Free Comic Book Day — the indie-bookstore article on this site made the same point about author events: these are the moat Amazon can't copy. Monthly, to the whole list; RSVP via the email, day-of reminders by SMS if you run Brevo. Trading cards are worth the same machinery: the Prana data says cards are one of the categories powering the +19.9% growth — the tournament-night email is the entry product for the next generation of regulars. The event sells the night; the calendar sells the shop.

The honest catches

  • Non-returnable inventory is the whole game. The trade's defining fact: the shop owns the books it orders. That makes the FOC pre-order machine above the most important email on this page — and it makes every other campaign cheaper, because un-sold inventory is the tax everything else pays. Email that doesn't convert pre-orders before FOC is decoration.
  • Variant fatigue traps cash in covers. Ratio variants and retailer incentives are catnip for collectors but poison for ordering discipline. The email program should sell stories to readers (who buy every month, forever) and ration the variant hype to the collector segment (who buy covers, sometimes). A variant email that reaches the story-driven reader is noise; a variant email that reaches the cover collector is revenue.
  • The distribution world is shifting under your feet. Diamond's bankruptcy is actively reshaping who ships what — the market is now a multi-distributor world (Lunar for DC, Penguin Random House for Marvel and IDW). Date your solicitation references, never promise distributor-specific dates in evergreen email copy, and build the calendar around your shop's actual street dates, not the catalog's.
  • Graded-market swings are real. The graded-collectibles lane is the highest-margin line and the most sentiment-driven: key-issue prices move on speculation, not fundamentals. Nurture the collector segment with honest condition notes and real market ranges, never price predictions. The auction-houses guide on this site covers the same collector trust sell in more depth.
  • Unclaimed pull boxes are silent churn. The subscriber who goes quiet doesn't cancel — they just stop showing up, and their box grows on the shelf. The week-2/week-4 win-back is non-optional infrastructure, not nice-to-have. A pull list with no pickup contract and no win-back is a warehouse program, not a subscription program.
  • Free tiers last longer here — and that's a trap of its own. A 400-person list of subscribers sits inside MailerLite's free tier (250 subs) or Kit's (10,000) for a long time. The trap: building the FOC pre-order machine on a free plan's automation ceiling, then discovering the deadline-branching can't run when the solicitation program needs it. Start free, but pick the tool whose paid automations fit the 5-year pull-list program, not the 2-month one.
  • The box is the closer; email is the invitation. Nobody drives across town for an email — they drive for the ritual of Wednesday, the gaming table, the signing line. Email's job is getting people into the building and making sure subscribers never miss an FOC deadline. The shop that tries to sell the whole collection by email loses to the shop whose email got the collector through the door.

FAQ

How often should a comic shop email?

Weekly minimum, publisher-segmented. The Wednesday new-releases email is the heartbeat — subscribers train themselves around a fixed schedule, like the record-store Friday drop this site documented. Add the monthly solicitation digest, the FOC-deadline reminder on deadline weeks, and the event calendar monthly. Daily is too much; monthly is too little to hold the ritual.

How do you manage a pull list with email?

Three sequences: the welcome (how the list works, title preferences, the pickup contract), the Wednesday "your box is ready" cadence, and the week-2/week-4 unclaimed-box win-back. The title-preference intake is the segmentation data that powers the FOC pre-order machine — get it at signup or you'll be flying blind at every deadline.

What actually converts browsers into pull-list subscribers?

The FOC reminder plus the discount framing. The "last call for pre-orders" deadline email is the highest-converting ask in the business because it has a real scarcity mechanism — after FOC, the shop can't guarantee the copy. Pair it with the 10–15% subscriber discount: the subscription framing (the pull list is a membership, not a coupon) is what turns the browser into the subscriber.

SMS or email for box-ready reminders?

Both, different jobs. The "your box is ready" alert goes by SMS — it's time-sensitive and transactional in spirit. The Wednesday digest, the solicitation catalog, and the graded-key stories stay email — they're browseable and visual. Every marketing text needs real consent; the transactional pickup text is the safest SMS a shop sends.

What's the first automation a comic shop should build?

The pull-list welcome → preference intake → pickup-contract sequence. It's the highest-leverage flow in the business because every new subscriber is weekly recurring revenue; the automation removes the clerk's memory from the chain. The FOC-deadline reminder cadence comes second. The Wednesday digest can be a manual send for a year — the subscriber infrastructure should be automated from week one.
