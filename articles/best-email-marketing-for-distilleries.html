A distillery sells a product it can barely advertise the way other businesses advertise theirs, to a buyer who may never set foot in the state it's made in. And the market it sells into is in a shakeout: the American Craft Spirits Association and Park Street's 2026 Craft Spirits Data Project — published October 6, 2026, covering the 2025 calendar year — puts U.S. craft spirits at 11.7 million 9-liter cases and $7.3 billion in sales (industry research, labeled), the third consecutive year of decline, with active craft distillers down to 2,131 as of August 2026 — the first year closures outpaced openings (industry research, labeled). The report hides the opportunity in plain sight: sales at the distillery climbed from 14% of craft spirits sales in 2015 to 26% in 2025 (industry research, labeled) — more than one bottle in four now sells directly to a visitor who walked through the door — while out-of-state sales fell to 51% from 54% in 2020 and exports dropped 13.4% (industry research, labeled). ACSA's own summary, paraphrased: for a small distillery, the home state is the business — and the email list is how the home state gets reached. Every tool below is scored against the machine: the tour-and-tasting calendar, the bottle-club membership, the distribution account lane, the visitor-to-buyer nurture, and the private-event and custom-label lane.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The tour-and-tasting calendar engine. The reservation funnel (book the tour, not "learn more"), the day-before reminder, the no-show rescue (the no-show is a lead, not a loss), the tasted-but-didn't-buy nurture, and the post-visit review ask. The operator pattern (estimate, labeled): the tasted-but-didn't-buy sequence is the highest-ROI email in the building — and most distilleries never send it. Scored on: the date-first cadence, the tour-date segmentation, and the post-visit machine.
  2. The bottle-club membership engine. The allocation-drop email (member first-access window on limited releases, then the public remainder, then the sold-out close — scarcity with a paper trail), the tier ladder (entry-style Discovery, quarterly-bottle Gold, annual-allocation Platinum — the ladder moves members up, not out), the club-event lane (virtual tastings, the quarterly gathering), and the lapsed-member win-back (the paused pickup is a membership problem, not a product problem). Industry examples, labeled: Bluegrass Distillers' quarterly Bottle Club (launched September 2026 at Elkwood Farm, Kentucky — quarterly bottle plus early access to select releases, four complimentary tours a year, a monthly member pour); Olde Raleigh's Whiskey Society ($139/mo, $1,251 annual, a members-only bottle every month plus first rights on every release); Remus Bourbon's quarterly club (limited bottles, virtual tastings, first access to hard-to-find releases). The operator math (estimate, labeled): a 200-member club at roughly $100 per quarter is $80,000 a year of recurring revenue before anyone walks through the tasting-room door. Scored on: the allocation discipline, the tier ladder, and the win-back machine.
  3. The distribution account lane. The local bar, restaurant, and retail buyer: the new-expression sell sheet (the account version of the allocation drop), the reorder reminder (the well bottle runs out on a schedule — the email knows it), the account check-in cadence, and the staff-training invite (the bartender who has tasted the whiskey sells the whiskey). In a market where distributors are narrowing portfolios and out-of-state share is falling, the home-state account list is the pipeline. Scored on: the reorder discipline, the account segmentation, and the local focus.
  4. The visitor-to-buyer nurture. The trail tourist (more than 40 distillery trails drive visitors nationwide — industry figure, labeled), the "your favorite flight" follow-up (the pour they loved, named, emailed within 48 hours), the birthday and anniversary nurture, and the Q4 gifting lane (gift sets, the holiday gift-guide blast, corporate and personal). Scored on: the flight-memory follow-up, the trail-season cadence, and the gifting season.
  5. The private-event and custom-label lane. Private events (weddings, corporate — the tasting room as the venue), the single-barrel program (bars and clubs buying their own barrel — the highest-margin email in the building), and the co-branded bottle. Distinct from the caterer's lane: the caterer sells the event's food; the distillery sells the event's room and the barrel. Scored on: the event funnel, the single-barrel pipeline, and the venue-versus-food discipline.

The honest catches

  • The age-21 line: never email anyone you can't verify is 21+. Tasting-room signups gate at age — and the list builder must gate too: no pre-checked tourist lists, no imported "visitors" without an age check. Email plus alcohol is a gated list or no list. Include the responsible-drinking line where the state requires it; some states treat alcohol email as a restricted category — check before the first send.
  • The DTC shipping trap: most states won't let you ship the bottle. Most states prohibit distillers from shipping directly to consumers — one craft distillery's club lists over ten states it can't ship to (industry example, labeled). Never email a "ships to your door" promise unless the buyer's state allows it. The club email names the states it ships to; the checkout enforces it; check the law before the campaign.
  • Never promise an allocation you can't fill. The club-drop email must match the barrel inventory — the allocation is the trust product. Overselling a limited bottle is the fastest way to kill a club; when the bottle count changes, update the campaign, not just the shelf. The same discipline as the caterer's per-head price — see Best Email Marketing for Caterers for the food-event version of the same honesty.
  • Keep tasting-room visitors and distribution accounts on separate lanes. The bar manager doesn't want the tourist's tasting notes; the club member doesn't want the sell sheet; the tourist doesn't want the reorder reminder. Three buyers, three cadences, zero cross-sends. The operator pattern (estimate, labeled): the distillery that cross-sends teaches all three audiences to ignore it.
  • The proof on the label is the law. The email's ABV, proof, and age statement must match the bottle's federally approved label — the label is what the bottle is legally allowed to claim, and the campaign library gets refreshed when expressions change. Stale release details in old campaigns are how one-star reviews are born ("their email said cask strength").

FAQ

How many craft distilleries are in the U.S.?

2,131 active craft distillers as of August 2026 (ACSA and Park Street's 2026 Craft Spirits Data Project, published October 6, 2026, covering calendar year 2025 — industry research, labeled) — the first year closures outpaced openings, in the category's third straight year of sales decline. Craft's share of U.S. spirits fell to 4.2% by volume and 7.3% by value.

How big is the U.S. craft spirits market?

$7.3 billion in 2025 sales on 11.7 million 9-liter cases (industry research, labeled) — volume down 8% and value down 3.7% year over year. The wider U.S. spirits market (Distilled Spirits Council figures, labeled) sits at $36.4 billion in supplier revenue for 2025, down 2.2% — a market correcting after a boom, not collapsing.

Why is the tasting room the engine now?

Sales at the distillery rose from 14% of craft spirits sales in 2015 to 26% in 2025 (industry research, labeled) — more than one bottle in four now sells to a visitor who walked through the door — while out-of-state sales fell to 51% from 54% in 2020 and exports dropped 13.4%. ACSA's summary, paraphrased: for a small distillery, the home state is the business. The email list is how the home state gets reached: the tour calendar, the club, and the local accounts.

How is distillery email different from brewery email?

The federal distilling permit, the bottle club as the recurring-revenue product, and the allocation drop as the scarcity engine — beer has the taproom pint; spirits has the allocated bottle. The age-21 gate, the DTC shipping trap, and the label-truth discipline have no real brewery equivalent. For the beer side, see Best Email Marketing for Breweries & Taprooms.
