A nursery does not sell plants — it sells the spring. The trade group's standing benchmark says it plainly: independent garden centers make roughly 60% of their sales in the 12 weeks of spring (The Garden Center Group's Danny Summers, quoted in the trade press). Twelve weeks. The other forty carry the overhead — the greenhouse heating, the year-round staff, the inventory that doesn't sell itself. And the category is real: US lawn and garden retail is projected near $94 billion in 2026 (Mintel), 80% of US households did lawn-and-garden activities in 2022 at $616 a household (National Gardening Association), and the Garden Center Group shows independent-center revenue up 5.3% year over year in the first half of 2026 with average tickets up 3.6%. But here is the number that decides the email question: Rapid Garden POS's analysis of 148 US garden centers found that only 30% run a loyalty program — yet in those stores, loyalty members drive 56.64% of all revenue, with a median basket of $106.75 (5.24% over non-members), and loyalty sales growing 36% year over year. The loyalty program IS the email list. Email's job in this business: manage the 12-week flood without drowning it, keep the other 40 weeks alive, and turn the spring crowd into the loyalty base that funds the whole year. Here is how the best tool runs it.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The spring-rush engine. Sixty percent of the year in twelve weeks — the marketing plan is a countdown. The machinery: the February warm-up (the list has been quiet since Christmas; the "we're opening the greenhouse" email wakes it before the first truck), the weekly "what's ready" cadence (March through May, the trucks land and the email goes out the same day — the center that emails the new arrivals sells them; the one that posts a sign doesn't), the weather-delay comm (the late-frost warning is the trust builder — "cover your tomatoes tonight" from a nursery is the email people forward), the sell-through sequence (the weekend email with what bloomed this week, the Mother's Day gift rush, the Memorial Day last-chance). The trap: you cannot build the list during the rush — the register line is too long and the staff is too busy. The list is built in fall and winter. Scored on the tools: time-sensitive sends, same-day reliability, and segmentable lists (vegetable people vs perennial people vs houseplant people).
  2. The planting-calendar education drip. This is the expertise moat, and it is the reason the independent nursery exists at all. The machinery: the zone-specific "what to plant now" drip (the customer's zip code decides the advice — the email that says "your last frost date is April 18, plant these this weekend" beats Home Depot on the one axis that matters), the new-gardener series (the 2022 pandemic cohort: 80% of households, millions of first-timers who still don't know what a "hardiness zone" is — teach them in March and they buy from you all summer), the variety introductions (the trial-garden winners, the new introductions — Fort Collins Nursery runs a new-plants column to its 20,000-customer list, and the customers come in asking for them by name), the failure-prevention emails (the "why your hydrangea didn't bloom" email sells the replacement AND the fertilizer). Scored on the tools: automation drip builders, rich content templates, and easy list segmentation by interest.
  3. The loyalty program machine. The data is the whole argument: 56.64% of revenue from loyalty members in the 30% of stores that run programs, 36% year-over-year growth, top stores pulling over 75% of sales from members (Rapid Garden POS, 148 centers). The machinery: the register enrollment (the polite ask at checkout — the industry's own guidance says about half of customers say yes when asked in person, and the in-store ask converts far above any online popup), the tier ladder (points per dollar, the "member Monday" early-access sale, the birthday reward), the loyalty-club-only specials (Esbenshade's runs them on a limited basis so walk-ins aren't alienated — scarcity sells, permanent discounts train), the purchase-triggered follow-ups (bought the Japanese maple? The care-guide email arrives Thursday, and the "companion plants for that maple" email arrives Saturday). Scored on the tools: coupon and promotion generation, purchase-triggered automations, and segments that talk to the POS data.
  4. The fall + holiday second season. Spring is the flood, but fall and Christmas are the second and third acts — and the center that emails them owns them. The machinery: the mums campaign (September-October — the emblematic fall plant, the porch refresh, the pumpkin-season cross-sell; customers treat them as seasonal decor like poinsettias, so sell the display, not the lifespan), the holiday greenery launch (poinsettias are still "the plant for Christmas" — but Shelmerdine Garden Centre's Nicole Bent warns they are "getting harder and harder to sell" against the Walmart $9.99 commodity: the garden center wins with dressed-up versions, better pot covers, colors beyond red — and the email that shows the $14.99 difference), the winter houseplant lane (the trade's own houseplant report: 40% of centers see consistent year-round houseplant sales — the January email keeps the greenhouse list warm when it's 20 degrees outside), the gift-card season (the November email, because the garden center gift card is the gift the gardener actually wants). Scored on the tools: seasonal launch templates, beautiful product emails, and the November/December cadence.
  5. The workshop + event calendar engine. The workshop is the list-builder, the event is the foot-traffic machine, and both are email-powered. The machinery: the spring event (Family Tree Nursery's March event: three days, elaborate displays, "cooking up containers" workshops, children's activities, live music — parking at a premium; the email list is what fills it), the class drip (container workshops in April, herb starts in May, wreath-making in December — the email announces, the sign-up form captures, the reminder email fills the seats), the Perennial Palooza model (Fort Collins Nursery's March program: three days, #1 perennials/grasses/vines at 50% off — the sale that starts the season's customer programs), the year-round touch (one workshop a month keeps the list opening emails in August and January, when nothing is blooming). Scored on the tools: event templates, RSVP forms, and reminder sequences.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first — in this order:

  1. The spring-launch sequence. Trigger: mid-February. Sequence: the "we're opening the greenhouse" wake-up (the list has slept since Christmas — one email before the first truck, not five emails the week of), the weekly "what's ready" cadence March through May (the truck lands Tuesday, the email goes out Tuesday — the center that emails the arrivals sells them), the Mother's Day gift rush (the weekend email with the ready-made baskets — the gift the gardener actually wants, done for the non-gardener buyer), the Memorial Day last-chance (the spring inventory closeout that clears the benches). The rule: the spring is won in the 60 days BEFORE the first weekend, not during it.
  2. The truck-unload flash. Trigger: delivery day. Sequence: the same-day "fresh on the benches" email (photos from the truck — the new hydrangea varieties, the 5-gallon maples, the herb starts), the 48-hour follow-up for the ones still on the bench (the "we still have 30 of the good ones" email sells the stragglers), the loyalty-member early access for the rare arrivals (the $200 specimen tree never hits the public email — members get it first, and the membership is the reason to sign up). The rule: perishable inventory moves fastest when the email arrives while the plants are still on the cart.
  3. The planting-calendar drip. Trigger: signup or season start, segmented by zip. Sequence: the welcome + zone confirmation ("your last frost date is April 18 — here's what goes in this weekend"), the weekly "plant this now" emails through the season (vegetable people get the tomatoes; perennial people get the division guide; houseplant people get the repotting tutorial), the failure-prevention series (the "why your hydrangea didn't bloom" email sells the replacement and the fertilizer), the fall extension (the "second planting season" email — August sowings, fall garlic, the season most gardeners miss entirely).

  5. The fall + holiday second season. Trigger: Labor Day. Sequence: the mums campaign (September — the porch refresh, the pumpkin-season cross-sell; sell the display, not the lifespan), the holiday greenery preview (October/November — the dressed-up poinsettias that beat the Walmart $9.99 commodity, the Norfolk Island pines, the fresh garlands; the email that shows the difference is the one that sells the $14.99 plant), the gift-card season (the November "give them the nursery" email — the gift card the gardener actually wants), the January houseplant lane (the trade's own report shows houseplants sell year-round at 40% of centers — the January email keeps the greenhouse list warm when it's 20 degrees outside).

The honest catches

  • Email is not a POS. The loyalty data lives in your point-of-sale software (Rapid Garden POS, Eagle by Epicor — the systems the trade actually runs); the purchase history lives there too. The email's job is the communication around the purchase — the care guide, the companion-plant follow-up, the event invite — not the points ledger. Don't shop for an email tool by asking "does it track loyalty points"; it shouldn't. Pick the tool that triggers off the POS data best.
  • You can't build the list during the rush. The 60%-in-12-weeks calendar means the spring staff is underwater — and the register ask is the highest-converting enrollment channel there is. The list is built in fall and winter: the holiday season, the January houseplant lane, the workshop signups. If you start list-building in March, you've already lost the season.
  • Don't email-discount against the big box. Shelmerdine Garden Centre's warning is the lesson: the Walmart $9.99 poinsettia will beat you on price every time, so don't send price emails. Send expertise emails — the frost alert, the planting guide, the "why our $14.99 poinsettia looks like $14.99" photo. The trade press has the phrase for it: every email needs solid content, and "20 percent off bedding plants" is not solid content.
  • Weather whiplash is a trust opportunity, not a cancellation. The late-frost warning email ("cover your tomatoes tonight — here's the 30-second version") is the most-forwarded email a nursery will ever send. The centers that email the frost alert own the summer; the ones that stay quiet watch their customers' tomatoes die on the competitor's advice.
  • Spring volume breaks per-email math. Brevo looks cheap until the 12-week flood has you sending weekly "what's ready" blasts + the truck-unload flashes + the loyalty sequences to 3,000 contacts. Model the flood, not the quiet January.

FAQ

How big does a nursery email list need to be?
Bigger than you'd guess for a single location: a few thousand — every customer who walks through the register line in spring, every workshop attendee, every loyalty member. Fort Collins Nursery emails 20,000 customers. The list is worth more per subscriber than most retail — the loyalty data says the median member basket is $106.75, and the loyalty base drives the majority of revenue.

How do you collect emails when customers are rushing through the register?
You don't — you collect them in fall and winter, when the line is short and the staff has breath. The in-person ask at checkout is still the highest-converting channel the trade knows (about half say yes when asked face to face), but it works in November, not in April. The workshop signups, the holiday pre-orders, and the gift-card season are your enrollment windows.

Do we need a loyalty program to make email work?
No — but the data makes the case hard to ignore: only 30% of garden centers run one, and in those stores members drive 56.64% of revenue with sales growing 36% a year. Email and loyalty are the same machine: the program gives people a reason to hand over the email, and the email gives the program a reason to exist.

Should we really email in winter?
Yes — winter is the list's enrollment season, not its dead season. The holiday greenery preview, the gift-card push, the January houseplant lane (houseplants sell year-round at 40% of centers per the trade's own report), and the February spring-warm-up sequence mean the list never goes cold. The center that emails in January owns March.
