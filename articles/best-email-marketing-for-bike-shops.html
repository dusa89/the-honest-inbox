The bike shop business has inverted in five years, and most shops haven't rewritten the playbook. The NBDA's 2026 Bicycle Consumer Report says it plainly: consumers now prioritize trust and experience over price, and e-bikes are 50%+ of revenue and growing (Bicycle Retailer & Industry News, May 4, 2026). The US e-bike market is estimated around $3.78B in 2025, heading toward $5.78B by 2031 (Mordor Intelligence, via industry roundup, October 2026) — and the e-bike buyer is not a cyclist. They're a commuter, a parent, a 60-year-old with a bad knee who needs someone to explain the battery. The shop that wins is not the one with the lowest price on a derailleur — it's the one that owns the service relationship: the spring tune-up calendar, the e-bike education sequence, the repair-ticket reactivation, the Saturday group ride. Every one of those runs on email. Here is how the best tool for the shop runs them.

What this guide scores

Every bike-shop guide on this site is scored against the shop's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The spring-tune engine. The calendar is the product. Every year, the same flood: March arrives, every bike in the county comes out of the garage, and the service queue goes from empty to six weeks deep in a fortnight. The shop that emails in February — the "beat the spring rush" booking sequence — spreads the flood across the slow months and fills the stand in the dead of winter. The machinery: the pre-season booking campaign (February/March, the "your bike sat all winter, skip the April line" push), the tune-up ladder (the $90 standard vs. the $150 advanced vs. the $350 full-suspension — per The Bike Hub's published menu — upsold at booking, not at pickup), the reminder sequence for the booked-but-haven't-dropped-off, and the post-tune upsell (the worn chain spotted on the stand becomes the new-chain email the next week). Scored on the tools: can it run a date-triggered seasonal campaign to a segmented list, with booking reminders?
  2. The e-bike service ladder. The highest-margin customer you'll ever meet. E-bikes are 50%+ of revenue and growing — and the e-bike buyer buys MORE, not less: the $120 e-bike tune-up, the firmware updates, the battery diagnostics, the accessories (the lock, the panniers, the second charger). The US e-bike market's growth (~7.32% CAGR through 2031 per Mordor) means this ladder gets longer every year. The machinery: the buyer education sequence (5–7 emails over 30 days — battery care, charging best practices, when to bring it in for diagnostics — the exact sequence Workstand's bike-shop guide prescribes), the 6-month service reminder ("your bike has been on the road for six months — time for a quick tune-up?" — the most natural upsell in cycling retail, per the same guide), the recall/service-bulletin broadcast (the safety email that proves the shop is the responsible party), and the trade-up track (the two-year-old e-bike owner is your next new-e-bike buyer). Scored on the tools: can it run a purchase-triggered 30-day education drip, and segment e-bike owners from acoustic-bike owners?
  3. The service-ticket reactivation. The repair customer is the next bike buyer. Industry modeling puts the average bike repair around $80 (financialmodelslab, 2026) — and the person who brings a bike in for a repair is already in your shop, already trusting your wrenches, already one conversation from a new bike. The machinery: the service-reminder trigger (the bike that had a repair 12 months ago gets the annual tune-up nudge — the automated version of the 6-month post-purchase reminder), the declined-work follow-up (the customer who said no to the new chain in March gets the "how's that chain holding up" in June — half of declined work is just bad timing), the lapsed-rider win-back (the ticket from two years ago, the customer who hasn't been back — the "we miss your bike" email), and the repair-to-retail bridge (the brake-pad customer gets the group-ride invitation, not a coupon). Scored on the tools: can it trigger on service-ticket dates and re-engage lapsed customers automatically?
  4. The club-ride community list. The moat Amazon can't copy — from the service side. The NBDA's headline finding is that trust and experience beat price; the shop that hosts the Saturday no-drop ride owns the community the way the indie bookstore owns the author event (see our bookstores vertical — same moat, different engine). The machinery: the ride-announcement sequence (the Thursday "Saturday's route" email with the pace groups and the coffee stop — the email that fills the parking lot), the ride capture (the sign-in sheet IS the list-building moment — the new rider who shows up for the free ride is next month's tune-up customer), the photo recap (the ride photos in Monday's email — the social proof that sells the next ride), and the advocacy track (the club riders are the review-writers, the referral engines, the ones who answer "where should I buy a bike" for their whole office). Scored on the tools: can it run a weekly recurring announcement to a segmented ride list, and capture contacts at the event?
  5. The margin-defense engine. You can't win on price, so stop trying. The shop loses the accessory sale to the internet every time it competes on price — and wins it every time it competes on fit, service, and trust (the NBDA finding, stated as strategy). The machinery: the fit-first framing (the email that sells the fitting, not the saddle — the $200 fitting that makes the $80 saddle irreplaceable), the bundle (the new-bike email that bundles the lock, the lights, the first tune-up — the accessories sold at purchase have the highest margin and the lowest price-sensitivity), the "we install what we sell" guarantee (the accessory bought online has no one to install it — the shop's install service is the product), and the winter list-build (the dead months are for growing the list — the clinic emails, the maintenance-class signups, the "ask a mechanic" series — so spring starts with an audience). Scored on the tools: is it cheap enough for a shop's margins, and does it make the owned list — not the marketplace — the center of the business?

Notice what's missing: no generic "monthly newsletter" (your product is the season and the service calendar — the newsletter is the calendar, or it's noise), no discount spam on tune-ups (the tune-up is the thing that pays the rent — discounting it is paying customers to leave), no platform dependency (the list lives in your tool, not in someone's marketplace). The season fills the stands, the e-bike ladder climbs the margins, the tickets reactivate the buyers, the rides build the moat, and the margin engine keeps the list — and the shop — alive. A bike shop whose email is "the monthly newsletter" is running a service business on a broadcast calendar.

The honest catches

You cannot win the accessory sale on price. This is the shop's version of the rented-audience trap, and it wears the friendliest face: the customer who test-rides in your shop and buys the helmet online. The NBDA's finding is the strategy — trust and experience beat price. The machinery is the fit-first framing (sell the fitting, not the saddle), the bundle (accessories sold at bike purchase have the highest margin and the lowest price sensitivity), and the install guarantee (the part bought online has nobody to install it — your service bay is the product). The shop that races the internet to the bottom on price loses the margin it needs to keep the wrenches employed.

Email can fill the shop faster than the wrenches. The spring-tune engine has a failure mode: the campaign works too well, the stand is booked six weeks out, and the next email you send is an apology. Cap the booking sequence at the stand's real capacity — the "beat the rush" campaign needs a "the rush is here" off-switch. The shop that emails past its capacity is manufacturing its own bad reviews.

The tune-up ladder only works if the base price is honest. The temptation is to advertise the $49 loss-leader tune-up and upsell on the stand. One customer who feels ambushed and the trust — the thing the NBDA says you sell — is gone. Publish the real ladder (the $90 standard, the $150 advanced, the e-bike and suspension tiers), and let the customer climb it themselves. The upsell offered at booking is service; the upsell sprung at pickup is a shakedown.

Winter is the list-building season. The dead months feel like the time to go quiet — they're the opposite. The clinic emails, the maintenance-class signups, the "ask a mechanic" series, the indoor-trainer content — winter is when the shop grows the audience that spring monetizes. The shop that emails all winter starts March with a list; the shop that goes dark starts March with a prayer. The season belongs to whoever built the list in the off-season.

The creator inversion has a boundary. The partnership with local cycling creators works — until the shop starts treating the creator's audience as its own. The creator's list is rented attention; the shop's list is owned relationships. Cross-promote, co-host the ride, share the content — but the shop's email program answers to the shop's customers, not to someone else's subscriber count.

An event with no capture is theater. Forty riders on a Saturday and six new contacts is a nice morning, not a business. The sign-in sheet, the QR code on the route card, the "first ride free, second ride's on the list" — the capture IS the ride's job, not its afterthought. The riders go home; the list stays. Plan the capture before you plan the route.

Should a bike shop use Kit?

For the shop's customer list: no — that's this page's honest inversion. Kit is the right tool for the cycling creators (their audiences, their launches, their courses), and the wrong tool for tune-up calendars, e-bike education drips, and ride announcements: steep subscriber-based pricing and templates built for creators selling content to followers. The partnership version works: the creator promotes the shop's group ride to their Kit list, the shop promotes the creator's maintenance video to its own list. Two tools, one community.

Does this work with my shop's POS?

The engines on this page trigger on dates and purchases — which means the tool needs to know about them. Before you pick, check the integration list for your POS (Lightspeed, Ascend, and the other bike-shop systems) or confirm a Zapier path exists. The shop whose tool can't see the ticket date is running the service-ticket reactivation by hand — which means it isn't running it. Integration is a selection criterion, not an afterthought.
