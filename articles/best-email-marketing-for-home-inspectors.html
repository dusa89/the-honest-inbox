A home inspector is the rare trade business whose customer is not the person paying the invoice. The numbers frame the fight: a $6.8 billion US market in 2026 with 33,873 businesses (IBISWorld Building Inspectors report, published February 2026), growing 2.0% a year — and no company holding more than 5% share, the most fragmented fight on this site. And 78% of inspection business comes through agent referrals (Spectora's 2026 Industry Report). So the inspector has two audiences, not one: the AGENT, who is the customer — the referral source that must be stayed top-of-mind every week of the spring market; and the BUYER, who is the transaction — the one-job customer whose report must arrive in hours, whose ancillaries fund the margin, and whose review is the second revenue. The inspectors that win run six engines: the agent-referral engine (the realtor partnership lane), the report-delivery follow-up (the 24-hour promise), the ancillary upsell ladder (radon, sewer, termite, mold — the margin), the pre-listing pipeline (seller-side growth), the 11th-month warranty timer (the recurring-job machine hiding inside one-time inspections), and the seasonal inspection calendar. Here is how the best tool for the firm runs them.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

1. The agent-referral engine. The agent is the customer — and 78% of the business flows through the referral, so the referral must be farmed like a crop. The cadence: the weekly value email (not a pitch — one genuinely useful thing: the month's most common defect found, the seasonal watch item, the plain-language explainer the agent can forward to their own buyers), the office-visit follow-up (the drop-by with the sample report packet, followed by the "great meeting you — here's the report sample I mentioned" email within 24 hours), the closing-day touch (the agent's buyer closed — the firm emails the agent the congratulations and the buyer the review ask; the agent watches both), and the quarterly agent-appreciation (the top-5 referring agents get the handwritten note plus the email version — the list knows exactly who referred what, so the thank-you is specific, not generic). The engine's real product is familiarity: when the buyer's agent reaches for the phone during the contingency window, the first name in the head wins. The email machine keeps the firm's name first in that head, week after week, spring market after spring market.

2. The report-delivery follow-up. The 24-hour promise is the referral currency — accuracy (53%) and timeliness (46%) are what the agents survey for. The flow: the inspection ends, the report uploads, and the automation fires — the buyer gets the report link with the plain-language "what this means for your negotiation" framing, the AGENT gets the one-page plain-language summary (the agent does not read 60 pages; the summary is what the agent forwards to negotiate), and both get the review ask timed to the report, not the inspection. The review ask rides the peak: the buyer just learned the truth about their future home — that is the highest-trust moment in the transaction. The follow-up ladder after the report: day 3 — the ancillary reminder ("the report mentioned the older roof — here's the sewer-scope note" — soft, not salesy); day 14 — the agent check-in ("report landed okay? anything the buyer's side needs?"). The firm that delivers the report and goes silent teaches the agent that the firm is a vendor. The firm that follows the report teaches the agent that the firm is a partner.

3. The ancillary upsell ladder. The standard inspection at $300-500 pays the day; the ancillaries pay the month. Radon is the engine: $125-200 net profit per test, industry capture ~46%, high performers at 55-60%+ — the ladder that closes the gap is email. The sequence: at booking — the "while we're already there" attach (radon monitor drop-off adds one visit, not one trip; the sewer scope rides the same appointment); at report delivery — the findings-triggered attach (the report flagged the older cast-iron waste line — the sewer-scope email writes itself); seasonally — the radon winter push (closed-house season is testing season; the email goes to last year's buyers whose homes were never tested). Termite ($75-125) and pool/spa ($100-200) ride the property-feature logic — the list knows which addresses have pools. The honest math the firm should track: every 10 points of radon capture on 100 inspections a year is $1,250-2,000 of near-pure-margin revenue the firm was already standing inside of. The email ladder is the cheapest capture-rate lever in the business.

4. The pre-listing pipeline + the 11th-month warranty timer. Two second-job machines hiding inside the one-job business. The pre-listing lane: the LISTING agent is a different customer than the buyer's agent — the listing agent wants the seller's home inspected BEFORE it lists ($300-500) to pre-empt the renegotiation chaos the buyer's inspection always causes. The email pitch to listing agents carries the NAR-shape logic: the inspection contingency is where deals wobble, and a clean pre-listing report is the seller's armor. The 11th-month timer: every new-construction inspection the firm ever did is a timed asset — the builder's one-year warranty expires at month 12, so the firm emails the buyer at month 10-11: "your builder warranty expires soon — here's the 11th-month inspection that catches what the first year broke." Zero marketing cost, a second $300-500 job, and the buyer remembers the firm as the one who protected them twice. The timer runs on the inspection date the firm already has — the list is the asset, the automation is the harvester.

5. The seasonal inspection calendar + the review engine. Spring is the Super Bowl: the market wakes, listings flood, and the agents' phones ring — the firm emails its agent list in February with the "spring capacity is booking" note, not April when everyone is already booked. The seasonal gears: the spring surge (agent capacity warnings + the buyer "inspection contingency" education email), the summer storm season (roof and drainage findings spike — the follow-up content writes itself), the fall radon season (closed-house testing, the winter push), and the winter planning season (the slowest inspection months — the firm emails agents the year-in-review: defects found, reports delivered, the "here's what we caught together" note that no competitor sends). The review engine closes the loop: the buyer review ask at report delivery (peak trust), the agent testimonial ask at closing (the agent whose deal the firm saved is the agent who will say so on camera), and the Google review funnel — 33,873 businesses, no company above 5%, and the fight is the map pack. The firm with 300 reviews in a county of 40 inspectors does not compete on price. It does not have to.

The honest catches

The agent is the customer AND the buyer is the customer — two audiences, never crossed. Agents get the referral-maintenance cadence (value, familiarity, the partnership tone). Buyers get the transaction flow (booking, report, review — logistics and trust). The agent who gets the buyer's booking confirmation learns the firm is sloppy with lists; the buyer who gets the agent-appreciation email learns the firm sees them as inventory. Two lists, two cadences, zero cross-sends — the same discipline as every other vertical on this site, with higher stakes, because the agent audience IS the revenue.

The deal-killer paradox: thoroughness is the product, not the enemy. The industry's oldest fear is being labeled the inspector who kills deals — and the forum lore is full of inspectors who learned that "agent-friendly" can mean "soft." The survey data settles it: 53% of agents keep referring for accuracy and thoroughness — the top factor, ahead of communication and timeliness. Agents refer the inspector whose report they can hand a buyer without translating it (48% want clear, easy-to-understand reports). The email machine's job is to make the thoroughness legible: the plain-language summary, the "what this means for the negotiation" framing, the report the agent can forward with confidence. The inspector who softens findings to protect the deal is renting referrals. The inspector who makes findings clear is owning them.

Report speed is the referral currency — the 24-hour promise must be real. Timeliness is 46% of the referral decision, and the contingency window is 7-10 days of pure pressure. The email that says "report within 24 hours" while the report takes three days is not a marketing problem — it is a referral-killer. The automation should only promise what the operations deliver: if the firm delivers same-day, the email says same-day; if the firm delivers next-morning, the email says next-morning. The fastest way to lose an agent is to make them chase a report while their buyer's contingency clock burns. Automate the delivery, the summary, and the notification — then keep the promise the automation makes.

The buyer's data is not your marketing list. The buyer gave their email to book an inspection, not to join a newsletter. The transaction flow (booking, report, review ask, the 11th-month timer) is consented by the transaction itself. The marketing list — the seasonal content, the referral asks, the "know anyone buying?" emails — needs the buyer's actual opt-in, and the firm should ask for it plainly at the review-ask moment ("want the seasonal home-watch emails?"). The agent list is cleaner: the business relationship is the consent, and every agent email carries the unsubscribe. The firm that spams buyers is trading one $400 job for the review that never comes.

The contingency window means instant booking or the competitor wins. Seven to ten days from offer to inspection contingency — the agent books the inspector who answers first. The email machine's job here is the confirmation, not the capture: the booking form, the instant confirmation email, the calendar invite, the "what to expect" pre-inspection email (bring the disclosure, clear the attic access, the pets plan). Every hour of silence after the booking request is an hour the agent spends calling the next name on their list. The firm does not need the fanciest automation on this bench — it needs the fastest confirmation.

FAQ

How often should I email agents without being annoying? The cadence answer: weekly value, monthly ask. One short useful email a week — the defect of the week, the seasonal watch item, the one-paragraph explainer the agent can forward to buyers — and one referral-relevant ask a month (the capacity note, the new-ancillary announcement, the sample-report refresh). The spam line is not the count; it is the pitch ratio. The agent who gets four value emails and one ask reads all five. The agent who gets five asks unsubscribes after two. The 78% referral figure is a relationship number — relationships are built on giving, not asking.

Same list for buyers and agents? Never. Two lists, two cadences, two tones. The agent list gets the partnership voice (familiarity, capacity, the "here's what we caught together" notes). The buyer list gets the transaction voice (logistics, the report, the review, the 11th-month timer). The cross-send is the fastest way to teach both audiences that the firm does not know who they are. Segment at booking: the form knows which one they are.

What about the 11th-month warranty inspection? The timer: tag every new-construction buyer at the inspection, then automate the month-10 email — "your builder's one-year warranty expires at month 12; the 11th-month inspection catches what the first year broke before the builder stops paying." It is a second $300-500 job at zero marketing cost, and it is the highest-trust email the firm will ever send — the firm is protecting the buyer from the builder's clock. The inspectors who run this timer call it the easiest revenue in the business; the ones who don't are leaving a second job on every new-construction inspection they ever did.

How do I get agents to actually open the emails? The subject line is the sample report. Agents open what helps them close: "the 3 defects killing deals this month (with photos)," "the contingency-clock email your buyers need," "the plain-language summary your last 5 reports shipped with." The body should be skimmable in 30 seconds — the agent reads email between showings. And the deepest lever is the forward: write the email so the agent can forward it to their buyer with one line added. The email that gets forwarded is the email that gets the next referral.
