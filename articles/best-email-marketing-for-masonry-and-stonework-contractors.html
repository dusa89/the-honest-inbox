The 5 jobs a masonry shop actually needs email for

  1. The portfolio funnel. Masonry sells by sight: the crumbling chimney next to the repointed one, the tired concrete slab next to the new stone patio. A photo-first email sequence — one job per send, honest captions, the ticket range beside it — turns last summer's portfolio into this spring's estimate requests. No other marketing format does this; a flyer can't drip-feed 40 jobs, and social feeds bury them in a day.
  2. The inspection-season engine. Masonry runs on seasons: spring inspections after winter's freeze-thaw, fall tuckpointing before the freeze returns (mortar cures best in mild, dry weather — Angi). A spring "chimney inspection" email to last year's list and a fall "book repointing before the freeze" sequence fill the two shoulder seasons that decide the year's revenue.
  3. The outdoor-living ticket builder. The patio is the wedge, not the job. A 20x20 paver patio at $3,200–$10,000 (Angi 2026) buys the relationship; the post-install sequence sells the phases: the fire pit, the outdoor kitchen footing, the seating wall, the veneer accent on the house. Each phase is its own campaign, each priced like a job because it is one.
  4. The neighbor-referral engine. A new stone wall is visible from the street for decades — every neighbor is a prospect who has already seen the work. The post-job "show your neighbors" email (a photo of THEIR wall, a referral incentive, and permission to put the sign in the yard for two weeks) turns each job into a street.
  5. The winter list-warmth drip. Masonry crews go quiet in deep winter, and a list that hears nothing for four months is dead by spring. One honest monthly email — a finished job, a material explainer, a "what to watch on your chimney this winter" note — keeps the shop the first call when the thaw comes. This is the sequence most shops skip and the one that decides April.

The 5 campaigns that pay for the account

  1. The 10-job portfolio drip. Ten sends, ten finished jobs, each with before/after photos and the honest ticket range. "Chimney repoint, $1,200, 3 days." This is the list's education: what the work costs, what it looks like, that you're the crew that did it. Runs year-round to new subscribers; the shop's best salesperson, automated.
  2. The spring inspection blast + sequence. One blast: "freeze-thaw season is over — here's what to check on your chimney and brick." Followed by a 4-email sequence on spalling, cracked crowns, and efflorescence, each ending in the inspection booking link. Timed to the first warm week, not the calendar.
  3. The patio-phase ladder. Every completed patio triggers a timed sequence: month 1, the fire pit add-on; month 3, the seating wall; month 6, the veneer accent. A $5,000 patio becomes $15,000 of lifetime work per homeowner, one honest phase at a time.
  4. The storm-and-freeze alert. After a hard freeze or a hail event: "walk your chimney tonight — here's the 2-minute check." Useful first, sales second. The shop that sends the warning gets the call when the homeowner finds the crack.
  5. The winter warmth monthly. December through February: one email a month — a finished job story, a material explainer ("why natural stone veneer costs what it costs"), the "what to watch this winter" note. Keeps the list alive so spring doesn't start from zero.

The honest catches

Email never wins the 2am emergency call — when the chimney is leaking into the living room, the homeowner calls the first truck they can reach, not the nicest newsletter. Email wins everything around the emergency: the inspection that finds it first, the referral after, the phase-2 job next year. Second: never publish a price list that anchors low. Every job's range belongs in context ("a 20x20 patio runs $3,200–$10,000 depending on material" — Angi 2026); a flat "$1,200 chimney" email trains every reader to haggle against your average. Third: winter quiet is real — keep the monthly drip honest about the season ("we're scheduling spring now") instead of inventing winter discounts that train customers to wait. Fourth: mortar matching is the trade's pride — the email should say it, because the cheapest quote in the inbox never matches the mortar and the homeowner learns that too late.

FAQ

How often should a masonry shop email its list?

In season, twice a month: the portfolio drip does the heavy lifting and the inspection/seasonal sequences ride alongside. In winter, once a month is enough to stay alive — the "warmth" email. More than weekly burns the list; silence for four months kills it.

What should go in the first email to a new subscriber?

Three things: the 3 best before/after photos you've ever taken (this trade sells by sight), the honest ranges for the two jobs people ask about most (tuckpointing, patios), and one clear path to an estimate. Save the credentials for email two — lead with the work.

Is stone veneer worth covering in email content?

Yes — it's the most misunderstood product in the trade. Homeowners hear "$14.25 a square foot" for thin veneer flats (a real 2026 material rate, Select Stone) and assume that's the installed price; it isn't — corners, wall prep, freight, and the mason's labor all stack on top. The shop that explains this honestly wins the trust before the quote.

Do masonry customers actually read email?

The homeowners who hire masons are planning $5,000–$25,000 projects — they research like buyers of anything expensive. A photo-heavy email with real ticket ranges outperforms every flyer the trade has ever printed, and unlike a yard sign, it reaches the whole street at once.
