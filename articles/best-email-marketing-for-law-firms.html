What a law firm actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. Intake follow-up. This is the money. More than 70% of clients initially contact attorneys by phone, 46% by phone first and 27% by email (CallRail/MyCase data), and half of prospective clients expect a same-day response (FindLaw, via mycase.com). An automated sequence — instant confirmation, a next-day check-in with the attorney bio, a 3-day "still happy to help" — catches the consultations that leak out of a busy office. Selzy's 2026 benchmark of 602 million emails found automated messages were opened at 31.4% vs 11% for mass campaigns and generated 5.3x the clicks (lifestyle.clientim.com, Sept 2026) — automation, not newsletters, is where legal email earns its keep.
  2. Seminar and webinar nurture drips. Estate-planning, elder-law, and business-formation firms live on educational seminars. A 4-email sequence — invitation, reminder 7 days out, reminder day-of, recording-follow-up with the consultation booking link — is the entire playbook. If the tool has a built-in webinar module, the registration, reminder, and replay emails come free.
  3. Client-update newsletters. 68% of legal clients appreciate updates on relevant legal changes that might affect them (profiletree.com), and firms that send regular newsletters report 27% higher client retention (same source — their numbers, treat as directional). Legal opens are strong: industry benchmarks from MailerLite/ConstantContact data put legal services at 42.45% open rates (au.hostadvice.com, older benchmark — benchmark data, not a promise), and GrowLaw cites a 22% average with personalized subject lines lifting opens ~50%.
  4. Review-request sequences. Post-matter-close, one email: thank-you + Google review link. Two-email maximum; legal clients read a third ask as pressure. Reviews are the intake funnel for PI, family, and criminal-defense practices — this sequence is not optional.
  5. Referral-request sequences. Referrals are the highest-value intake source in legal, and they can be asked for systematically: a post-matter email 30 days after close ("if you know anyone who needs X, here's how to refer them") plus a quarterly touch to past clients. This is the sequence almost no firm builds and the one that compounds the longest.

Notice what's missing: no landing-page-builder Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. Your practice-management software (Clio, MyCase) already runs matters and billing; the email tool only has to hold the contact list, send the campaigns, and run a handful of automations. 41% of law firms already use email newsletters (GrowLaw — up from 26% in 2020), so this is not an exotic move; it's a competitive baseline you're either meeting or not.

The bar-rules catch (read this before you send anything)

Lawyer advertising is regulated, and the ABA Model Rules 7.1–7.4 set the floor that most states build on (growlaw.co's 2026 compliance guide; rule text at americanbar.org). The plain version:

  • 7.1 — nothing false or misleading. No outcome guarantees in a newsletter, no "best divorce lawyer in town" claims, no omitting material facts. Your email copy gets the same standard as your billboard.
  • 7.2 — no paying for recommendations. You can pay for the email tool; you cannot pay someone for sending you the referrals that email brings in. And every ad must include the name and office address of at least one lawyer responsible for its content — your footer needs to actually carry this.
  • 7.3 — no targeted solicitation. This is the big one for email. You may not direct a communication to a specific person you know needs legal help in a particular matter, for financial gain — a PI firm may not email accident victims it found in police reports. Mass newsletters to your opted-in list are fine; targeted cold outreach to a person in need is not. (Note: the ABA's 2018 amendments dropped the old "advertising material" labeling requirement, but some states still have their own quirks — Florida historically even required pre-approval for ads. Check your state bar.)
  • 7.4 — no "specialist" claims without certification. "Board-certified family law specialist" in a subject line is only legal if the certification is from a state-approved or ABA-accredited organization and you name it.

The practical impact on tool choice: any tool works under these rules — compliance lives in your copy and your list practices, not your software. But it argues for simple, honest tools over aggressive growth-hack platforms, and for keeping a consent trail (opt-in records) on every list, because a bar complaint starts with "where did you get my address?"

The 4 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these four first:

  1. The intake follow-up sequence. Trigger: new website inquiry or call. Email 1 (immediate): confirmation + what happens next + the attorney's bio. Email 2 (next day): "the consultation is free and takes 20 minutes — here's the booking link." Email 3 (day 3): the soft check-in with one useful resource for their situation. Three emails, then stop — a fourth reads as chasing. Given half of prospects expect a same-day response (FindLaw), the speed of email 1 matters more than its polish.
  2. The seminar drip. Invitation to the targeted list (estate-planning seminar → 45+ demographic segment; business-formation seminar → business contacts). Reminder 7 days out, reminder day-of, and the recording email 24 hours after with the consultation booking link — attendees who don't book get a 14-day "questions the seminar raised?" follow-up. This is the sequence GetResponse's webinar module was built for.
  3. The review-request sequence. Trigger: matter closed in your practice-management system. Email 1: thank-you + direct Google review link, next business day. If no review after 14 days, email 2: the gentle nudge. Two emails maximum, then retire it — a third ask reads as pressure, and pressure in legal marketing is both a bad look and a bar-rules smell.
  4. The referral and reactivation drip. 30 days after matter close: the referral ask ("if you know anyone facing X, here's how they can reach us"). Quarterly: a short "legal changes that might affect you" email to past clients — the 68%-appreciate-updates finding (profiletree.com) is your content permission slip, and past clients are the cheapest source of high-intent new matters you own.

The honest catches

Your past-client list import is the real setup cost — and the consent record matters more in legal. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting from your practice-management system and cleaning it. Keep the opt-in trail: prior professional relationship is a defensible basis for client newsletters in most jurisdictions, but "where did you get my address" is the first question in any complaint. The deliverability payoff is real too — a stale import is how firm lists end up in spam. Our deliverability audit checklist covers the fix.

Testimonials and outcomes are the ethics tripwire. Some states restrict or ban client testimonials in advertising; Model Rule 7.1's "no false or misleading" standard means a client's glowing result quote can be misleading if it implies a guaranteed outcome. Write review asks as "share your experience," never "tell them we won your case" — and check your state's rule before publishing any of it.

The $42-to-$1 ROI figure is old. The widely cited "email returns $42 for every $1 spent" comes from a Litmus study that legal marketing sites still quote; Litmus's own 2025 research puts the more measured band at $10–$50 for most senders (growlaw.co). Directionally true for owned-list email in legal — it's why the channel beats buying leads — but it's a vendor study, not a measurement of your firm. Your real number is consultations booked per campaign; track that, not the industry stat.

Your list is small — that's a feature. Selzy's 2026 benchmark found campaigns to under 100 recipients got 23.6% opens and 4.8% CTR vs 5.7% and 0.4% at 100k+ scale (lifestyle.clientim.com) — roughly 4x the opens and 12x the clicks for the small, targeted send. Segment by practice area and matter type: the estate-planning client and the PI client should never get the same email.

FAQ

What's the cheapest email tool for a solo practice?

At typical firm list sizes (500–2,000 contacts, estimate), MailerLite's paid plans are the cheapest of the five tools here with real automations included — paid starts from $10/mo. Brevo is cheaper if you send weekly to a bigger list ($9/mo for 5,000 emails). All prices checked October 3, 2026 — verify live before checkout.

Can I email past clients about new services?

Generally yes — a prior attorney-client relationship is the standard defensible basis for firm newsletters in most states — but it is not a blank check: provide an unsubscribe in every email, keep the opt-in/relationship record, and check your state's bar rules for variations. This is general information, not legal advice.

Do the bar advertising rules really apply to email newsletters?

Yes. ABA Model Rules 7.1–7.4 apply to every communication about your services, including newsletters, seminar invitations, and email signatures that advertise. The practical version: no outcome guarantees, no paid referrals, no targeting people you know need help in a specific matter, no "specialist" claims without certification, and put the responsible lawyer's name and office address in the footer.

Is Kit any good for law firms?

Technically usable, practically wrong. Kit is priced and built for creators selling digital products — steep subscriber-based price jumps and no legal-oriented anything. MailerLite or Brevo do the same job cheaper.

What about Constant Contact or Mailchimp?

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and Constant Contact's main legal-marketing selling point is template simplicity rather than price.
