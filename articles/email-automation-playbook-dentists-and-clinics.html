Your practice management software already sends appointment reminders. Dentrix, Open Dental, Eaglesoft, Curve — they all fire SMS and email confirmations off the schedule, with two-way confirmation. Buying an email marketing tool to do “appointment reminders” is buying a second hose when the first one already waters the garden, so this playbook assumes you will not rebuild that in MailerLite or Kit. The marketing tool has its own lane, and it’s the lane that decides whether chairs stay full: 7 automations — the four every practice should build first (the new-patient welcome, the 24-hour review request, the no-show rescue, and the unscheduled-treatment follow-up) plus three that separate the growing practice from the flat one: the reactivation drip, hygiene-recall fills, and high-value service campaigns. This playbook is the trigger-by-trigger build order, the tier-by-tier tool math at dental list sizes, and the one rule that runs underneath everything on this page: what you may never put in a marketing email. The 4 baseline flows (build these first)

These are the flows that decide whether a new patient becomes a regular and whether a happy visit becomes a public review. Build them before anything clever. One rule governs all four, and it’s the dental rule: your practice management software owns the appointment layer — confirmations, reminders, cancellations, receipts. Your marketing tool owns the growth layer — welcome, reviews, reactivation, fills, campaigns. Cross the lanes and you either duplicate emails (patients train to ignore both) or leak clinical information into a system with no HIPAA Business Associate Agreement on its self-serve plans (as of October 1, 2026 — verify with the vendor). Keep the marketing tool strictly on reviews, reactivation, recalls, and campaigns, and read every template once: does this email reveal a health fact about the recipient? If yes, it doesn’t send. (The full line, with the “Tuesday at 2 PM” safe test, is in the dental vertical guide.)

1. The new-patient welcome (3 emails, 14 days)

Trigger: first appointment booked or completed — tag “new-patient” from the PMS or the booking form. Sequence: email one (same day as first visit): the “welcome to the practice” email — what to expect next time, where to park, the front-desk number, and one line that answers the unasked question: how recall reminders work here (you’re setting up the permission for flow #6). Email two (day 7): the comfort email — sedation options if you offer them, payment and insurance facts, the financing line — framed for the patient who is deciding whether this is their dentist now. Email three (day 14): the soft review ask — a satisfied new patient is your most review-ready segment, and if they’re happy they’ll click now. The honest mechanics: this is not the PMS’s confirmation email — that system sends the time and the door code. Yours sends the relationship, and nothing in it may reveal what was treated. “Great to meet you Tuesday” is safe; “hope the crown feels good” is not. That filter applies to every template in this playbook, once, before you build.

2. The 24-hour review request (1 email per visit, +24–48h)

Trigger: completed appointment, 24–48 hours later. Sequence: one email, every time: thank the patient, one-click Google review link, nothing else. The filter is everything — send it only to patients flagged as satisfied (your PMS status or a one-question post-visit text does the triage), because the unhappy patient gets the private “how can we make this right?” path, not the public one. The numbers: a dental practice lives or dies on its Google review count and recency (vertical guide, October 2026), and this automation is why the tool pays for itself — review volume is marketing you don’t pay for monthly, and it compounds in local search rankings. This is the flow that gets the single automation slot if you’re on a one-workflow tier like GetResponse Starter or Flodesk Lite — it out-earns every other flow in the playbook, so it goes first, full stop. The honest catch: review gating that suppresses negative reviews can violate platform policies and, in some jurisdictions, consumer-protection rules — the triage is for service recovery, not for hiding outcomes. The satisfied patients get the link; the dissatisfied get a human being. That’s the line.

3. The no-show / missed-appointment rescue (2 emails: same day, +3 days)

Trigger: appointment marked missed or no-show in the PMS. Sequence: email one the same day — no guilt, one line (“missed you today — want us to hold your next slot?”) with the booking link; email two at +3 days with the friction-handler (the rebooking is one click; the hygiene gap is why it matters) and a low-friction rebook (the same hygienist, a different time). The honest mechanics: this flow needs the PMS’s missed-appointment status to fire — export the day’s missed list or wire the integration; for a single-location practice the daily-export routine takes 10 minutes and is the honest starting point. The empty chair is the most expensive unit in the practice, and a recovered appointment costs nothing while a lost one costs the whole revenue of the slot. This email must never say what the appointment was — “your appointment” is the whole description, because the moment it names the treatment it has crossed the HIPAA line. Let the PMS handle anything clinical; the marketing tool handles the rebook.

4. The unscheduled-treatment follow-up (the money flow: +2 days, +14 days, +60 days)

Trigger: treatment presented but not scheduled — the crown quote, the whitening plan, the Invisalign consult the patient “needs to think about.” Sequence: three emails: day 2 — the plain-language recap (“here’s what we discussed and the next step when you’re ready,” no clinical detail in the email itself — the patient knows what it was; the email must not name it); day 14 — the question-handler (the three questions patients ask before they say yes: cost, time, will it hurt — answered as practice information, not medical advice); day 60 — the re-open (“this is still open when you’re ready” with the booking link). The numbers: this is the highest-ROI automation in the playbook — one converted Invisalign case pays for years of the email tool (vertical guide, October 2026), and unscheduled treatment plans sitting in the PMS are revenue you already earned the right to collect. The honest mechanics: the email can never describe the treatment — write every template to the generic “your treatment plan” and let the patient’s memory do the rest. Segment the invitation by age band for high-value campaigns (adults 18–45 is our estimate of the segment that converts best for ortho-style services), but the treatment follow-up is one-to-one: the patient who heard the quote gets the email, and nobody else does.

The 3 advanced automations

5. The reactivation drip (quarterly, dormancy-triggered)

Trigger: no visit for 6–12 months — the dormant list, refreshed from the PMS export. Sequence: a quarterly 3-email campaign, not a continuous drip: email one is the “we miss you” check-in (genuinely warm, no offer — the patient who’s embarrassed about the gap responds to a human, not a coupon); email two is the friction-remover (new-hygienist introduction, the “come in for one hygiene visit, no commitment” line); email three is the honest offer (the exam-and-x-rays welcome-back special). The numbers: a quarterly campaign to 500 dormant patients that brings back even 20 is a five-figure revenue event from one email, and a rebooked hygiene patient is worth roughly $200–$400 in annual hygiene revenue per patient (our estimates — vertical guide, October 2026). This is the business case for the tool, not newsletters. The honest mechanics: the dormant list decays — clean it before you mail it (the billing-traps section of the vertical guide), and never let email one name why you think they left. “It’s been a while” is the whole frame.

6. Hygiene-recall fills (perishable openings, 2–3 weeks out)

Trigger: your schedule — the recurring “we have openings next Tuesday and Thursday” moment. Sequence: one broadcast per gap-window to overdue-for-recall patients only (the PMS export, filtered on last-hygiene date): the subject names the window (“openings next week”), the body names two specific days, the booking link names one click. The numbers: hygiene openings 2–3 weeks out are perishable inventory (vertical guide, October 2026) — a broadcast that fills even one chair beats the empty one, and this is the flow that keeps the hygienists booked instead of the practice eating overhead on empty operatories. Two honest catches. First, this is a broadcast, not an automation — it runs when you have a gap, so it doesn’t consume an automation-workflow slot on a one-workflow tier (that’s exactly why GetResponse Starter + manual broadcasts is the honest answer for most practices). Second, the list is “overdue for recall,” never “patients with periodontal disease” — schedule-based segments are marketing segments; condition-based segments are a HIPAA crossing. The email names days, never diagnoses.

7. High-value service campaigns (quarterly launches: whitening days, Invisalign consult days, implant seminars)

Trigger: your calendar — the whitening day, the Invisalign/ortho consult day, the implant seminar. Sequence: two weeks before: the announcement to the adult segment with the what/why; one week before: the social-proof email (last consult day’s results, names optional — or the before/after wall with consent); launch day: the enrollment email with the consult-day price vs the regular price; the day after: the “we still have two consult slots” last call to the opened-but-didn’t-book segment. The numbers: one converted Invisalign case pays for years of the email tool (vertical guide, October 2026) — these campaigns are the margin layer, and the email list is the cheapest enrollment channel the practice owns. The honest mechanics: segment by age band (adults 18–45 is our estimate of the segment that converts best for these services) and invite them — don’t blast everyone. The seminar variant needs Constant Contact’s event tools or a landing page with registration (MailerLite and GetResponse both include landing pages at the entry tiers); the whitening day needs a booking link and a reason to act today (time-boxed consult-day pricing, not perpetual discounts, which train patients to wait).

The send math: the single-location practice worked example

Worked example — a practice with 2,000 patients on MailerLite Comfort ($19/mo at the 1,000-contact band... verify your band at checkout, send cap roughly 10× the list = ~20,000 emails/mo at the 2,000 band): monthly newsletter to 2,000 patients = 2,000 emails; quarterly reactivation blast to ~500 dormant = 500; the review-request automation firing on every visit (~12 visits a day × ~22 working days = ~264 emails/mo); the welcome and no-show drips = a few hundred more. Total: roughly 3,000–3,500 emails a month against a ~20,000 cap — under a fifth of the allowance, with room to triple the list before the cap even enters the conversation. The $19 tier is not the cheap-looking tier; it’s the tier the practice never outgrows until it goes multi-location. Your volume will differ; add your campaigns plus drip sends before you pick the tier.

The PMS question: what it sends vs what only you can send

Before you build a single flow, look at what your practice management system already sends: appointment confirmations, reminders, cancellation confirmations, and payment receipts — usually by SMS and email with two-way confirmation. The honest division of labor: the PMS owns the appointment layer because it holds the schedule and because it’s covered by your existing BAA vendor agreements; your marketing tool owns the growth layer because the PMS will never tell a patient story, run a reactivation drip on 9 months of silence, or launch your Invisalign consult day. The seam is the export: flows #2, #3, #5, and #6 need to know who visited, who missed, and who hasn’t — tag from PMS exports (CSV) or wire the integration once; for a single-location practice the daily-export routine takes 10 minutes. Practices that duplicate the PMS’s confirmations in their marketing tool send double emails and train patients to ignore both — and worse, they create a second copy of clinical communication outside the BAA-covered system. Wire the seam once, and the two systems stop fighting.

The 4 mistakes that kill dental email

  • Building on the wrong tier. Kit’s free plan covers 10,000 subscribers and none of your automations. GetResponse Starter gives you one workflow for a seven-flow playbook. Flodesk Lite gives you one workflow; Pro is unlimited but costs more than Comfort for a simpler builder. Brevo free gives you zero automations and a 300-a-day cap. Every flow built on a tier that can’t run it is a rebuild. Pick the tier from the table above, then build.
  • Putting clinical detail in marketing emails. “Great to meet you Tuesday” is safe; “hope the root canal settles” has crossed the line, and none of the self-serve tools on this page offers a BAA on standard plans (as of October 1, 2026 — verify with the vendor). The one exception people ask about: genuine PHI emailing needs a dedicated HIPAA-compliant service like Paubox — a different product class, and we don’t carry an affiliate link for it. The uncompensated safe answer stays in the article.
  • Duplicating PMS reminders. The moment a patient gets the PMS confirmation and your MailerLite confirmation for the same appointment, both systems start looking like spam. The marketing tool never sends anything the schedule already sends — and every duplicated clinical message is also a copy of patient communication outside your BAA-covered system.
  • Asking the wrong patients for reviews. Blasting the review link to everyone who walked in — including the patient still fuming in the parking lot — turns a growth engine into a reputation liability. The satisfied-patient filter isn’t optional decoration; it’s the flow. Unhappy patients get the private service-recovery path and a human being, never the public link.
