Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. The estimate follow-up machine. A $2,800 crane-job quote dies on the counter because the number is scary, not because the customer chose someone else. The sequence: the same-day estimate email with the itemized scope (labor, crane time, debris hauling, stump grinding as a separate line — the 2026 trade itemization that turns a number into a plan), the 72-hour check-in ("any questions on the scope — happy to walk the property again"), the 7-day value email (the dead-limb-over-the-roof photo from their own property with one line of plain English), and the 21-day expiry notice ("this quote holds through the month"). Big tickets need 3–5 touches; the shops that send one estimate and wait lose to the shops that follow up.
  2. The storm-season alert engine. May through July is the peak severe-storm window across the central and southern US (NWS, via the June 2026 industry release), and the release's ISA-cited data says emergency tree-service demand is up 35% over five years in storm corridors — while insurers are tightening scrutiny of claims tied to known hazards. That sentence is the campaign: the pre-season hazard-assessment offer ("the limb hanging over your garage is a known hazard — here's what it costs to handle it before the wind does"), timed to April, with the honest line that a preventive removal costs a fraction of a post-collapse claim fight. Post-storm, the same list gets the capacity email ("crews are running — here's the queue order").
  3. The pruning-cycle calendar. The recurring-revenue spine of the trade: late-winter pruning is the industry-recommended window (October 2026 climate guides), and every tree on a property is a candidate for a scheduled cycle — not a one-time event. The engine: the annual inspection email (every customer, every February), the cycle proposal (the 2- or 3-year rotation by species and location), and the reminder drip for the customers who pruned last year ("your oaks are due — same crew, same price band"). The email version turns the one-time $600 trimming customer (typical estimate) into a 5-year relationship.
  4. The stump-grind attach sequence. The trade's purest upsell: the stump you leave behind is priced separately at $150–$500 for grinding (Angi 2026) or $2–$5 per inch of diameter, and the 2026 PUGET Sound chart puts a 12–24-inch stump at $200–$500 and 24–36 at $500–$900. The sequence fires the day after the removal: the photo of their stump with the two-line quote, the 7-day reminder ("the grinding takes 20 minutes — the crew is in your neighborhood Thursday"), and the mulch honesty (the chips are theirs to keep or we haul). A removal ticket plus the attach is how a $900 job becomes $1,200 with no new lead.
  5. The HOA/property-manager drip. The commercial buyer is worth thirty homeowners: the HOA board with 400 trees doesn't care about your truck gallery — she cares about the liability walk, the documented hazard assessment, and the 72-hour-post-storm response capability (real municipalities are now enforcing exactly that window). The engine: the monthly B2B touch — current crew capacity, one useful line about the season, segmented away from consumers entirely. The drip is the relationship; the standing contract is the payoff.

The honest catches

  • The crew's response time wins the storm call, not the drip. Email keeps the customer for years, but the 24-hour emergency response wins the post-storm call — the company that answers and arrives beats the company with the prettier alert email. Automate the follow-up, not the dispatch.
  • Your list import is the real setup cost. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting the customer list and tagging it properly — service (removal/trimming/stump/emergency), property type (residential/HOA/commercial), stage (estimate sent/follow-up scheduled/customer) (estimate — most dispatch exports won't have all of these out of the box). Budget an afternoon, not ten minutes. A list without service tags can't run the estimate engine or the pruning calendar — the tags are the product. Our deliverability audit checklist covers the list-hygiene half.
  • Email can't fix a 1.9-star reputation. The follow-up drip multiplies what the crews deliver — a company with 1.9 stars will automate its way to more detailed complaints, and the tree that got topped gets photographed. Fix the service first, then automate the ask.
  • Never claim a credential you don't hold. ISA Certified Arborist, TCIA accreditation, licensed and insured — these are checkable in minutes, and the June 2026 insurer-scrutiny story means the market is looking harder than ever. If you carry the credential, put it in the footer of every email; if you don't, say nothing — a borrowed logo is a reputation death sentence when it surfaces.

How often should a tree service company email past customers?

Three seasonal blasts a year — the February pruning-cycle kickoff, the April pre-storm hazard assessment, the post-storm capacity email — plus the 72-hour estimate follow-up after every quote and the pruning reminders on the annual calendar. Past customers don't need a newsletter — they need the estimate that doesn't expire into silence, the storm warning before the wind, and the email that arrives the year their trees are due. The HOA segment is different: monthly, short, useful.

Should emergency and pruning customers be on the same list?

Same tool, different segments — not different accounts. The estimate follow-up branches by service (the emergency removal gets the "call now" version; the pruning inquiry gets the calendar version), and the campaigns run on separate calendars. Tagging handles it; one account keeps the list, the automations, and the pruning program in one place.

Can email actually sell annual pruning programs?

Yes — and it's the highest-ROI campaign on this page. The conversion email is pure math: the customer's last trimming ticket (a typical $300–$800 estimate for the job) versus the scheduled cycle that costs less per year than emergency work, plus the inspection that finds the dead limb over the garage before the storm does. The February kickoff email to the whole past-customer list is the single highest-leverage send a tree company runs all year.
