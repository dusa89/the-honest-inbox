What a vet practice actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. The recall drip. The highest-ROI email a practice sends is the one that goes out before the client forgets: the "your pet is due for annual vaccines" email at 11 months, the overdue nudge at 13 months, the reactivation at 18 months. Every overdue patient already trusts you — the drip just books them before a competitor's postcard does. Tag the list by last-visit date and this sequence runs itself.
  2. The wellness-plan retention engine. If the practice sells wellness plans (monthly preventive-care memberships — the industry's recurring-revenue answer to one-and-done visits), email is what keeps members from lapsing: the monthly value-reminder ("your plan covered this exam"), the renewal at month 11, the missed-benefit nudge for members who haven't booked. The plan is only recurring revenue if the members stay on the plan — the drip does the staying.
  3. The no-show rescue. A missed appointment is the most expensive email problem in the building. The sequence: the missed-visit email same day with the reschedule link, the check-in a week later, the reactivation to chronic no-shows. This is the automation that pays for the tool.
  4. Species-and-age segmentation. Puppy owners and senior-cat owners do not read the same email. Tag the list by species and life stage (puppy/kitten, adult, senior — senior is roughly 7+ for dogs, 11+ for cats as a general veterinary consensus, not a quoted statistic) and the campaigns write themselves: the puppy-vaccine schedule for new dog owners, the senior-wellness pitch for the aging list, the dental campaign for the breeds that always need it. Generic "pet newsletter" is what every vendor sends; segmented is what books visits.
  5. Review moat after every visit. Nobody picks a vet from a coupon mailer — they pick from the three practices with 400+ Google reviews, and they pick the one their neighbor recommended. The automated review ask ~48 hours after the visit, with the direct Google review link and the doctor's name in the subject line if the client met them, is the highest-ROI email the practice sends. Two emails max, then stop. In a neighborhood where the map pack is the front page, this sequence is a moat-builder.

Notice what's missing: no lead scoring, no landing-page builder Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. The practice management software (Cornerstone, AVImark, ezyVet — all established names in the vet PMS space) already holds the patient records and sends appointment confirmations; the vet suites (Demandforce, PetDesk-style client apps) own the reminder layer. The email tool only has to hold the marketing list, send the campaigns, and run the recall-and-review automations. Paying for a marketing department's toolkit is the trap.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a practice leaves on the table:

  1. The vaccine/annual-exam recall. Trigger: last visit ~11 months ago. Email 1: the due-date reminder with the online booking link, the pet's name in the subject line. Email 2 (one month later, still unbooked): the overdue nudge — missed preventive care is the pitch, not guilt. Email 3 (six months later): the reactivation — the "we miss [pet's name]" email with the review-free service note. The honest economics: preventive visits are the practice's cheapest revenue to book and the most profitable per minute; the drip turns last year's patients into next year's appointments.
  2. The no-show rescue. Trigger: appointment marked missed. Email 1 (same day): the sympathetic reschedule — life happens, here's the booking link. Email 2 (one week later, still unbooked): the check-in with the doctor's note if the visit was for something time-sensitive. The empty slot is the most expensive thing in the building; this sequence fills the next one.
  3. The puppy/kitten welcome sequence. Trigger: new-pet first visit. Email 1 (the day after): the vaccine-schedule cheat sheet for the pet's first year — the single most-asked question at every first visit, answered before the client Googles it. Email 2 (two weeks later): the socialization-and-training resources. Email 3 (~6 weeks later): the spay/neuter timing conversation. This sequence builds the relationship that books the next 15 years of visits — and every puppy owner becomes a wellness-plan prospect.
  4. The senior-pet care sequence. Trigger: tagged senior (roughly 7+ dogs, 11+ cats — general veterinary consensus, not a quoted statistic). Email 1: the senior-wellness panel pitch — bloodwork, dental, weight checks, priced against the cost of the emergency that finds the problem late. Email 2 (a month later, unbooked): the quality-of-life conversation starter. Senior pets are the practice's highest-value patients and its most-overdue segment — this sequence speaks to the owners who need permission to book, not a reminder.
  5. The review sequence. Trigger: visit marked complete. Email 1 (~48 hours): the thank-you + direct Google review link, the doctor's name in the subject line if the client met them. If no review after 14 days, email 2: the gentle nudge with the link. Keep it to two emails — more reads as begging. Pair with the front desk asking in person at checkout; the email is the follow-through, and in a neighborhood where the map pack is the front page, the review count is the moat.

The honest catches

Your PMS already sends reminders — don't double-send. Cornerstone, AVImark, ezyVet, and the vet suites (Demandforce, PetDesk-style client apps) already handle appointment confirmations and recall reminders at the transactional layer. Let them own the transactional job communication; let the email tool handle marketing campaigns, the marketing-side recall drip, newsletters, reactivation, and review sequences. Check what your practice software already does before you pay for an email tool to do it twice. If the PMS's reminder engine is already strong, the email tool's job gets smaller — buy accordingly.

Reminder texts are consent-tracked — the email list is the marketing subset. Appointment reminders are transactional; newsletters, campaigns, and promotional recall emails are marketing and need opt-in. The exported client file is not the email list — the email list is the subset that opted into marketing. Expect the email list to be smaller than the patient file (estimate — opt-in rates vary widely by practice); the campaigns here are built for that smaller, warmer list, which converts better anyway.

Species segmentation is the tags; the tags are the work. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting the patient file and tagging it properly — species, life stage, last-visit date, wellness-plan member, dental due (estimate — most PMS exports won't have these tags out of the box). Budget an afternoon, not ten minutes. A list without species and last-visit tags can't run the recall drip or the senior-pet sequence — the tags are the product. Our deliverability audit checklist covers the list-hygiene half.

Text beats email for day-of logistics — use both. The "appointment is in 30 minutes" text prevents the no-show; the lab-results follow-up rides on transactional email. Email wins the campaigns nobody texts about: the recall drip, the wellness-plan retention, the puppy sequence, the senior-pet pitch, the review moat. If you only do one, do the email — it costs almost nothing and the list compounds.

PetDesk-style apps and Demandforce do the reminder job on purpose. They're built for it — PMS integration, automated recall, two-way texting — and they're not in our affiliate bench, so naming them costs us nothing and tells you something. Use them for reminders; use the tools on this page for campaigns, newsletters, and reviews. The practices that win run both layers, not one tool doing both jobs badly.

Can I import my client list from Cornerstone or AVImark?

Yes — every tool here takes a CSV export, and the practice software exports one. The honest workflow for most practices is a scheduled CSV export and import, with species, life-stage, and last-visit tags added as custom fields. The reminder layer (PMS + vet suites) stays where it is; the email tool only needs the marketing subset — the clients who opted into marketing emails.

Do I need an email tool if my practice software already sends reminders?

Yes — for different jobs. The PMS (or Demandforce/PetDesk-style apps) owns transactional reminders: confirmations, day-of texts, recall nudges. The email tool owns marketing: the newsletter, the seasonal campaigns, the wellness-plan retention drip, the puppy and senior-pet sequences, the review ask. Different tools, different jobs — run both layers.

When should the recall drip go out?

Before the due date, not after — at ~11 months for annual vaccines and wellness exams, with the overdue nudge at ~13 months and the reactivation at ~18 months. Pre-build the segment with last-visit dates; writing the reminder after the client is six months overdue means the appointment already went to whoever reminded them first. The drip repeats forever — template it once.

Are Mailchimp or Constant Contact fine for vet practices?

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and both are built for generic small business rather than this niche. Mailchimp's free tier was cut in January 2026 to 250 contacts and 500 emails/month, and Essentials starts at $13/mo for 500 contacts. See Mailchimp alternatives for the migration math.

What about the practice's groomers or boarders?

Same engine, different campaigns — if the practice runs boarding or grooming, the same list powers the holiday boarding book-ahead (Thanksgiving and Christmas fill weeks out), the seasonal grooming reminders, and the daycare punch-card reactivation. Tag those clients by service and the whole playbook above transfers. The pet-care marketing tools (Gingr and its class) exist for pure pet-care businesses; for a vet practice with add-on services, the email tools on this page are cheaper and better at campaigns.
