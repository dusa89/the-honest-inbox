The shop that emails its customer list in November, before the first freeze, books the winter's burst-pipe jobs before the emergency calls even come in. What a plumbing company actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. The freeze-prep campaign. Where winters freeze, the highest-ROI email a plumber sends goes out in late fall: the burst-pipe prevention pitch — pipe insulation, hose-bib shutoffs, the vacation checklist — to the whole customer list. The jobs are nearly all booked before the emergency season, the estimates are scheduled in daylight, and the shop is already top of mind when a pipe lets go at 2 a.m. Same muscle in the south: the summer water-heater strain campaign, when hard-water systems die in the heat. Pre-write the template; it goes out every year.
  2. The maintenance-contract ladder. The most profitable customer in plumbing is the one on the annual plan — the whole-house inspection, the water-heater flush, the drain maintenance, the priority-scheduling promise — because the plan is recurring revenue in a one-and-done trade. Email builds the ladder: the install-day signup, the 30-day check-in, the renewal at 11 months before the customer forgets what the contract was. Every water-heater install, every repipe, every sewer-line replacement is an entry point to the list; the automation turns the one-time job into the recurring customer.
  3. Emergency reactivation. Past customers are the warmest audience you'll ever own — they've already trusted you inside their house. The reactivation sequence runs on the dormant segment: everyone who hasn't booked in 18+ months gets the seasonal pitch (freeze prep in fall, the water-heater tune-up, the drain-cleaning reminder) before the shop ever considers buying a single lead. A dead list of 1,000 past customers is a competitor's gift; a reactivated one is nearly free revenue.
  4. Seasonal job campaigns. Water heaters fail in waves (age + hard water), drains back up in the rainy season, sump pumps get replaced every spring before the storms. Each season has its hero campaign: the water-heater age sequence (tag every install with the install year, automate the "your heater is 9 years old" pitch), the spring sump-pump check, the sewer-line inspection offer in neighborhoods where the clay pipes are all the same age. Tag the list by equipment and service history and the campaigns write themselves.
  5. Review moat with crew photos. Nobody picks a plumber from a Facebook ad — they pick from the three shops with 200+ Google reviews, and they pick the one their neighbor recommended. The automated review ask ~48 hours after the job, with the direct Google review link and the crew name in the subject line if the customer knew them, is the highest-ROI email a plumber sends. Two emails max, then stop. In a trade where the map pack is the front page, this sequence is a moat-builder.

Notice what's missing: no lead scoring, no landing-page builder Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. Your field software (ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, Service Fusion — all established names in the trades CRM space) already handles dispatch, estimates, invoicing, and visit reminders; the email tool only has to hold the customer list, send the campaigns, and run a handful of seasonal automations. Paying for a marketing department's toolkit is the trap.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a plumbing company leaves on the table:

  1. The freeze-prep campaign. Trigger: late fall, before the first freeze (adjust to your climate; in the south, swap to the summer water-heater-strain campaign). Email 1: the burst-pipe prevention pitch to the whole customer list — pipe insulation, hose-bib shutoffs, the vacation checklist, the "we're booking now" pressure. Email 2 (one week later, to non-openers): shorter, same offer. The honest economics: prevention jobs are booked in daylight at your schedule, not in emergency hours at the customer's panic price — and every booked job makes your shop the one they call when a pipe lets go in January.
  2. The maintenance-contract ladder. Trigger: every completed job. Email 1 (a few days after the job): the annual-plan pitch — the whole-house inspection, the water-heater flush, the priority-scheduling promise, priced against the cost of one emergency visit. Email 2 (~11 months later, to plan members): the renewal before the contract expires, with the year's visit history as the receipt. The install day is the highest-converting entry point — tag every install customer into this sequence.
  3. The reactivation. Trigger: no booking in 18+ months. Email 1: the seasonal pitch for the customer's situation — freeze prep, the water-heater tune-up, the drain-cleaning reminder. Email 2 (two weeks later): the "still on our list?" honesty line with the review-free ask — past customers reactivate when you give them a reason to remember you exist, and the reason is always seasonal.
  4. The equipment-age sequence. Tag every install with the install year and equipment type. Water heaters: the "your heater is 9 years old" email at year 9, the replacement pitch at year 11 (average lifespan ~10-12 years is the industry consensus — label the number, then verify against the unit's plate). Sump pumps: the spring check before storm season. Sewer lines: the inspection offer in neighborhoods where the clay pipes are all aging together. This sequence sells replacement jobs before the failure, which is where the margin is.
  5. The review sequence. Trigger: job marked complete. Email 1 (~48 hours): the thank-you + direct Google review link, crew name in the subject line if the customer knew them by name. If no review after 14 days, email 2: the gentle nudge with the link. Keep it to two emails — more reads as begging. Pair with the tech asking in person on the final walkthrough; the email is the follow-through, and in a trade where the map pack is the front page, the review count is the moat.

The honest catches

Your field software already sends some of this — don't double-send. ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, and Service Fusion all send appointment reminders, estimate links, and invoices; several run customer communications of their own. Let the field software handle transactional job communication; let the email tool handle marketing campaigns, seasonal sequences, reactivation, and review drips. Check what your field software already does before you pay for an email tool to do it twice.

The dormant list is dormant, not dead — treat it with cadence discipline. A plumbing list that only hears from you twice a year is normal, and the reactivation only works if the list isn't burned. That doesn't mean blasting the base in June with a coupon they don't need — the seasonal cadence is the discipline: freeze prep in fall, the equipment check in spring, and nothing else unless you have real news. A list that's been ignored for three years and then blasted daily in November gets spam complaints, not bookings.

Emergency SEO is not email's job — pair them. Nobody in a burst-pipe emergency is checking your newsletter; they're Googling "emergency plumber near me." Email wins the planned and preventable revenue — maintenance contracts, freeze prep, equipment replacement — while search wins the emergencies. The two together are the pattern: search catches the crisis, email prevents the crisis and books the contract.

Text beats email for day-of logistics — use both. The "tech's on the way" text prevents the locked-door miss; the estimate follow-up rides on transactional email. Email wins the campaigns nobody texts about: freeze prep, the contract ladder, reactivation, seasonal equipment pitches. If you only do one, do the email — it costs almost nothing and the list compounds.

Your list import is the real setup cost. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting the customer file and tagging it properly — service history, equipment and install year, last-service date, one-time vs maintenance-contract member (estimate — most field-software exports won't have these tags out of the box). Budget an afternoon, not ten minutes. A list without service tags can't run the freeze campaign or the equipment-age sequence — the tags are the product. When should the freeze-prep email go out?

Before your customers start thinking about winter, not after — late fall in freeze country, adjusted to your climate. Pre-build the segment in October; writing the email on the first freeze warning means it goes out a week late, which is when your competitor's went out. The campaign repeats every year — template it once.

How do I sell maintenance contracts without sounding like a warranty telemarketer?

Lead with the math, not the membership: one annual visit costs less than one emergency call, and the pitch lives in the install-day email when the customer's relief is freshest. Price the contract against a single after-hours visit — the customer who sees the comparison buys; the customer who hears "priority scheduling" tunes out. The 11-month renewal email does the heavy lifting from there.

Are Mailchimp or Constant Contact fine for plumbing?

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and both are built for generic small business rather than this niche. Mailchimp's free tier was cut in January 2026 to 250 contacts and 500 emails/month, and Essentials starts at $13/mo for 500 contacts. See Mailchimp alternatives for the migration math.

Do I need email if my field software already texts customers?

Yes — for different jobs. Text wins the urgent, time-sensitive jobs (day-of logistics, gate codes, the review ask while the tech is still at the door). Email wins the campaigns nobody texts about: freeze prep, the contract ladder, reactivation, seasonal equipment pitches. The two together are the pattern that moves both bookings and the review moat.
