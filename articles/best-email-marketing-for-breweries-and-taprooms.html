What a brewery actually needs from email

Forget "open rates." The six engines that fill the taproom:

  1. The mug-club engine. Paid loyalty is the brewery's retention product: members pay in, get the mug, the member pricing, the early access. Industry operators on the ProBrewer board put it plainly — mug club members become the brewery's ambassadors. The engine is the enrollment drive (a window, a cap, a reason to join now), the member-only early-access emails, the renewal conversation, and the member event calendar. This is the closest thing brewing has to a subscription — and it is the highest-ROI email a brewery ever sends, because every member is a pre-paid regular.
  2. The can-release drop list. Limited releases are the brewery's product-launch business: a batch, a count, a sell-through window. The list is the launch channel — the Friday announcement, the Saturday-morning reminder, the sold-out notice that trains the next drop's urgency. The drop sells through the list before the taproom doors open; walk-ins get what's left. Scored on the tools: can you segment the drop list by style preference (the sour fans vs the stout fans), and can the announcement go out in minutes when the canning line confirms?
  3. The event-calendar engine. Trivia night, live music, beer school, the anniversary party — the recurring reasons a local becomes a regular. The engine is the weekly calendar send, the event reminder sequence (the day-before nudge, the day-of text), the post-event thank-you that feeds the next one. Events are the brewery's acquisition channel that doesn't buy ads; the calendar email is what keeps them full.
  4. The seasonal rotation engine. The beer year has a cadence — Oktoberfest, the holiday stouts, the spring lagers, the summer crushers — and each season is a launch. The engine is the seasonal announcement sequence, the "back on tap" revival emails, the weather-triggered sends (the first hot Saturday deserves a lager announcement). Seasonal releases are limited by nature; the list is what converts scarcity into attendance.
  5. The DTC notification list. Where the law allows it, the release email becomes the checkout. The 2026 Sovos ShipCompliant/Brewers Association report puts the demand in hard numbers: 72% of regular craft drinkers would increase purchasing if they could have beer shipped to their home, with spend intent at $104 a month. The catch is the map — DtC beer shipping is legal in only 11 states plus D.C., against wine's 48. The list is the DTC channel: the drop announcement, the shipping window, the club shipment notices. Where it isn't legal, the same list powers merch, gift cards, and taproom pickup orders instead.
  6. The first-visit-to-regular pipeline. The taproom's most underpriced asset is the Saturday walk-in who never comes back — and the industry's own data names the gap: only 13.9% of brewery staff encourage guests to join the mailing list or rewards program. The pipeline is the capture script at the register ("first taste of the new sour is on the list"), the post-visit welcome sequence, the second-visit invitation with a reason (the trivia night, the drop), and the lapsed-regular winback. The email tool only has to carry six rhythms — and a taproom where the only ask is the check has its front door swinging both ways.

Notice what's missing: no cart recovery, no coupon blasts, no generic newsletters. The mug club does the retention work, the drop list does the scarcity work, the calendar does the frequency work, the seasonal engine does the novelty work, the DTC list does the shipping work, and the pipeline does the compounding. A brewery whose only email is the occasional "we have a new IPA" is running one engine out of six.

The honest catches

Only 13.9% of brewery staff ask. That is the whole game. The industry's own data says the list-building gap is not the channel — it is the register. The fix is not a better tool, it is a script: "first taste of the new sour is on the list" at every pour, the QR on every table tent, the signup in the reservation flow and the merch checkout. The brewery with the capture script beats the brewery with the bigger budget — every time, because the list compounds and the ad spend doesn't.

The list is the owned channel — social is rented. Industry operators have been saying it for years: organic reach on the big platforms keeps shrinking, and breweries are moving toward a weekly email-list approach because the list is the one audience nobody can throttle. Every event promoted only on social is an event that happened or didn't based on an algorithm's mood. The email list is the taproom's own distribution — build it like the asset it is.

Alcohol has rules — keep the compliance boring. Beer marketing rides state liquor laws, the three-tier system, and platform policies that all have opinions about alcohol advertising. The email rules are the familiar ones (consent, opt-out, honest subject lines — CAN-SPAM basics), but age-gating the list signup and keeping the DTC shipping inside the states where it's legal (11 states plus D.C. as of the 2026 Sovos/Brewers Association report) are brewery-specific obligations. Keep opt-in records from day one, check your state's current rules before launching DTC, and never buy cold prospect lists. Boring compliance is what lets the interesting marketing run.

SMS is the day-of channel; email is the calendar. The "cans drop Saturday, doors at noon" text needs the customer's eyes in minutes — that is SMS's job, not email's. Email carries the drop sequence, the calendar, and the seasonal launches. And the marketing texts need the same consent records as the marketing emails, with the alcohol context on top. Keep the channels honest and the consent records cleaner than either, and each one works.
