A commercial cleaning company doesn't sell cleaning — it sells contracts. The U.S. janitorial services market runs about $112 billion a year (IBISWorld, 2026), the commercial janitorial slice about $81 billion (The Business Research Company), and almost none of that money changes hands one transaction at a time. It moves in 12-month service agreements won through bids, renewed on procurement calendars, and lost to the vendor who followed up better. The bid mechanics are documented, not mysterious: facility managers typically give vendors 2–4 weeks to return a proposal (CleanLink/AFFLINK's RFP guidance), run pre-bid walkthroughs for complicated facilities, and openly warn against low bidders who didn't understand the requirements. A full formal RFP cycle for services can stretch 8–14 weeks end to end (a general-services figure, not cleaning-specific — label it as such when you quote it). That long, multi-stakeholder, paper-heavy sales cycle is exactly why email is the commercial cleaner's highest-leverage tool: nobody wins a $60,000-a-year contract in a single email, but plenty of contracts are won by the vendor who was still there, still useful, still following up in week six. Every tool below is scored against that machinery: the bid, the renewal, the newsletter, the ladder, the win-back.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The bid pipeline engine. Bids live in the follow-up gap: quote sent, then silence. The documented window is 2–4 weeks for the vendor's proposal, but the buyer's side runs longer — walkthroughs, evaluations, approvals. The machinery: a quote follow-up ladder (check-in, value proof, walkthrough invite), bid-stage tagging, and knowing exactly which deals went quiet and when. Scored on: timed automation sequences, pipeline/tag visibility, and proposal-attachment support.
  2. The contract-renewal runway. Renewals are won before the re-bid, not during it. Start 120 days out: the performance recap (their own numbers — response times, scope covered, issues closed), the updated scope for the next term, the early-renewal incentive that makes re-bidding feel like work. Scored on: date-based automations, annual-cycle sequences, and the ability to attach real reports to real emails.
  3. The facility-manager newsletter. The trust instrument in a market that punishes low bidders. Monthly, useful, barely salesy: flu-season disinfection planning, post-construction cleanup windows, restroom-consumable cost notes, green-certification updates. The reader is a person whose job includes not getting embarrassed by a vendor. Scored on: template quality, segmentation (healthcare vs office vs school vs industrial), and plain deliverability into corporate inboxes.
  4. The multi-location ladder. Portfolio managers award site by site and region by region — the same company, five buildings, five decisions. The machinery: modular quotes per site, rollout-sequence messaging (pilot site first, expand on the wins), and one email program per region or portfolio. Scored on: multi-list and sub-account management, team seats, and per-audience reporting.
  5. The lost-bid win-back. The low bidder who wins your deal often loses it later — the cheap vendor under-scopes, understaffs, and the buyer remembers who quoted properly. Estimate: operators treat 12–18 months as the typical window for a lost contract to come back around; treat that as operator pattern, not a study. The machinery: a quarterly light-touch nurture of every lost bid, tagged by why it was lost. Scored on: long-cycle nurture automations, loss-reason tagging, and re-engagement without looking desperate.

The ranking

1. MailerLite — the value pick that survives send-heavy B2B lists

Best for: commercial cleaners running the renewal runway and the facility-manager newsletter on one tool.

Commercial lists are small — hundreds of facility managers, not thousands of consumers — so MailerLite's pricing stays friendly here: Comfort at 500 subscribers is $12/mo (5,000 sends, the 10x send cap), 1,000 subs $19/mo, 2,500 subs $33/mo. But B2B lists are send-heavy: the newsletter + the bid ladder + the renewal runway + the win-back drip can be 4–6 sends a month to the same few hundred people, and the 10x Comfort cap ambushes exactly this pattern. Budget for Power (from $25/mo, removes the cap) once the program is running. The automation builder is strong enough for the whole contract engine, and the free tier (250 subs / 2,500 emails) genuinely covers a small cleaner's list while they build the newsletter habit. Honest check: at the list sizes commercial cleaning runs, the free tier lasts longer here than in any B2C vertical on this site — don't pay for software before you have the sends to justify it.

2. GetResponse — the automation depth pick for the bid pipeline

Best for: cleaners who live in proposals and want the follow-up ladder automated to the hilt.

Starter at $19/mo (sender) or Marketer/Creator at $59–$69/mo (platform). The automation builder is the strongest on this list for multi-branch bid sequences — different follow-ups for won, lost, and ghosted quotes, conditional on engagement. That depth is exactly what the bid pipeline and the lost-bid win-back want. The honest demerit: GetResponse's famous webinar and funnel machinery is near-worthless for a local cleaning company — you're not hosting webinars for facility managers — so you're paying platform prices for the automation half. If your sales process is genuinely pipeline-driven (RFPs, multi-location bids), the automation half earns it; if you're really just sending a monthly newsletter, MailerLite or Kit beats it on price.

3. Kit — the simplest path to a newsletter that actually ships

Best for: cleaners whose whole email program is the facility-manager newsletter and a renewal sequence.

Free to 10,000 subscribers at $0, then the bill jumps hard (one subscriber past 10,000 lands at $139/mo) — but a commercial cleaner will never see 10,000 subscribers, so the free tier is effectively permanent here, which is rare on this list. The trade: Kit's automations are thinner than MailerLite's and much thinner than GetResponse's — the bid ladder gets clunky beyond a few steps. If the business is "monthly newsletter + renewal reminders," Kit is the cheapest way to look professional doing it. If the business is "we bid constantly," the automation ceiling shows up fast.

4. Brevo — the email-plus-SMS pick for dispatch-adjacent follow-up

Best for: cleaners who want quote follow-ups by text and email in one place.

The SMS lane matters in commercial cleaning for a specific reason: the 24-hour bid-walkthrough reminder and the "quote expires Friday" text get opened when the email sat unread in a facility manager's flooded inbox. Brevo bundles email and SMS, prices email on volume (friendly for small B2B lists), and its CRM-lite keeps the bid pipeline visible. Honest check: SMS is a scalpel here, not a channel — the newsletter stays email, and every marketing text needs consent discipline that a B2B buyer will actually enforce. Use text for logistics and deadlines, never for the menu-equivalent.

5. AWeber — the reliable old guard, priced like it

Best for: cleaners who want a known quantity and will pay for it.

Deliverability reputation is real and corporate inboxes are where it matters most — AWeber's long track record counts in B2B. But the pricing climbs with list size on a plan structure built for bigger consumer lists, and the automation builder lags the top two for pipeline work. Fine if you inherit it; not the pick if you're choosing fresh in 2026.

6. Flodesk — the design pick (editorial mention, no affiliate program)

Best for: nobody's pipeline — but the templates are the prettiest on this list.

Flodesk makes the facility-manager newsletter look expensive, and in a trust-driven bid market, looking expensive has real value. The problem is everything underneath: the automation is too shallow for the bid ladder, the renewal runway, or the win-back drip. If the entire email program is one gorgeous monthly newsletter and the sales team handles everything else by hand, it's defensible. For the full contract engine, it isn't a contender. The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The quote follow-up ladder. Day 0: the proposal, itemized and clean. Day 3: the check-in (“questions on the scope?”). Day 7: the value proof — a one-page scope checklist showing what the low bid leaves out, not a discount. Day 14: the walkthrough invite (get them on-site; on-site wins). The documented vendor window is 2–4 weeks, so the ladder lives inside the window the buyer already set. Estimate: our operator-pattern estimate is that most commercial quotes that die do so in the follow-up gap, not on price — treat it as pattern, not a study.
  2. The 120-day renewal runway. T-minus 120 days: the performance recap with their numbers (response times, scope delivered, issues closed). T-minus 90: the updated scope for the next term — what changed in their building, what should change in the contract. T-minus 60: the early-renewal incentive (a locked rate, a scope upgrade) that makes re-bidding feel like unnecessary work. Renewal is a retention campaign, not a negotiation — the negotiation is what happens when the campaign didn't run.
  3. The facility-manager newsletter. Monthly, useful, barely salesy: seasonal scope planning (flu-season disinfection surge, post-construction cleanup), consumable-cost notes, one compliance or certification item, one client win told as a case study. Segment by facility type — the hospital-adjacent reader and the office-park reader need different emails. This is the channel that makes you the familiar expert when the RFP lands.
  4. The lost-bid drip. Every lost quote gets tagged with the loss reason and enters a quarterly light touch: the newsletter, one useful seasonal note, zero pleading. When the low bidder under-delivers — and the bid guidance warns buyers about exactly this — you are the vendor who quoted properly and stayed professional. Estimate: treat the 12–18-month return window as operator pattern, not a measured figure.
  5. The scope-upgrade cross-sell. The existing book is the cheapest revenue: day porterage add-ons, floor-care programs, restroom-consumable supply, post-event deep cleans. One targeted email per quarter per account, tied to their building's calendar (their lease renewal, their busy season). Email the customers you already clean; strangers get the bid ladder.

The honest catches

  • Send caps ambush B2B programs first. MailerLite's Comfort cap is 10x subscribers a month. A 500-person facility list gets 5,000 sends; newsletter + bid ladder + renewal runway + win-back = the cap, fast. Price the tool at your send volume, not your list size — Power ($25/mo+) exists for exactly this reason.
  • Email doesn't shorten procurement; it survives it. A formal RFP cycle can run 8–14 weeks (general-services figure, labeled above). No email platform compresses a procurement calendar. The job is staying present and useful across the whole cycle — the newsletter in month one is what gets the follow-up opened in month three.
  • Corporate inboxes are the hard mode. Facility managers live in Outlook behind corporate filters. Authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) is non-optional for B2B senders — run the per-tool setup in our email authentication guide before the first newsletter, not after the first spam-folder complaint. Bounce and complaint rates that a consumer list forgives will get a corporate domain throttled.
  • Free tiers last longer here — and that's a trap of its own. A 300-person B2B list sits inside MailerLite's free tier (250 subs) or Kit's (10,000) for a long time. The trap: building the whole contract engine on a free plan's automation ceiling, then discovering the bid ladder can't branch the way the business needs. Start free, but pick the tool whose paid automations fit the 12-month program, not the 2-month one.
  • Scope-first beats price-first, always. The bid guidance is explicit: beware low bidders who didn't understand the requirements. Every email in the bid ladder should teach scope — what a proper cleaning specification covers — because a buyer educated on scope can't unsee a thin bid. Discount emails train the buyer to wait; scope emails train the buyer to choose.

FAQ

How often should a commercial cleaning company email its list?

The newsletter: monthly, no more. The bid ladder: 3–4 touches inside the 2–4-week vendor window, then it stops — pestering a procurement process is how you get remembered badly. The renewal runway: 3 touches across 120 days. The lost-bid drip: quarterly. B2B rewards rhythm and restraint; the fastest way to get filtered is to email a facility manager like a consumer list.

Is email or the bid portal more important for winning contracts?

The bid portal is the arena; email is the warm-up and the follow-up. Nobody wins a contract in an email. But the newsletter is what makes the buyer open your proposal, the follow-up ladder is what keeps it alive through evaluation, and the renewal runway is what makes the re-bid unnecessary. Email wins the business around the bid, not the bid itself.

Can email marketing work when my buyers are facility managers, not consumers?

Yes — facility managers are people with inboxes and risk-averse jobs, and risk-averse buyers overvalue familiarity. A monthly newsletter that teaches scope and compliance for a year makes you the safe choice when the contract comes up. The content just has to be useful instead of promotional: nobody forwards a coupon to their boss, but they do forward a flu-season disinfection checklist.

Which tool if I only send a monthly newsletter?

Kit (free at these list sizes, simplest editor) or MailerLite's free tier (250 subscribers). Don't buy automation depth you won't use. The moment the program grows a bid ladder or a renewal runway, re-read the ranking — that's when MailerLite's or GetResponse's automations start earning their price.

Do commercial cleaners need SMS?

For marketing: no. For logistics: yes, selectively — the bid-walkthrough reminder and the quote-expiry text. Keep SMS to deadlines and confirmations with proper consent, run it through Brevo if you want it in the same tool, and never send the newsletter by text. A facility manager's phone is not a marketing channel; it's an interruption budget, and you spend it on the things that need interrupting.
