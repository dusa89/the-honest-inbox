What a food truck actually needs from email

Forget "open rates." The six engines that decide whether the truck eats this week:

  1. The location-drop engine. The weekly "where today" email — the schedule, the spot, the hours — timed to arrive 1-2 days before the serving days (the vendor-guide cadence that works: frequent enough to plan around, rare enough nobody unsubscribes). This is the owned version of what Kogi invented on Twitter in the mid-2000s: gather the crowd yourself instead of renting the algorithm's attention. A food-vendor list guide puts the math plainly: a 200-person engaged list generating ~$200/week in orders is $10,400 a year — from a free tool and five minutes of writing. And start at 10 subscribers, not 10,000: the habit matters more than the size. Scored on the tools: can it run a dependable weekly broadcast with the schedule front and center, and can the day-of change go out fast?
  2. The catering-quote engine. The high-ticket track. Weddings, corporate lunches, private parties, brewery takeovers — the contracts where one booking equals a week of window sales, and the track veteran operators call the real money. The machinery: the inquiry follow-up within the hour (the truck that responds first wins the date more often than the truck with the best menu), the quote sequence (the proposal, the tasting option, the gentle second nudge), the corporate-account drip (the office that books the monthly lunch), and the post-event review capture (one corporate catering review books the next ten). A 2026 industry launch guide credits catering with the power to increase a truck's revenue 100%+ within about a year and a half. Scored on the tools: can it run a quote pipeline with follow-ups, reminders, and a separate B2B voice?
  3. The festival-season calendar. Festivals, farmers markets, brewery rotations, school events — the high-footfall calendar that built this industry. The list's job: the season announcement (where we'll be all summer), the pre-event "find us" send, the post-event thank-you that converts foot traffic into subscribers, and the off-season warmth drip that keeps the list alive through winter. The truck with no festival calendar is a truck with no growth engine.
  4. The regulars loop. The repeat-customer cadence. More than half of food trucks run loyalty programs, and the ones that do see roughly a 30% increase in repeat visits (2026 industry guide). The email version: the visit-frequency trigger, the "it's been a while" nudge, the loyalty-member-only menu drop, the birthday freebie that costs a taco and buys a regular. The truck's margins are 6-9% — it cannot afford to re-acquire every customer every week.
  5. The menu-drop engine. Limited runs, seasonal specials, the new item worth lining up for. This is an event list, not a daily list: the drop announcement, the sell-through notice, the "sold out — next batch Friday" follow-up that trains the list to show up early next time. Distinct from every fixed-location vertical: the truck's menu drop is a destination event (people drive to you), not a case-race (people come before it's gone). Scored on the tools: can it handle the high-open-rate blast without a per-email price shock?
  6. The review engine. The "food truck near me" pipeline starts on your star rating — the hungry phone-scroller filters by stars before reading a word. The engine: the post-visit review ask (the QR on the window is the capture point), the post-catering review ask (a corporate client's review is worth ten window reviews — it books the next contract), the response cadence, and the review-to-list pipeline. The truck that captures the reviewer's email owns the reviewer twice.

Notice what's missing: no generic newsletters, no "we're a local business!" filler. The location drop does the revenue-work of telling people where to go, the catering engine does the high-ticket closing work, the festival calendar does the growth work, the regulars loop does the margin work, the menu drops do the event work, and the review engine does the trust work. A truck whose only email is the "happy holidays!" blast is running a mobile business on a fixed-location playbook.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a truck leaves on the table:

  1. The weekly location drop. Trigger: the schedule is set, weekly, timed 1-2 days before the serving days. The spot, the hours, the menu note — short enough to read in the time it takes to decide lunch. The bakery guide next door (bakeries) runs a daily-case sell-through; the truck runs a weekly location drop, because the truck's question is "where," not "what's left." Consistency trains the habit: the same send, the same day, every week, so regulars plan around it.
  2. The catering-inquiry sequence. Trigger: the inquiry, automated. The follow-up within the hour (the truck that responds first wins the date more often than the truck with the best menu), the quote with the tasting option, the gentle second nudge at 48 hours, the calendar-hold close. Then the corporate-account capture: every one-off catering client gets the "monthly lunch?" drip. One corporate account is worth a hundred window regulars.
  3. The festival-season announcement. Trigger: the calendar, scheduled. The season kickoff ("here's where we'll be all summer"), the pre-event "find us at the north gate" send, the post-event thank-you with the list signup for everyone who ate but didn't subscribe. Festivals are the list-growth engine; the thank-you email is the conversion step most trucks skip.
  4. The new-spot conversion. Trigger: first signup at the window (the QR code on the truck is the capture point). The first email is the hook: next week's full schedule, the catering menu link, the loyalty-program invite. The signup-to-second-visit window is days, not weeks — the truck is only there when it's there.
  5. The lapsed-regular winback. Trigger: the lapse (30-60-90 days no visit), automated. The check-in ("new spot near you on Thursdays"), the menu-drop exclusive, the last-call before the account goes cold. At 6-9% margins the truck cannot afford to lose a regular to forgetfulness — the winback email is the cheapest revenue on this page.

The honest catches

Your followers already get the location post — email is for everyone else. This is the truck's version of the rented-audience trap. Instagram reaches a fraction of your followers on any given day; the algorithm decides who sees the "we're here" post, and the people it skips are the people standing at the empty spot wondering where you went. Email's job isn't to replace the location post — it's to reach the people the algorithm skipped, and to arrive 1-2 days before the serving day so regulars can plan. Don't buy an email tool to do Instagram's job. Buy it to do the job Instagram can't: guaranteed delivery of the schedule.

Your ordering/POS stack already owns part of the flow. Don't double-buy it. Square and its cousins already send the transactional layer: the receipt, the order confirmation, the "your order is ready." Those are not this tool's job. The email tool owns the location schedule and the catering pipeline — the weekly drop, the inquiry follow-up, the quote sequence, the festival calendar. Audit what your stack already sends before you pay for an email tool to send it twice.

Day-of is SMS, week-ahead is email — don't mix them up. "We're at 5th & Main until 2pm" belongs in a text message at 11:40am, not in an inbox at 9am tomorrow. The weekly schedule belongs in email, planned and skimmable. The truck that emails the day-of change learns the hard way that nobody checks email at lunch. And the frequency ceiling is real: the vendor guide's rule is two emails a week, tops, unless something is genuinely time-sensitive — the list that gets the daily "we're open!" blast unsubscribes by Friday.

The schedule is the product — act like it. A location email that arrives after the location changed is an operations problem wearing an email costume. The weekly drop only works if the schedule is set before the send goes out. The truck whose spots change daily needs the SMS tool first and the email tool second; the truck with a set weekly rotation needs the email tool now. Know which truck you are.

How is this different from the ice cream shops guide?

The ice cream shops guide covers the visit business: a fixed address, walk-in traffic, flavor drops that sell through the list in days, the birthday-party pipeline, and a winter revenue cliff. The truck has no fixed address — that's the entire difference. Do I need email marketing if I already have 10,000 Instagram followers?

Yes — that's exactly the truck that needs it most. Ten thousand followers with algorithmic reach of a fraction is a few hundred people seeing the location post on a good day; the rest don't know you're there. The email list is the owned backup of the audience you already built: guaranteed delivery of the weekly schedule to the people who care most. The truck that relies on Instagram alone is renting its location announcements from the algorithm. The list is the landlord move.
