A pickleball club doesn't sell courts — it sells the league night. The numbers behind that are the wildest in American sport: 24.3 million Americans played pickleball in 2025, up from roughly 4.2 million in 2020 — more than fivefold in five years — and the Sports & Fitness Industry Association has named it the fastest-growing U.S. sport for five consecutive years (SFIA 2026 Pickleball Single Sport Report, released June 4, 2026). The audience split is the operator's whole playbook: 16.8 million casual players (1–7 times a year) against 7.48 million core players (8+ times a year) — the core segment grew from 1.4 million in 2020, and those 7.48 million are the league ladder, the membership base, the durable revenue. The infrastructure is chasing demand: USA Pickleball's database listed 82,613 courts across 18,258 locations in 2025, growing by roughly 39 courts a day, with 4,000+ tennis courts converted since 2021; SFIA estimates another ~25,000 courts and ~$855 million in investment will be needed over the next five to seven years (marked SFIA estimate). The customer looks nothing like the old stereotype: ages 13–24 post the highest participation rate of any segment, and women are 42.9% of players (up from 38.6% in 2020). And the honest headwind: SFIA's mid-2026 data showed growth moderating to +1.3% in the first half of 2026 — the land-grab phase is cooling into a consolidation phase, which means clubs win on retention now, not just signups. Every tool below is scored against that machine: the first paddle, the league ladder, the booking logistics, and the casual-to-core win-back.

  5. The casual-to-core win-back. 16.8 million casual players vs 7.48 million core — the club's growth lives in moving players up that ladder, and reactivating the ones who stalled. Scored on: play-frequency segmentation, re-engagement sequences, and the pause-don't-quit machinery.

3. Brevo — the email-plus-SMS pick for the court-status text

Weather calls are SMS-shaped: "courts 3-5 closed for rain, league play moves indoors" has to land in minutes, not at inbox speed. So are waitlist fills: the open slot goes to the first player who answers, and a text beats an email by hours. Honest check: SMS is the scalpel here, not the channel — the league newsletter and the season announcements stay email, and every marketing text needs real consent. Use text for court status, waitlist fills, and league-night reminders; never for the newsletter itself.

4. Kit — the simplest path to a weekly email that actually ships

The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The first-paddle welcome sequence. Day 0: the intro clinic confirmation with the "bring your own paddle or use ours" answer (the question every beginner has). Day 2: the "how your first night works" email. Day 7: the invite to the beginner ladder. The facility-level evidence says dedicated first-timer programming is where the big operators are investing — the newest mega-facilities build first-timer courts on purpose. Estimate: our operator-pattern estimate is that clubs converting the first session into a second visit within 7 days win the membership, and the welcome sequence is what manufactures the second visit — treat it as pattern, not a study.
  2. The league-season machine. Season open → early-bird ladder → the skill-tiered invites → season start → mid-season hype → playoff build → season recap that sells next season. The league is the retention product: it manufactures the weekly habit and the social graph that renews. The season recap email — photos, the rivalry stories, the "next season opens Tuesday" line — is the highest-ROI send a club writes, because every registered player is already sold.
  3. The day-pass-to-member ladder. Premium boards run $35–$40 for a day pass against $49–$149/mo memberships; one operator caps standard membership at 400 players. The math email: "you've played 6 day passes this month ($210) — a membership is $99" is the nudge that converts. The automation: count day-pass purchases per contact, fire the conversion email at the break-even threshold, cap the membership while scarcity is real. Never pitch membership to the tourist who played once; always pitch it to the player who hit the math.
  4. The court-status and waitlist sequence. The transactional layer: booking confirmations, the waitlist-fill text when a slot opens, the weather call that moves outdoor play indoors. This layer is judged on speed and accuracy, not copywriting — a booking email that lands in spam is a court that goes empty. Authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) runs before the first send, per our email authentication guide. The waitlist is the demand signal: a consistently full waitlist is the case for the next 4 courts, and the emails prove it.
  5. The casual-to-core reactivation. 16.8 million casual players against 7.48 million core — most of a club's list is casual, and the core segment doubled fivefold in five years, which means casual players DO convert when the path is clear. The sequence: the "you haven't booked in 30 days" check-in, the intro-to-ladder invite, the seasonal re-entry offer (fall leagues sell themselves). Segment by actual play frequency from the booking system, not by email-open rates.

The honest catches

  • The moderation signal is real. SFIA's mid-2026 data showed growth at +1.3% in the first half of 2026 after five years as the fastest-growing sport — and the 2026 industry narrative is consolidation and shakeout. A club built for the land-grab phase (signups at any cost) is misconfigured for the phase that's here: retention, league depth, and the casual-to-core ladder are where the money is now.
  • League ratings are noisy — segment by observed play, not by DUPR alone. DUPR is the most widely used rating system (3M registered users, official with USA Pickleball), but a 2026 industry analysis notes rec players widely find ratings inaccurate for thin match histories. Auto-seeding league emails off a rating field you don't trust is how 3.0 players end up in the 4.5 ladder and quit. Segment by the front desk's observed play plus self-rated skill, and let ratings inform — not decide — the ladder emails.
  • The day-pass trap. At $35–$40 a day pass, a club can fill courts with transient players and starve the membership base — and the email program has to know which list it's feeding. Every day-pass confirmation should carry the break-even math; every membership nudge should go to players who've hit it. A club that emails membership pitches to tourists and day-pass deals to members gets both wrong.
  • Initiation waivers are real deadlines. One LA club waived its $249 initiation fee through June 2026 and caps standard membership at 400 players — when a club actually has a cap and an expiry, the email program should say so loudly. But never manufacture urgency you don't have: if membership isn't capped, "only 3 spots left" is a lie the players will verify at the desk.
  • Outdoor clubs live and die by the weather text. The outdoor court is the product that can vanish by noon — and the "courts open" email that went out before the storm is the kind of message that teaches a list to ignore you. The weather call is SMS-shaped, the correction must go out on the same channel, and the indoor club's whole pitch is that it never has this problem.
  • Teens play most — but the parent pays. Ages 13–24 post the highest participation rate of any segment, which means the league ladder for juniors sells to the player and the membership sells to the parent. The climbing-gym article on this site made the same point about youth teams: two buyer emails, two value props, one household. One welcome sequence per age bracket is not a nice-to-have.

FAQ

How often should a pickleball club email?

Weekly minimum, segmented. The league/season newsletter is the heartbeat; court-status and waitlist fills go by SMS as they fire. Add the season-open machinery (a 4-6 week cadence around each season), the first-paddle welcome sequence for every new player, and the 30-day reactivation for lapsing players. Daily is too much unless it's season-open week; monthly is too little to hold the league habit.

How do you fill a league by email?

Skill-tiered invites to the players who already book at that level — the observed-play segment, not the whole list. The season-opens blast goes wide once; everything after is tiered. The highest-converting follow-up is the "two spots left in the 3.5 ladder" text to the waitlist, not a second mass email. The season recap email the week after finals — photos, stories, next season's date — is what fills the next league.

SMS or email for court-status alerts?

Both, different jobs. The weather call, the court closure, the waitlist fill — SMS, because it's time-sensitive. The season announcements, the league newsletters, the day-pass math — email, because they're browseable and visual. Every marketing text needs real consent; the booking-confirmation and weather-call texts are the safest SMS a club sends.

How do you win back a player who stopped coming?

By play frequency, not by guilt. The booking system knows who played 3x a week in spring and vanished in August — that player gets the fall-league invite, not the "we miss you" blast. The honest re-entry offer is the seasonal one: leagues sell themselves at season open. Estimate: our operator-pattern estimate is that reactivation offers timed to season open convert roughly 3-5x better than off-cycle win-back blasts — treat it as pattern, not a study.

What's the first automation a pickleball club should build?

The first-paddle welcome → second-visit sequence. The conversion event is the first session; the automation's job is manufacturing the second visit within 7 days. The league machine can be a manual send for a season. The first-paddle sequence should be automated from day one — it's the funnel every other engine on this page feeds.
