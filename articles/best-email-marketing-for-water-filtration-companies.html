A water-filtration company doesn't sell equipment — it sells the test result. The free in-home water test is this trade's lead magnet: a licensed water expert at the kitchen tap for about 30 minutes, on-site results for chlorine, iron, and hardness, and an equipment estimate before they leave — no cost, no obligation. The ticket is real money: a professionally installed reverse-osmosis system averages $2,200 nationally in 2026 (most homeowners spend $1,000–$4,800, per Angi's September 2026 data), water softeners run $1,500–$3,000 installed, and under-sink RO starts in the low $1,000s. Then the installed base pays forever: under-sink RO maintenance costs $50–$100 a year in filters (sediment and carbon every 6–12 months, the membrane every 2–3 years), whole-house systems run $400–$700 a year amortized — and the family spending $50–$100 a month on bottled water recoups the install in 18–24 months. The honest catches

  • The test is the product — never sell equipment before it. The operator who quotes blind is guessing: hard water gets the $1,500–$3,000 softener, contamination gets RO, taste-and-odor gets the $800–$2,000 carbon tank. The email program's job is booking tests, not selling boxes. A list that gets equipment pitches before test bookings is a list that unsubscribes.
  • Filter neglect kills the machine silently. The customer can't taste a dying pre-filter; they can taste the $400 membrane replacement that follows it. The 6-month reminder email is the cheapest insurance this business sells — treat it like revenue protection, not marketing. Filters every 6 months, membranes every 2–5 years (Angi 2026) — the schedule is the product.
  • The contamination-news spike is a send-cap event. When a local water-quality story breaks — and they break — the whole list gets emailed in one day, exactly the spike that trips MailerLite's 10x Comfort send cap (5,000 sends on 500 subscribers). The fix is boring: stagger the blast by ZIP code over three days, or run Power during news weeks (from $25/mo). The worst outcome is the trust email bouncing on the single best selling day of the year.
  • Reviews are the permission slip. The homeowner is letting a stranger test their tap water and quote $2,200 at the kitchen table — the review count is what makes that feel safe. Harvest the review at the install (the happy moment), not at the test (the anxious moment). The email program feeds the review profile before it feeds anything else.
  • The commercial sale is a different animal. The residential machine runs on health and taste; the commercial machine runs on equipment protection and operating cost. The buyer is a facilities manager with a budget cycle, not a homeowner with a faucet. One list, one voice, and the commercial buyer smells the mismatch instantly. Segment from day one.

FAQ

How much does a reverse osmosis system cost to install?

The national average is $2,200 in 2026 (Angi, updated September 2026); most homeowners spend $1,000–$4,800. Under-sink systems start in the low $1,000s installed; whole-house RO runs $2,000–$25,000+. The honest answer: the water test decides which one you need — a softener ($1,500–$3,000) solves hard water for a fraction of whole-house RO, and RO is the answer for PFAS, nitrates, and high dissolved solids.

How often do reverse osmosis filters need to be replaced?

Sediment and carbon pre-filters every 6–12 months ($8–$20 each), the RO membrane every 2–3 years ($30–$60), the polishing filter yearly. Annual DIY parts cost: $50–$100 for under-sink systems; whole-house runs $400–$700 a year amortized (2026 maintenance figures). Skip the pre-filters and the membrane dies early — that's the expensive lesson, and it's the reason the 6-month reminder exists.

Should I get a water softener or reverse osmosis?

Different jobs. The softener removes hardness minerals — the scale, the spots, the soap that won't lather. RO removes dissolved solids — lead, PFAS, nitrates, fluoride, the stuff you can't see. Hard water with no contamination → softener. Contamination or bad taste → RO. Plenty of homes end up with both: the softener for the whole house, RO at the kitchen sink for drinking water.

Are free in-home water tests legitimate?

The test is real: about 30 minutes at your tap, on-site results for chlorine, iron, and hardness, with a lab option for lead, PFAS, nitrates, and arsenic. It IS a lead magnet — the technician will give you an equipment estimate before they leave, and you should expect the pitch. There's no obligation. The smart move: get the numbers, then get a second quote from a different dealer before you sign anything.

What's the first automation a water-filtration company should build?

The 6-month filter reminder. It's the recurring revenue, the membrane insurance, and the reason the customer remembers your name — all in one email. Second: the test-booking confirmation and the morning-of no-show rescue. Third: the test-result follow-up ladder. Everything else on this page is optimization; the filter reminder is the business. Build it first, test it on ten installs, then scale the rest of the machine on top of it.
