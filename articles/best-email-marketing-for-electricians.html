That rhythm is why email works in this trade — the shop that emails its whole customer list in March about generator season books the summer's storm-prep installs before a single hurricane warning airs. The shop that tags every panel it ever serviced sells the panel upgrade before the customer buys an EV and discovers their 100-amp service can't feed it. What an electrical contractor actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. The safety-inspection ladder. The home electrical safety inspection is this trade's foot in the door — a low-ticket visit that finds the high-ticket work: the overloaded panel, the missing GFCI protection, the smoke alarms older than the teenager who lives there. Email's job is to fill the inspection calendar every spring and fall; the inspection itself sells the panel upgrade and the whole-home surge protector. The numbers back the pitch: the NFPA's May 2026 report estimates an annual average of 46,652 home electrical structure fires (2020–2024 data), with nearly half involving electrical distribution or lighting equipment — and those figures give the inspection email its honest hook.
  2. The panel-upgrade campaign. The most profitable upgrade in residential electrical is the panel — fuse box to breakers, 60/100-amp to 200-amp — and it is sold by age, not by fear. Tag every service call with the panel's age, brand, and amperage; the automation pitches the upgrade to homes with 20+ year-old panels, fuse boxes, or 100-amp service feeding a house that now runs central air and wants an EV charger. Many insurers ask about panel brands on new policies (Federal Pacific Stab-Lok is the famous example), so the upgrade email also sells insurance math — which beats scare copy every time.
  3. The EV-charger install sequence. Home EV-charger installs are the highest-margin residential add-on of the mid-2020s, and they almost always start with a panel conversation — a level-2 charger wants a 200-amp service. The sequence runs on intent signals: tag every customer who asked about an EV, everyone whose panel you just serviced, everyone in neighborhoods where EVs are multiplying in the driveways. The pitch writes itself: "We just serviced your panel — here's what a home charger would need from it, and the price to install it while we're already scheduled."
  4. The storm-season generator campaign. Whole-home generators are a once-a-decade purchase with a seasonal trigger — and the trigger is the calendar, not the forecast. The generator campaign goes out before the season (spring in hurricane country, fall where winter storms take the grid down), sells the peace-of-mind pitch, and books the install queue before it fills. For existing generator owners, the campaign becomes the annual service plan: the transfer-switch check, the exercise-test reminder, the maintenance visit — recurring revenue in a one-and-done trade.
  5. Review moat with crew photos. Nobody picks an electrician from a banner ad — they pick from the three shops with 200+ Google reviews, because this is the trade where a stranger works inside your walls and around your kids. The automated review ask ~48 hours after the job, with the direct Google review link and the tech's name in the subject line, is the highest-ROI email an electrical shop sends. Two emails max, then stop. In a trade where the map pack is the front page, this sequence is a moat-builder.

Notice what's missing: no lead scoring, no landing-page builder Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. Your field software (ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, Service Fusion — all established names in the trades CRM space) already handles dispatch, estimates, invoicing, and visit reminders; the email tool only has to hold the customer list, send the campaigns, and run a handful of seasonal automations. Paying for a marketing department's toolkit is the trap.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue an electrical company leaves on the table:

  1. The safety-inspection campaign. Trigger: spring and fall, before the seasons that stress electrical systems. Email 1: the home electrical safety inspection pitch to the whole customer list — the overloaded-panel check, the GFCI gaps, the aging smoke alarms — priced as the low-ticket entry visit. Email 2 (one week later, to non-openers): shorter, with the NFPA figure — an estimated 46,652 home electrical fires a year (May 2026 NFPA report, 2020–2024 average), nearly half tied to distribution or lighting equipment. The honest economics: the inspection is the foot in the door that finds the panel upgrade, the surge protector, and the detector replacements — the email books the inspections, the inspections book the real jobs.
  2. The panel-and-EV-charger ladder. Trigger: panel age tag — 20+ years old, fuse box, 60 or 100-amp service. Email 1: the inspection invite framed around capacity ("your 100-amp service vs a level-2 EV charger — here's the math"). Email 2: the upgrade pitch with the insurance angle — many insurers ask about panel brands and age, and the upgrade lowers the homeowner's insurance friction as well as their tripping breakers. Email 3 (to everyone who asked about EVs): the home-charger install pitch, timed to the panel conversation — a level-2 charger wants a 200-amp service, and the panel upgrade plus the charger is one job, one visit, one invoice.
  3. The generator storm-season campaign. Trigger: the pre-season window — early spring in hurricane country, early fall where winter storms take the grid down. Email 1: the whole-home generator pitch to the whole base, with the install-queue pressure ("we book out by June"). Email 2 (one week later): the maintenance-plan pitch to existing generator owners — the transfer-switch check, the exercise-test reminder, the annual service visit. The install is the decade purchase; the service plan is the recurring revenue, and the campaign runs every year.
  4. The seasonal service campaign. Trigger: the calendar. Holiday lighting installs get booked in October, not December — the October email books the season before the ladders are all spoken for. Ceiling-fan installs go out in spring before the heat. And the detector-replacement sequence runs on install-year tags: the NFPA says to replace all smoke alarms 10 years after their manufacturing date, so the "your detectors expire this year" email is a legitimate, time-stamped campaign — tag the install year at every detector job and the automation sells the replacements a decade later.
  5. The review sequence. Trigger: job marked complete. Email 1 (~48 hours): the thank-you + direct Google review link, the tech's name in the subject line if the customer knew them by name. If no review after 14 days, email 2: the gentle nudge with the link. Keep it to two emails — more reads as begging. Pair with the tech asking in person on the final walkthrough; the email is the follow-through, and in a trade where the map pack is the front page, the review count is the moat.

The honest catches

Emergencies are search's job — email wins the planned and preventable. Nobody whose power is out at 11 p.m. is checking your newsletter; they're Googling "emergency electrician near me." Email wins the revenue that can wait: panel upgrades, generators, EV chargers, inspections, holiday lighting. The two together are the pattern: search catches the crisis, email prevents the crisis and books the contract.

Text beats email for day-of logistics — use both. The "tech's on the way" text prevents the locked-door miss; the estimate follow-up rides on transactional email. Email wins the campaigns nobody texts about: generator season, the panel ladder, the EV-charger sequence, the detector replacements, holiday lighting. If you only do one, do the email — it costs almost nothing and the list compounds.

Lead with math, not scare tactics. The "your house will burn down" email burns trust faster than it sells inspections. The insurance angle is the honest pitch: panel age and brand are things insurers actually ask about, and a 60-amp service genuinely can't feed a level-2 charger. The NFPA's electrical-fire figures are real and current (May 2026 report), but they're the context for the inspection, not the hammer — the inspection sells itself on the panel's age and the home's load.

Your field software already sends some of this — don't double-send. ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, and Service Fusion all send appointment reminders, estimate links, and invoices; several run customer communications of their own. Let the field software handle transactional job communication; let the email tool handle marketing campaigns, seasonal sequences, reactivation, and review drips. When should the generator campaign go out?

Before the season, not during it — early spring in hurricane country (before the install queue fills), early fall where winter storms take the grid down. Pre-build the segment months ahead; writing the email on the first storm warning means it goes out when every other shop's does. The campaign repeats every year — template it once, and the maintenance-plan version runs to existing generator owners automatically.

How do I sell panel upgrades without scare tactics?

Lead with capacity math, then insurance math. "Your 100-amp service feeds the whole house; a level-2 EV charger wants 40–50 amps of that by itself" is a fact the customer can see. Then the insurance angle: panel age and brand are things insurers actually ask about on new policies. The pitch that respects the customer's intelligence beats the one that tries to frighten them — and the customers who buy from the math refer their neighbors.

Are Mailchimp or Constant Contact fine for electricians?

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and both are built for generic small business rather than this niche. Do I need email if my field software already texts customers?

Yes — for different jobs. Text wins the urgent, time-sensitive jobs (day-of logistics, gate codes, the review ask while the tech is still at the door). Email wins the campaigns nobody texts about: generator season, the panel ladder, the EV-charger sequence, detector replacements, holiday lighting.
