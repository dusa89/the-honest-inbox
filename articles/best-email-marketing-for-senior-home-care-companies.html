A senior-home-care company sells the one thing no other home-service trade sells: trust on a delay. The buyer — the adult daughter, 48, searching at 2 a.m. after her dad's fall — will never meet the product (the caregiver) before the purchase. She is buying a promise that a stranger will treat her parent well, made at the worst moment of her month. The economics are honest: the national median home care rate is $34/hour (A Place for Mom's 2026 report, 3,200+ agencies), with most families paying $20–$35/hour for companion care and $25–$45/hour for personal care; 15 hours a week runs about $2,208/month, and 24/7 live-in care runs $10,000–$22,000+/month (2026 industry figures). Research firms put the global senior home care service market at ~$658 billion in 2026 (estimate, marked), and the U.S. Census Bureau projects Americans 65+ growing from 58 million (2022) to 82 million by 2050. And this trade has an email problem no other vertical has: the list has two audiences with opposite needs — frightened families, and the caregivers themselves. Every tool below is scored against that machine: the adult-child buyer, the schedule-and-shift communication, the hospital-discharge lane, the caregiver pipeline, and the care-level upsell.

The honest catches

  • No health details in marketing email — ever. The weekly family care summary carries care notes, but the marketing newsletter must never carry health information about a client: no diagnoses, no conditions, no "how Mom's dementia is progressing" in a blast. Marketing emails carry zero PHI — and the vendor holding the family list needs a signed business-associate agreement. This is a compliance caution, not legal advice, but the agencies that blur it get burned by regulators long before they get burned by competitors.
  • The decider isn't the recipient — segment accordingly. The family list and the senior's own inbox are different audiences with different permissions. Marketing to the adult child about care for the parent is the business; marketing to the senior about their own decline is a different conversation entirely, and consent for one is not consent for the other. The segment boundaries are trust boundaries.
  • Never mix the caregiver list with the client list. A shift-fill broadcast to a grieving family is the fastest way to destroy a decade of trust. Two audiences, two segments, two consent records, zero cross-sends. The operator pattern (estimate, labeled): the agency that "accidentally" blasts caregivers from the family list loses both audiences in one send.
  • Home care is not home health — keep the lane clean. Home care is non-medical, private-pay help with daily life (companionship, bathing, meals); home health is skilled medical care, often Medicare-paid (nursing, wound care, therapy). Different buyers, different funnels, different compliance — and agencies that blur the two in copy confuse the buyer and invite regulator attention. Sell the lane you're actually in.
  • Trust decays when communication stops. The weekly care summary is the retention product, and the temptation after a smooth month is to skip it. Don't. The family that hears nothing for two weeks starts shopping — not because anything went wrong, but because silence reads as indifference to a buyer spending $4,000 a month on a promise. The agency that goes quiet after intake is manufacturing its own churn.

FAQ

How much does home care cost in 2026?

The national median is $34/hour (A Place for Mom's 2026 report, 3,200+ agencies, up from $33 in 2025). Typical ranges: companion/homemaker care $20–$35/hour, personal care $25–$45/hour, skilled nursing $40–$75+/hour. The monthly math at the $34 median: 15 hours a week runs about $2,208/month, 30 hours a week about $4,416/month, 44 hours a week about $6,478/month, and 24/7 live-in care $10,000–$22,000+/month (2026 industry figures). Private hires run cheaper ($20–$28/hour) but carry employer costs an agency absorbs.

What's the difference between home care and home health?

Home care is non-medical help with daily life — companionship, bathing, meals, light housekeeping — usually paid privately by the family. Home health is skilled medical care at home — nursing, wound care, physical therapy — often covered by Medicare after a hospitalization. Different buyers, different funnels, different rules; agencies sell one lane or the other, and the buyer should know which they're buying.

How do home care agencies find clients?

Three channels: hospital discharge planners (the 48-hour discharge window decides where the patient goes), the adult-child crisis search (the 2 a.m. Google query after a fall), and referrals from families, pastors, and senior communities. The 2026 industry surveys keep naming the same top pain point on the other side of the desk: 53% of agencies call caregiver shortages their key problem, and turnover approaches 77% — the agencies that win are the ones that recruit as hard as they market.

Why is caregiver turnover so high?

The 2026 industry figures are stark: turnover approaching 77%, recruiting at $2,600–$5,000 per hire, and 80% of new hires leaving within their first 100 days (AxisCare industry benchmarks). The Bipartisan Policy Center projects 8.9 million direct care job openings through 2032 that the current workforce won't fill. The work is emotionally heavy, often lonely, and wages are low — which is exactly why the first-100-day retention email sequence is the highest-ROI campaign in the business.

How big is the home care market?

Research firms put the global senior home care service market at roughly $658 billion in 2026 (Verified Market Reports, estimate, marked), and industry forecasting has U.S. home care projected to surpass $165 billion in revenue. The demographic engine: the U.S. Census Bureau projects Americans 65+ growing from 58 million in 2022 to 82 million by 2050 (a 47% jump). Fragmented, franchise-heavy (Home Instead, Right at Home, BrightStar Care, Visiting Angels), and growing — the tailwinds are real, and so is the staffing crisis.
