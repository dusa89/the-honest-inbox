What churches and nonprofits actually need (and don't)

Nonprofit lists are medium-sized and grow slowly. A working congregation or small nonprofit's list runs 500–3,000 contacts (our estimate — weekly bulletins and event signups grow the list steadily, not virally). Price at 1,000 and 5,000; anything beyond is shopping for someone else's organization.

What matters: a discount program you can actually claim, an interface a volunteer can learn in an afternoon, automations for the 3–4 flows that matter (welcome, event reminder, donation thank-you, re-engagement), segmentation that separates donors from volunteers from general subscribers, and deliverability that holds up for fundraising appeals.

What doesn't matter much: advanced e-commerce carts, multi-user CRM scoring, webinar suites (unless you're an advocacy org), and SMS upsells that a volunteer board has to budget-approve.

The fundraising email playbook (tool-agnostic)

The tool is secondary. Donation revenue comes from cadence and segmentation, not software. The 4 flows every nonprofit should run:

  1. New-subscriber welcome (3 emails). Mission story → what the organization does with money → soft first ask. Most nonprofits never build this; it's the highest-leverage automation they can add.
  2. Post-gift thank-you (2 emails). Immediate receipt-style thanks, then a 30-day impact update ("here's what your gift did"). Donor retention is the cheapest fundraising there is — the second gift is won in the thank-you, not the ask.
  3. Event reminder sequence. 14 days out, 3 days out, day-of. For churches: service times changes, special services, volunteer call-outs.
  4. Lapsed-donor re-engagement (annual). One honest email to donors who haven't given in 12+ months. Not a drip campaign — one email, one ask, one unsubscribe-clean list after.

The tool differentiator isn't capability — every paid tool above runs all 4. It's whether the free or discounted tier lets you start them: Kit's free plan gives you 1 automation (the welcome series), GetResponse Starter gives you 1 (same), and everything else needs the paid tier. Plan the upgrade into the budget from day one.

The volunteer problem: simplicity is the feature

Nonprofit email is run by volunteers, and volunteers rotate. The most common failure mode we see in this vertical isn't a bad tool — it's the tool being owned by one person's inbox. Three rules that matter more than the pricing table:

  1. The account belongs to the organization. Sign up with an org email (office@, communications@), not the volunteer's Gmail. Enable 2-factor on an org-owned number. Every discount program above requires organizational documentation anyway — that's your forcing function.
  2. Document the 5-minute handoff. Login location, the list segments, the 4 automations above, the discount renewal date. When the volunteer leaves (and they will), the next one inherits a working system, not a mystery.
  3. Put the discount renewal on the calendar. GetResponse's discount runs on a fixed term; MailerLite's doesn't expire but the proof must stay current; Constant Contact's is prepaid. A lapsed discount is an unbudgeted price increase.

Billing traps, nonprofit edition

  • The determination-letter gotcha — several discount programs require the formal IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter. Churches that are automatically tax-exempt (or self-declared) may not qualify; confirm eligibility with each vendor before building your budget around a discount. Kit's free plan needs no documentation at all.
  • GetResponse's logo requirement — the 50% discount requires placing their logo on your website within 4 weeks, and they verify it. If homepage real estate needs board approval, budget the approval into the timeline.
  • Discounts don't stack — MailerLite's 30% can't combine with the 10% annual rate; GetResponse's 50% can't combine with annual/biennial rates. You get the best single discount, not all of them.
  • Constant Contact's prepay lock — the 30% rate means 12 months paid up front. On a tight budget, that's a cash-flow decision, not just a discount.
  • One-automation ceilings — Kit's free plan and GetResponse Starter each cap you at 1 automation workflow. That's the welcome series; the thank-you and re-engagement flows need the paid tier. Map all 4 flows to the plan before you commit.
  • Counting unsubscribed contacts — several tools bill contacts, not active subscribers. A 15-year-old member list with thousands of dead addresses costs real money; clean bounces and unsubscribes before importing, or you pay for ghosts.
  • AWeber's auto-upgrades — AWeber bumps you to the next pricing tier automatically when your list grows, but downgrades are manual. After a big event-driven list spike, call to step back down.
