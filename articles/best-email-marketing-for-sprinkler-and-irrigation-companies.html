A sprinkler-and-irrigation company doesn't sell lawns — it sells the calendar. Nobody in home services lives by the clock the way this trade does: the water comes on in spring, the pipes get blown out in fall, and the whole year's revenue is decided in two booking windows — the March startup rush and the October winterization close. The economics are honest and repeatable: spring activation runs $50–$230 a visit (HomeAdvisor) and its real value is what the tech finds when the water comes back on — the cracked heads, the split pipes, the valves winter killed. Repairs run $65–$90 for a head, $75–$300 for a valve, $120–$400 for a pipe leak, up to $450–$1,700 for a backflow preventer (2026 cost guides). The fall close: professional winterization averages $93 nationally (Angi, October 2026, from 8,225 verified projects), and the damage it prevents shows up as $500–$2,000 in spring repairs when the system wasn't blown out right. Research firms put the global sprinkler irrigation market at $2.5–$4.7 billion in 2026 depending on scope (all estimates, marked) — and the segment growing fastest is the residential landscape side. Every tool below is scored against that machine: the spring-activation engine, the summer repair route, the winterization close, the controller-upgrade upsell, and the service-plan annuity.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The spring-activation rush. The calendar opens in March (wait for the last deep freeze). The startup visit — turn the water on zone by zone, check every head, set the controller — is priced at $50–$230, but its real revenue is the inspection: every cracked head and split pipe the tech finds becomes a quote. Scored on: the booking campaign that fills the March calendar, the startup-to-repair quote sequence, and the post-visit "here's what we found" email.
  2. The summer repair route. After the rush, the trade runs a repair route all summer: heads mowed off, valves failing, pressure problems. Repair tickets are $65–$1,700 depending on the component. Scored on: the mid-season check-up campaign ("dry spots = broken heads"), the repair-quote follow-up, and the reactivation of customers who haven't called in two seasons.
  3. The winterization close. The second booking window, and the one with real urgency: the first hard freeze is coming whether the customer books or not. The $93-average blowout is the easiest yes in the trade — but only if you ask in September and early October, because the October slots fill. Scored on: the winterization booking campaign, the "booked out" urgency email, and the fall-to-winter calendar close.
  4. The controller-upgrade upsell. The smart-controller ladder: a Wi-Fi controller with rain sensor installed at $425–$950 is the highest-ticket add-on the trade has — and the sell closes on the water bill, not the gadget. Smart controllers cut water use up to 30% (vendor claim repeated across guides, estimate labeled). Scored on: the "while we're here" upgrade sequence, the water-bill-math email, and the smart-upgrade drip for older systems.
  5. The service-plan annuity. The trade's recurring-revenue engine: $240–$350 a year or $20–$30 a month covers the startup, the mid-season visit, and the winterization — and trade reporting says monthly billing improves retention. This is what turns a seasonal trade into an annuity. Scored on: the plan-signup sequence, the plan-renewal campaign, and the member-vs-nonmember booking priority.

The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The March startup-booking campaign. The calendar-open campaign: "bookings are open, the water comes on after the last freeze" — sent to the full list in late February/early March, with the booking link and the route-map framing ("we run startups March 10–31, book your week"). The follow-up names the stakes honestly: the startup is priced at $50–$230, and what it finds is the product — winter damage becomes spring repairs. The campaign's job is slot-filling, not branding: every empty March day is a day the truck doesn't find repairs.
  2. The winterization close. The September campaign with the year's most honest urgency: the first hard freeze is coming, and the October slots fill. Lead with the price — the $93 national average, the local rate — and the damage math: a missed winterization becomes $500–$2,000 in spring repairs, and water expands about 9% when it freezes (estimate of the physics, confirmed by every burst pipe). The "three slots left" email in mid-October is the close; after the window closes, the campaign stops and the repair route resumes.
  3. The first-hard-freeze alert. The single highest-open email of the year — sent once, the week the forecast shows the first hard freeze: "freeze forecasted Thursday night — we still have winterization slots Wednesday." One per year (two if the first freeze is a false start). The rule is absolute: overuse this alert and it becomes noise; the customer who gets three "freeze alerts" stops believing the fourth.
  4. The controller-upgrade ladder. The drip for every system older than five years and every customer on an old-school timer: the water-bill math first (smart controllers cut water use up to 30%, estimate labeled), the installed price second ($425–$950 with rain sensor, per 2026 operator pricing), the utility-rebate note third (many water districts rebate smart controllers — check yours). And the "while we're here" version at every visit: the tech notes the controller age on the work order, and the upgrade email follows within 48 hours. Controllers are the fastest-growing component in the market for a reason — this is where the trade's upsell lives.
  5. The service-plan drip. The annuity campaign: the startup-to-winterization customer is sold the plan at the point of maximum relief — right after the first startup visit, when the system is running and the lawn is green. The framing is monthly math: $20–$30 a month covers the startup, the mid-season visit, and the winterization, and members get booking priority in October. Trade reporting says monthly billing improves retention — so sell the plan monthly, not annually, and the seasonal trade starts compounding.

The honest catches

  • The startup IS the inspection — quote on the spot. The tech doesn't just turn the water on; the tech is the salesperson. Every cracked head, every dead valve, every zone with weak pressure gets quoted on the spot — the truck is the showroom. The "here's what we found" email follows the same day with photos, but the quote lands verbally before the tech leaves. Email's job is to get the tech to the house; the tech's job is the sale.
  • DIY winterizations are your spring revenue — never sell the DIY. The homeowner's compressor (2–4 CFM) can't move the water a pro's rig moves (20–50 CFM), and the water left in low points becomes the $500–$2,000 spring repair. Never publish a "how to winterize it yourself" guide — that customer calls you in April anyway, but only after the pipe burst. Sell the $93 blowout; bank the April repairs from everyone who skipped it.
  • The calendar makes the year — March and October fill the revenue. This is the trade's defining truth: the business is won or lost in two booking windows. Email exists to fill slots, not to nurture "brand awareness." The newsletter is optional; the startup-booking campaign and the winterization-booking campaign are not. If you send twelve emails a year, make two of them the calendar campaigns and you did the job.
  • Sell the water bill, not the gadget. The controller upgrade closes on dollars, not Wi-Fi: the water-bill math, the up-to-30% usage cut (estimate, labeled), the rebate. The customer doesn't buy a smart controller — the customer buys a smaller water bill and a lawn that isn't brown in August. Lead the email with the bill, close with the schedule link.
  • The freeze alert dies if overused. One honest alert per year earns trust and fills the last slots; three alerts a season makes you the boy who cried freeze. If the forecast is wrong and the freeze misses, say so in the next email — the honest miss builds more trust than the manufactured urgency ever did.

FAQ

How much does it cost to install a sprinkler system in 2026?

Cost guides put residential installation at $0.88–$1.65 per square foot (LawnLove, 2026), $585–$1,340 per zone, with a typical quarter-acre yard needing 4–7 zones; HomeGuide's broader range is $0.50–$2.50 per square foot and $3,000–$10,000 total for a quarter-acre. NerdWallet's 2026 average is $2,539. Operator pricing (Kent, WA, 2026) runs $4,800–$8,400 for a complete 5-zone system on an average city lot.

How much does sprinkler winterization cost in 2026?

Angi's October 2026 data (8,225 verified projects) puts the national average at $93, with most homeowners paying $56–$137 and $10–$20 added per extra zone. Operator pricing published this month (Omaha, October 2026) is $104 for systems up to 7 zones, plus $10 per additional zone. Winterization season typically runs mid-September through mid-November, and October slots fill fast.

How much does a spring sprinkler startup cost?

HomeAdvisor's guide puts professional activation at $50–$230, including turning the water on, inspecting the system, and adjusting timers. One Ohio operator's 2026 pricing starts spring startups at $249 (activation, inspection, basic adjustments), with a 3-visit annual maintenance package (startup + mid-season + winterization) at $637. The real value is the inspection: what the startup finds — winter damage, cracked heads, dead valves — becomes the repair quote.

How much water does a smart sprinkler controller save?

Cost guides repeat the vendor claim that smart controllers cut irrigation water use up to 30% (estimate, labeled — actual savings depend on climate, soil, and how badly the old timer was overwatering). What's the difference between a sprinkler/irrigation company and a landscaping company?

The calendar, not the yard: landscapers design and build (installs, planting, hardscaping — see Best Email Marketing for Landscaping Companies), while irrigation companies run the seasonal machine — spring startup, summer repairs, fall winterization, controller upgrades — on the systems already in the ground. Different businesses, different email machines; the irrigation trade's entire year is two booking windows and a repair route.
