An art gallery does not sell paintings — it sells the night the red dot goes on the wall card. The US has roughly 5,000 art dealer and gallery establishments doing about $10 billion a year (First Research, 2026), dealers globally moved ~$34.8 billion in 2025 (Art Basel & UBS 2026), and the average dealer staged 8 exhibitions that year — 8 openings, 8 first nights, 8 races. Because here is the thing the art world knows and never says out loud: the show often sells before the opening. Galleries distribute PDF previews — digital dossiers, condition summaries, pricing — to established clients 10 to 14 days in advance, and the coveted works are frequently pre-sold or placed on reserve before anyone steps through the door. At the Armory Show this September, sales were reported "even before the fair officially opened through emailed previews." The preview is not marketing for the opening — the preview IS the sale. The machinery that runs it is all email: the preview-sale machine (the PDFs that move first), the opening-night engine (the RSVP tiers, the dot night), the collector list (segments by artist, medium, and price — the thing the mass blast destroys), the sold-work waitlist (one-of-one means the loser of the race is the next show's buyer), and the artist-roster engine (the artists who bring their own lists). Here is how the best tool for the gallery runs them.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The preview-sale machine. The core of the business. Weeks before the opening, the collectors who matter get the work by email: the PDF preview, the dossier, the condition summary, the price list. The fair world proved the pattern at scale — VIP previews are preceded by weeks of pre-selling, with primary works reserved before preview-day doors open. The gallery version: the tiered preview (top collectors see it 14 days out, the next tier 7, the list gets the public announcement the morning of), the reserve email (the "hold" that converts before the opening), and the preview-to-dot pipeline (the preview that becomes the red dot by night's end). Scored on the tools: can it run the tiered preview — different people, different dates, tracked interest — without the top tier seeing the bottom tier's email?
  2. The opening-night engine. The event the preview serves. Opening night is the gallery's whole marketing calendar compressed into one evening: the invite tiers (the closed-door preview for the inner circle — the Promenade Fine Art pattern — then the public opening), the RSVP engine (the confirmations, the plus-ones, the reminder that fills the room), and the dot-night sell-through (the first-come preview sales made right on the wall during the evening). The room is also the list-builder: every attendee who isn't yet a collector is the next show's audience. Scored on the tools: can it run the invite tiers and capture the room into the list the same night?
  3. The collector list. The asset the whole business runs on. This is the engine Artsy's own gallery guide warns about: email connects with collectors directly, but "used incorrectly it has the potential to do more harm than good" — the mistake is the mass blast. The collector who buys $40,000 abstract paintings must never receive the $800 print announcement, and the emerging-artist collector must never feel like the room they bought into. The machinery: the segments (by artist, by medium, by price band — the three cuts that matter), the personal-send discipline (Artsy: send invitations from your personal account, not through a mass blast — the collector opens the gallerist's name, not the brand's), and the interest records (what they looked at, what they asked about, what they almost bought — the fair world's version of this is standard practice). Scored on the tools: can it segment by taste and send like a person, not a platform?
  4. The sold-work waitlist. The one-of-one math. A book pre-order stacks — the indie bookstores guide runs that machine. A painting does not: when the dot goes on the card, the race ends, and the losers of the race are the most valuable people in the room. The machinery: the waitlist (the "next one like it" queue for the edition or the artist's next piece), the secondary track (the consignor and resale relationships that keep inventory moving between shows), and the near-miss save (the collector who asked about the sold piece gets the artist's next work FIRST — the loss becomes the preview). Scored on the tools: can it turn a sold piece into the next sale automatically?
  5. The artist-roster engine. The 2026 trend that changes the list. Galleries increasingly want artists who drive their own traffic — "artists with a growing email list," as the trade puts it: partners who arrive with an audience, not dependents who need one built. The machinery: the roster cross-pollination (each artist's show introduces the gallery's collectors to the next artist), the artist-co-marketing track (the artist emails their list about the show — the gallery supplies the assets), and the post-show nurture (the attendees of the spring show are the preview list for the fall). The gallery's list and the artists' lists are two engines that feed each other. Scored on the tools: can it run the roster's parallel shows without the lists bleeding into each other?

Notice what's missing: no generic "monthly newsletter" (the collector does not need content — they need the preview, the opening, and the personal note), no discounting (the gallery that discounts a $40,000 painting trains collectors to wait), no social-media dependence (the algorithm shows the preview to strangers; the email shows it to the buyers). The preview sells the show, the opening fills the room, the segments protect the relationships, the waitlist catches the near-misses, and the roster compounds the audiences. A gallery whose email is "the monthly newsletter" is running a collector business on a broadcast calendar.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a gallery leaves on the table:

  1. The tiered preview drop. Trigger: 10–14 days before the opening. The top tier gets the PDF dossier first, the next tier a week later, the list gets the public announcement the morning of. The collectors who buy before the doors open are not an accident — they are the product of the tier. The preview is the sale.
  2. The opening-night RSVP. Trigger: the invite tiers, two weeks out. The closed-door preview for the inner circle, then the public opening — the confirmation, the plus-one, the day-before reminder that fills the room. The room is the list-builder: every attendee who isn't a collector yet is the next show's audience.
  3. The dot-night sell-through. Trigger: opening night. The first-come preview sales made right on the wall — the dot on the card during the evening. The preview emails that didn't convert get one more send the morning after: "three works still available." The race doesn't end at midnight; it ends when the walls are empty.
  4. The sold-work waitlist. Trigger: the dot. The collector who lost the race gets the "next one like it" email — the waitlist for the edition, the artist's next piece, the first look at the next show. The near-miss is the hottest lead in the building.
  5. The post-show reactivation. Trigger: 30 days after the opening. The attendees who didn't buy get the story: the sold works, the artist's next show, the consignment invitation ("thinking of selling? the list is buying"). The quiet collector is reactivated by the roster, not by the discount.

The honest catches

The blast is the enemy. Artsy's own gallery guide says it plainly: email connects with collectors directly, but used wrong it does more harm than good. The mass blast — the same announcement to the $40,000 collector and the $400 print buyer — is how a gallery tells its best clients they are nobody special. The personal send from the gallerist's own name is not a tactic; it is the business model in an inbox.

The middle is being squeezed. The 2026 market data is blunt: dealer sales stabilized at ~$34.8B, but the middle of the market — $1M–$10M turnover — is flat on revenue with costs up, while small dealers grew fastest in percentage terms. The gallery that survives the squeeze is the lean one with the list: the preview machine and the collector segments are not marketing, they are the margin.

The preview can cannibalize the opening. If everything sells in the preview, the opening night is a party with empty walls — and the room full of people who came to see art is the point. The tiered preview is the discipline: the top collectors buy early, but the show must still be a show. Hold back the works the opening needs.

Never discount the work. The gallery that discounts a $40,000 painting to move it trains every collector on the list to wait for the discount. The waitlist, the secondary track, and the consignment relationships move inventory — the discount moves the floor under the whole roster's prices.

The artists are the other list. The 2026 trade trend: galleries want artists who bring their own audiences. Treat the roster like a distribution network — the artist who emails their Kit list about the show is doing the gallery's best marketing for free. But the gallery's collector list stays the gallery's: the preview machine runs on it, and it is never lent out.

The fair is a different business. The fair preview (the emailed dossier 10–14 days out, the reserves before the doors) runs on a faster clock than the gallery preview — and on a different list. Run them as separate tracks with separate segments, or the fair's pace will burn the gallery's relationships.
