The 2 foundational flows (build these first)

Everything downstream — paid conversions, retention, swaps, the back catalog — compounds on readers who open your emails and know who you are. Build these two before anything clever.

1. The new-subscriber orientation (day 0, day 3, day 7)

Trigger: signup from any source — reader magnet, Substack-style subscribe button, website form. Sequence: email one (day 0): the welcome — who you are in one sentence, what lands in their inbox and how often, and the one thing to do now: the “start here” link to your 3 best essays. Email two (day 3): the world-building email — for authors, this is the sample chapter, the world guide, the character list; for newsletter writers, the greatest-hits archive sorted by topic. Email three (day 7): the preference email — “what do you want more of?” with 2–3 topic links, and every click tags the subscriber. The honest mechanics: this sequence is not the book-launch funnel’s welcome (that one sells the author’s world toward a launch) — this one runs on every signup, forever, and its second job is segmentation: the topic tags from email three decide which cross-promo swaps and which back-catalog pitches each subscriber gets later. An unsegmented author list is a list where every book launch spams the wrong readers; the orientation is where segmentation is born, for free, at signup.

2. The free-to-paid conversion drip (behavior-triggered: 2 paywall hits in 7 days)

Trigger: behavior, not time — a free subscriber hits your paywall twice in 7 days, or reads 3+ paywalled previews in a month. These are your hottest conversion candidates; the paywall is doing the qualifying for you. Sequence: email one: the value recap — what paid gets, priced against what they’ve already been reading for free (“you’ve read 4 paywalled essays this month — paid is $8/mo”). Email two (day 3): the trial or the sample — a 7-day paid trial, or one full paywalled essay unlocked with the “there are 40 more like this” line. Email three (day 7): the annual nudge — the 12-for-10 framing on the annual plan, which the vertical guide calls cash-flow gold for a reason. The numbers: our estimate, consistent with reported newsletter benchmarks, is that 2–10% of a free list converts to paid — and the behavior-triggered ask converts at the top of that range, because it only fires on demonstrated appetite. This is the flow that gets the single automation slot on a one-workflow tier (GetResponse Starter, Flodesk Lite) — it out-earns every other flow in the playbook, so it goes first, full stop. The honest catch: don’t gate the wrong things — the vertical guide’s line stands (“the same newsletter but you feel good about it” doesn’t convert). Paid has to unlock something scarce: bonus essays, full archives, community, your time. Gate the right things and this drip is the highest-ROI automation an author owns.

The 3 retention and list-health flows

3. Paid-member onboarding + the day-21 engagement check

Trigger: a paid subscription starts. Sequence: email one (day 1): the paid welcome — what the money unlocks, where the paid archive lives, the community/thread invite, and one line that answers the unasked question (“here’s what lands in your inbox this month”). Email two (day 7): the depth email — the single best thing a paid member can do this week (the community thread, the bonus essay, the template you promised). Email three (day 21): the engagement check — the automation looks at opens: opened 2+ of the paid sends, nothing happens. Opened fewer than 2, the nudge fires: “you’re paying for this — here’s the one thing worth your 10 minutes.” The honest mechanics: most author churn isn’t price churn, it’s attention churn — the member forgot why they pay. The day-21 check is the only automation in this playbook that watches behavior after the sale, and it’s the difference between a paid list that decays and one that compounds. If the nudge bounces off (no opens at day 45), the member drops into flow #4’s paid variant: the honest “pause or stay” email, which saves more revenue than any discount.

4. The cold-subscriber win-back + sunset (90 days no opens)

Trigger: 90 days without an open, refreshed monthly. Sequence: email one: the honest check-in (“still want to hear from me?” — one click to stay, one link to your 3 best recent pieces). Email two (day 7): the preference downgrade — the monthly-digest option, the “books only, no essays” option; some readers don’t want to leave, they want less. Email three (day 14): the goodbye — “I’m removing you from the list this week; resubscribe anytime” — and then you actually remove them. The honest mechanics: this flow is a deliverability play wearing a politeness costume. Dead addresses drag your open rates down, and inbox providers read low engagement as “this sender’s mail isn’t wanted” — a clean list lands in more inboxes than a big one. Every author who has ever said “my opens collapsed” was mailing a list full of people who stopped reading in 2024. The sunset is how you find out, and it costs you the subscribers who were never going to buy anything anyway.

5. The cross-promo swap engine

Trigger: your calendar — monthly or quarterly swap weeks. Sequence: this one is half automation, half operations: find 3–5 newsletters at a similar size in adjacent (not competing) niches; agree the swap terms (one dedicated recommendation email each, same week); the automation is the swap-week broadcast to your list (their best piece, your honest one-line endorsement, the subscribe link with UTM tags so you can measure), plus the automated thank-you-and-results email to the partner afterward. The honest mechanics: this is the growth engine authors actually use, and it’s the reason the vertical guide treats Substack’s recommendation network as the one argument with teeth — but you don’t need Substack to run it. Kit’s Creator Network runs paid cross-promos (the guide notes Kit takes a cut on the paid ones); MailerLite and Beehiiv run it as plain broadcasts with UTM tracking. The UTM tags are the whole discipline: track which swaps actually convert, cut the ones that don’t, and double down on the genres that do. One good swap partner at your size beats a month of social posts; five good ones are a growth channel.

The 2 revenue flows

6. The back-catalog evergreen (series readers, samples, and the review ask)

Trigger: a purchase, a download, or a sample request — the reader raised their hand, and the hand-raise is fresh. Sequence: for fiction series: the reader buys or downloads book one → day 14, the “how was book one?” email with the book-two pitch and the one-click buy link; finished-series detection (or a simple day-60 nudge) fires the “readers who finished the trilogy also grabbed” pitch. For nonfiction: the sample-chapter download → day 14 follow-up (“did the sample land?” + the full-book link + the one objection-handler for your category). For every buyer: the day-30 review ask — Amazon and Goodreads links, one sentence of guidance (“even 3 lines helps more than you think”) — and the day-60 reminder for the non-responders. The honest mechanics: the vertical guide’s book-launch funnel is the launch-week broadcast sequence (announce, social proof, cart-close) — this flow is the always-on version that runs between launches, forever. A launch happens twice a year; the back catalog sells every day. The review ask is the compounding layer: reviews are the social proof the next launch sequence runs on, and an automated ask at day 30 beats every “please review!” plea you’ll ever write by hand, because it fires while the reading experience is still warm.

7. The annual-upgrade + founding-member campaign (month 11)

Trigger: a monthly payer hits month 11, or you open a founding-member tier. Sequence: the upgrade email: “you’ve paid $96 this year as a monthly member — switch to annual at $80 and the next 12 months cost less than the last 11.” The honest framing is the standard one (12 months for the price of 10), and it works because the member already decided the product is worth it — you’re only changing the billing rhythm. The founding-member variant: a limited-seat tier (name it, number it — “founding member, one of 50”), priced 2–3× the standard annual, with one scarce benefit (the annual planning call, the signed copy, the name in the acknowledgments). The honest mechanics: the vertical guide calls annual plans cash-flow gold — a year of revenue upfront from readers who would have paid monthly — and this automation is the trigger that collects it instead of hoping it happens. Run it every November; it’s the highest-margin email an author sends all year. The catch: the founding tier must actually be scarce and actually be better — a “founding member” badge on the same product is a coupon, not a tier, and your best readers can tell.

The send math: the 1,500-subscriber newsletter worked example

Worked example — a newsletter with 1,500 subscribers on MailerLite Comfort ($19/mo at the 1,000-contact band... verify your band at checkout, send cap roughly 10× the list): weekly broadcasts to 1,500 = ~6,500 emails/mo; the orientation sequence firing on ~50 new subscribers a month (3 emails each = 150); the conversion drip on ~30 behavior triggers (3 emails = 90); the win-back on ~40 cold subscribers quarterly (3 emails = 120/quarter, ~40/mo); review asks and upgrade emails = a few dozen. Total: roughly 7,000–7,500 emails a month against a ~15,000–20,000 cap — under half the allowance, with room to double the list before the cap enters the conversation. Your volume will differ; add your broadcasts plus drip sends before you pick the tier.

The Substack question: what stays, what moves

Before you build a single flow, answer the platform question the vertical guide frames: is your newsletter the product (paid subscriptions) or the channel (books, courses, the launch army)? If the answer is “product, and I’m on Substack,” the honest move is a second tool for the automation layer — Substack’s automations won’t run flows 2, 3, 4, or 7, and its segmentation won’t support the swap engine’s targeting. If the answer is “channel,” the cheapest honest stack is Kit free or MailerLite for the automations plus whatever publishes the newsletter. And if you’re starting the paid newsletter fresh: run the crossover math in the vertical guide before you pick — past ~$490/mo in subscription revenue at the 1k tier, the flat-fee stacks beat Substack’s 10%, and the gap only widens. The migration gets named on day one or it becomes year-three inertia; own your domain either way (~$12/year, the cheapest insurance in the vertical guide).

The 4 mistakes that kill author email

  • Building on the wrong tier. Substack for a 7-flow automation playbook. Kit’s free plan for automations it excludes. GetResponse Starter or Flodesk Lite for a 7-flow playbook on one workflow. Beehiiv Launch for a paid playbook it can’t monetize. Every flow built on a tier that can’t run it is a rebuild. Pick the tier from the table above, then build.
  • Gating the wrong things. “The same newsletter but you feel good about it” doesn’t convert — the vertical guide’s line, and the conversion drip can’t fix a paywall with nothing behind it. Paid unlocks something scarce: bonus essays, full archives, community, your time. Build the paid product before you automate the ask.
  • Letting cold subscribers rot. Every 90-day-silent address on your list is a small drag on deliverability and a large lie in your open-rate reports. The win-back and sunset isn’t list-shrinking — it’s the reason your real readers’ emails land in inboxes instead of spam.
  • Forgetting the annual campaign. Monthly payers who would have gone annual are a year of cash you left on the table, and the founding-member tier is the highest-margin product most authors will ever sell. November comes once a year; the automation means you don’t have to remember it.
