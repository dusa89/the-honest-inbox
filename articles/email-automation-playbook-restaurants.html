Restaurants don’t have a newsletter problem — they have a slow-Tuesday problem. The email list exists to fill seats on the nights that don’t fill themselves, and that job runs on 7 automations: the four flows every restaurant should build first — the welcome-to-first-visit sequence, the birthday and anniversary offer, the 60-day win-back, and the event pre-fill machine (three of which we mapped in the vertical guide) — plus three that separate a working list from an address book: reservation no-show recovery, lapsed-regular re-engagement, and the slow-night fill engine that turns a dead service into revenue on 48 hours’ notice. This playbook is the trigger-by-trigger build order, the tier-by-tier tool math (restaurants send weekly to big lists, so the wrong tier hurts more here than anywhere), and the four mistakes that kill restaurant email.

The 4 baseline flows (build these first)

These are the flows the list pays for. Build them before anything clever:

1. The welcome-to-first-visit sequence (3 emails, 7 days)

Trigger: signup — Wi-Fi splash, QR on the check presenter, website. Sequence: three emails over 7 days: the welcome (your story in one paragraph, the menu link), the first-visit offer (the free-appetizer-with-entrée or 10%-off nudge that converts a saved address into a table), and the reservation CTA (direct link to OpenTable/Resy/your booking page). Most restaurant lists collect addresses and never say hello — this sequence is the hello that pays.

2. The birthday and anniversary offer (date-triggered, set-and-forget)

Trigger: birthday or anniversary date captured at signup (make the field optional and you’ll still capture it from roughly half of signups — our estimate; make it required and signups fall). Action: one email 7 days before: the free dessert or birthday discount code. This is the single highest-ROI automation in restaurant email — a once-a-year email that feels personal and brings a table of four.

3. The 60-day win-back (first-visit follow-up)

Trigger: guest tagged at first visit (POS sync, reservation data, or staff-entered) who hasn’t returned in 60 days. Sequence: one email — “we miss you,” the incentive, the reservation link. Regulars are made here, not acquired: the guest who came once and came back is worth ten addresses that never dined. The honest mechanics: this flow needs your reservation or POS data to actually tag first visits — the automation fires from the email tool, the visit data comes from the booking system.

4. The event pre-fill machine (per event)

Trigger: calendar — wine dinner, chef’s table, holiday menu, live music night. Sequence: segment past event attendees first and give them 48-hour early access, then broadcast the remainder to the full list, then a 7-day-out reminder to opens-who-didn’t-book. Event attendees convert at multiples of the cold list (our estimate — event segments consistently outperform broad broadcasts). This is the flow that makes an events-heavy restaurant pick Constant Contact: native event registration forms keep the whole event inside one subscription.

The 3 advanced automations

5. Reservation no-show recovery (the 24-hour window)

Trigger: confirmed reservation with no check-in — the booking shows as no-show. Action: one email within 24 hours: no guilt, just the rebook link (“something came up? your table’s waiting”). The no-show guest is warm by definition — they already chose your restaurant once. This is also the flow that justifies integrating the booking system with the email tool: without the no-show signal crossing into the automation, there is no trigger. Zapier or native integrations bridge OpenTable/Resy/Tock into the email tool’s segments.

6. Lapsed-regular re-engagement (behavior-based, 90 days)

Trigger: guest with 3+ past visits and no visit in 90 days. Action: the conditional offer — “it’s been a while” plus the incentive sized to their history (the 5-visit regular gets a better offer than the 2-visit one). This is the only automation in the playbook that requires behavior-based multi-trigger logic — and that logic is a paid-tier feature: on MailerLite it means Power, not Comfort. Everywhere else it means the automation-grade tier too (Constant Contact Standard, Flodesk’s template logic stretches here, GetResponse Marketer, ActiveCampaign Plus). Price this jump before you promise yourself behavioral triggers.

7. The slow-night fill engine (day-of / 48-hour flash)

Trigger: your calendar — the Tuesday that’s looking thin, the private-event cancellation, the week after a holiday. Action: a same-day or 48-hour offer to the “local regulars” segment (segment by zip or past-visit recency): “tonight only” prix fixe, half-price bottles, the off-menu special. This is the only flow in the playbook that’s genuinely spontaneous — it fires from a template you pre-build once and fill in 10 minutes. The restaurants that make email pay its rent run this flow monthly. Note the send cost: a flash blast to 5,000 contacts is 5,000 emails in one day — on Brevo free’s 300-a-day cap that’s dead on arrival (see the send math below).

The send math: does the weekly special fit the cap?

Worked example — a restaurant with 5,000 contacts on MailerLite Comfort ($49/mo, send cap roughly 10× the list = ~50,000 emails/mo): four weekly specials = 20,000 emails. Two event blasts a month = 10,000. The drip volume (welcomes, birthdays, win-backs) = ~2,000–3,000. Total: roughly 32,000–33,000 emails a month — comfortably under the 50,000 cap, with room for one slow-night flash blast (5,000) a month. Constant Contact Standard at 5k allows 12× contacts (60,000) — fine. Flodesk: unlimited — fine at any volume. Brevo free at 300/day (~9,000/month) is dead on arrival for a 5,000-contact weekly sender — it’s a paid plan, not a free plan, for you. Your volume will differ; add your weekly sends plus event blasts plus drip sends before you pick the tier. Note: MailerLite only bills active subscribers you’ve emailed in the past 30 days (per independent pricing trackers) — a quiet edge for seasonal restaurants.

The reservation-system question: don’t buy what OpenTable already does

Before you build a single flow, look at what your reservation platform already sends: OpenTable, Resy, and Tock all ship confirmation and reminder emails, and many restaurants are paying for automation they never wired together. The honest division of labor: the reservation system owns transactional triggers (booking confirmations, day-before reminders, no-show flags) because it holds the booking data; the email tool owns the narrative flows (welcome sequences, win-backs, event marketing) because its builder, templates, and deliverability are built for it. The no-show and lapsed-regular automations live at the seam — they need booking data to flow into the email tool via Zapier or a native integration. Restaurants that duplicate confirmations in both systems send double emails and train guests to ignore both. Wire the seam once, and the two systems stop fighting.

The 4 mistakes that kill restaurant email

  • Building on the wrong tier. Constant Contact Lite gives you one automation template for a seven-flow playbook; Kit’s free plan covers your whole list and none of your automations; GetResponse Starter gives you one workflow; Brevo free gives you zero automations and a 300-a-day cap. Every flow built on a tier that can’t run it is a rebuild. Pick the tier from the table above, then build.
  • Automating from an unauthenticated domain. SPF and DKIM authenticate your domain with the sending platform — records set up for your reservation system do nothing for your email tool. Verify the sending domain in the email tool, confirm the green checks, and test-send before the first flow fires. A welcome sequence that lands in spam is worse than no sequence.
  • Capturing without consent. Wi-Fi splash pages and QR signups need a clear opt-in — you cannot scrape the reservation book into a marketing list. CAN-SPAM penalties run up to ~$53,000 per email, and every email tool appends an unsubscribe link automatically — let it. The birthday flow is worthless if it fires to someone who never agreed to hear from you.
  • Mailing so often people stop looking. The weekly special plus event blasts plus slow-night flashes is already 6–8 sends a month to some subscribers. Daily emails train guests to delete unread — and your trigger volume (birthdays, win-backs, no-shows) already gives the list plenty to hear about. Weekly broadcasts plus triggered flows is the ceiling; above that, every send costs you the next one’s open rate.

Compliance summary, not legal advice: consent to add, unsubscribe on every send, and clear sender identification. When in doubt, ask your accountant or attorney.
