A hailstorm crosses a metro on a Tuesday and every roofer in the market has a month's worth of work by Thursday — and the shop that emails its past customers and their neighborhoods first books the inspections before the storm chasers do. What a roofing company actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. Post-storm inspection campaigns. Hail or wind moves through your service area; within 24–48 hours you email past customers in the affected ZIP codes with the inspection offer and the reason (granule loss and hail bruising are invisible from the ground — homeowners don't know they're covered until someone looks). One email, timed to the storm, beats a month of generic advertising. The list has to be tagged by ZIP or territory for this to work — a blast to your whole list reads as noise to the neighborhoods that stayed dry.
  2. Unclosed-estimate follow-up drips. This is the annuity-rescue equivalent in roofing. You handed a homeowner a five-figure quote; they're "thinking about it" — which means they're getting two more quotes. An automated 5-email sequence over 21 days (the summary, the warranty explainer, the insurance-claim angle, the review/social-proof proof, the gentle deadline) closes estimates while you're on roofs. The tool needs a tag trigger — "estimate sent" → wait → send — and if yours can't do that automatically, it's the wrong tool. Every estimate that dies in silence was a $9,000–$14,000 job for a typical architectural-shingle replacement (2026 ranges via Fixly Guide and Sansouka).
  3. Insurance-claim education sequences. Storm restoration shops live on insurance work, and homeowners are confused by claims — what the adjuster does, what RCV/ACV means, why the deductible is their only out-of-pocket when damage is covered (a roof-claim guide updated for 2026 puts the national full-replacement range at $9,500–$28,000, and notes many homeowners pay only the deductible when insurance covers the damage). A 4-email sequence that explains the claim process in plain language, delivered right after the storm campaign, turns "I don't understand my claim" into a signed contingency agreement. Nobody sends this and everybody should.
  4. Post-install review sequences. A roofing job is the biggest check most homeowners write to a contractor, and it comes with a warranty they'd like to trust. The crew asks in person on the final walkthrough — that remains the highest-converting ask. The email is the follow-through: an automated ask ~48 hours after completion with the direct Google review link, one nudge two weeks later. Two emails, no more. In a trade where the top-rated shop with 300 reviews eats the market, this is a moat-builder.
  5. Re-roof anniversary and maintenance reminders. Your CRM knows every install date. At year 5 (architectural shingle midpoint), year 10, year 15 — a "your roof is aging, here's what to watch for" email to past customers converts into inspections and repairs, and eventually into the replacement you bid against nobody else because you're the company that remembered. Annual gutter/maintenance reminders ride along. This only works with install dates in the email tool or a bridged list — a stale import with no dates is a list you can't use.

Notice what's missing: no lead scoring, no landing-page builder Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. Your roofing CRM (AccuLynx for the insurance-restoration shops, JobNimbus for the residential teams — both among the 2026 category leaders per Zuper and Urban Build reviews) already handles leads, pipelines, estimates, and scheduling; the email tool only has to hold the customer list, send the campaigns, and run a handful of simple automations. Paying for a marketing department's toolkit is the trap.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a roofing company leaves on the table:

  1. The post-storm inspection campaign. Trigger: a hail or wind event in your service area. Email 1 (within 24–48 hours, affected ZIP tags only): the storm hit — granule loss and hail bruising are invisible from the ground, here's why an inspection is free and worth an hour. One email per event, not a week of follow-ups; the storm chasers are knocking doors and you are the company they already know. Segment by territory — a blast to dry neighborhoods trains the whole list to ignore you.
  2. The unclosed-estimate drip. Trigger: estimate sent, no contract after 3 days. Email 1 (day 3): the one-page recap of what you quoted and why. Email 2 (day 7): the warranty explainer — the manufacturer warranty and your workmanship warranty are the difference between you and the cheaper quote, and homeowners don't know that until someone explains it. Email 3 (day 12): the insurance-claim angle for storm-age roofs. Email 4 (day 16): the social proof — recent jobs with review links. Email 5 (day 21): the gentle deadline, honest about material price movement (roofing inputs kept rising through 2026). Then stop. The drip's job is the decision, not the chase.
  3. The claim-education sequence. Sent to storm-campaign respondents and anyone tagged "storm lead." Email 1: what the insurance claim process actually looks like, in plain language. Email 2: RCV vs ACV — what those letters mean for their out-of-pocket. Email 3: what the adjuster does on the inspection and how to prepare. Email 4: the deductible question answered honestly, and the contingency agreement explained. An educated homeowner signs with the company that taught them — usually you, because nobody else sent this.
  4. The post-install review sequence. Trigger: job marked complete in your CRM (or a weekly CSV export if you don't bridge). Email 1 (~48 hours): the final-walkthrough thank-you + direct Google review link. If no review after 14 days, email 2: the gentle nudge with the link. Keep it to two emails — more reads as begging. Pair with the crew asking in person on the final day; the email is the follow-through, and the review count is the moat.
  5. The re-roof anniversary. Trigger: install date + 5, 10, 15 years. "Your roof is now 10 years old — here's what to watch for" with the inspection offer. Annual gutter and maintenance reminders ride the same mechanism. Nobody competes for this email because you're the only one who knows the install date — and when the replacement finally comes, you bid against nobody.

The honest catches

Your roofing CRM already sends some of this — don't double-send. AccuLynx and JobNimbus both send appointment reminders, estimate links, and production updates. Let the field software handle transactional job communication; let the email tool handle marketing campaigns, storm sequences, and education/review drips. Check what your CRM already does before you pay for an email tool to do it twice.

The storm campaign has a timing window — automate the trigger, not just the email. The 24–48-hour window after a storm is the whole play. If "someone remembers to send the email" is your plan, the storm chasers win. Pre-write the storm template, pre-build the territory tags, and write the trigger into your process: storm report → pull affected ZIPs → send. The automation is the process, not just the tool.

Storm chasing has a compliance shadow — stay local and honest. Post-storm solicitation is regulated in some states and scrutinized everywhere; some jurisdictions require solicitor permits or have cooling-off rules on storm-related contracts. This is a reminder, not legal advice: market to your own past-customer and lead list first (that's the honest campaign), keep claims about damage conservative, and know your state's rules before your crews knock a door they weren't invited to.

Text beats email for appointment logistics — use both. The day-before-install text prevents the missed start; the email 48 hours after completion asks for the review. Email wins the campaigns nobody texts about: storm inspections, claim education, estimate chases, anniversary reminders. If you only do one, do the email — it costs almost nothing and the list compounds.

Your list import is the real setup cost. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting the CRM file and tagging it properly — install dates, territory/ZIP, lead source, estimate-sent status (estimate — most CRM exports won't have these tags out of the box). Budget an afternoon, not ten minutes. A list without dates can't run anniversary campaigns and a list without territories can't run storm campaigns — the tags are the product. FAQ

What's the cheapest email tool for a roofing company?

At typical shop list sizes (300–2,000 contacts, estimate), MailerLite's paid plans are the cheapest of the five tools here with real automations included — paid starts from $10/mo. Brevo is cheaper if you blast territory-wide storm campaigns to a bigger list ($9/mo for 5,000 emails). All prices checked October 3, 2026 — verify live before checkout.

Can I import my customer list from AccuLynx or JobNimbus?

Yes — every tool here takes a CSV export, and both CRMs export one (both are among the 2026 category leaders per third-party roofing-CRM roundups). The integration depth varies: direct or Zapier-path connections exist, but the honest workflow for most shops is a scheduled CSV export and import, with install dates and territory tags added as custom fields. Our MailerLite integrations guide walks through the bridge math.

How soon after a storm should I email my list?

Within 24–48 hours, to the affected territory only — that's the whole play. The inspection offer has to arrive while homeowners are still thinking about the storm, and before the storm chasers own the neighborhood. Pre-write the template and pre-build the territory tags now; writing from scratch on storm day means it goes out on day 4, which is too late.

How many follow-up emails should an unclosed estimate get?

Five over 21 days, then stop — recap, warranty explainer, insurance angle, social proof, honest deadline. A $9,000–$14,000 architectural-shingle replacement (2026 typical range) is a considered purchase, so the sequence earns each email by teaching, not by pressuring. After day 21 the estimate is dead; the anniversary sequence takes over at year 5.

Are Mailchimp or Constant Contact fine for roofing?

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and both are built for generic small business rather than this niche. See Mailchimp alternatives for the migration math.

Do I need email if I already text customers?

Yes — for different jobs. Text wins the urgent, time-sensitive jobs (install-day logistics, the review ask while the crew's visit is fresh). Email wins the campaigns nobody texts about: post-storm inspections, claim education, estimate chases, anniversary reminders. The two together are the pattern that moves both bookings and the review moat.
