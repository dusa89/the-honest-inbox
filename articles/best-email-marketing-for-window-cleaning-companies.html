A window-cleaning company doesn't sell clean glass — it sells the appointment, and the appointment repeats forever. The residential math is brutally simple: a home with 20–25 windows pays $250–$450 per visit (2026 national pricing figures), and it gets cleaned twice a year — spring and fall — which means a single household is worth $500–$900 a year in base revenue before a single upsell (screens, tracks, skylights, hard-water stain removal). The storefront math is even better: small storefronts run $40–$75 a visit on weekly or biweekly routes, which is $2,000–$3,900 a year per account for thirty minutes of ladder work. The commercial table is real money — one research firm puts the global commercial window cleaning market at ~$28.5 billion in 2025 (~$30.2 billion in 2026), with corporate offices as 45% of buyers (estimate, marked) — but the market's own research says it's "highly fragmented, dominated by a large population of small local operators," with the big franchise names (Fish, Window Gang, Window Genie) winning through recurring contracts and bundled services (window + gutter + pressure wash). Every tool below is scored against that machine: the repeat-cadence engine, the storefront route, and the seasonal rhythm that owns the year.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The repeat-cadence engine. Residential cleans twice a year (spring and fall); the money is the rebook. At $250–$450 per visit (2026 national figures), a household on cadence is $500–$900/year — and the operator who forgets to ask for the fall booking in September leaves half the revenue on the table.   2. The storefront route. Weekly/biweekly at $40–$75 a visit is the densest recurring route in exterior services — but it only works at density: one crew, one street, ten storefronts. The pitch is walk-in speed (the quote happens on the sidewalk in 60 seconds), the retention is reliability. Scored on: the route-building campaigns, the multi-location chain pitch, and the upsell to awnings and signage glass.
  3. The upsell engine. The base clean is the ticket in; the margin is the extras: screens, tracks, skylights, hard-water stain removal, storm windows, post-construction. Operator-published pricing puts the average residential job at $220 — the upsell is what moves it toward $400. Scored on: the add-on menu campaign, the "while we're here" sequence, and the hard-water treatment program (the real margin add-on).
  4. The seasonal rhythm. Spring is the rush (pollen, winter salt, curb appeal before the market); fall is the second rush (holiday guests, pre-winter). Summer is the slow fill; winter is the hole. The email program's job is booking the spring calendar in February and the fall calendar in August. Scored on: the seasonal launch campaigns, the slow-month fill sequences, and the weather-trigger honesty (the rain reschedule policy that keeps trust).
  5. The review-and-photo machine. Before/after photos sell this trade better than any copy — glass is the one service where the difference is visible in a single frame. Reviews are the actual top of funnel: the homeowner picks the 4.9-star company with 200 reviews. Scored on: the review-harvest automation, the photo-consent flow, and the neighborhood referral lane (one happy cul-de-sac = five households).

The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The spring launch blitz. Launch in February, sell March: "Pollen season is coming. The spring calendar opens now." Three tiers: early-bird (book by March 1: locked price), standard (March: full price), the "first week of April is 80% full" honesty blast when it is. The upsell layer: exterior-only for the budget buyer, screens-and-tracks for the full reset. Estimate: our operator-pattern read is that residential runs the 2x/year spring/fall cadence — treat it as pattern, not a study — which means the spring blitz isn't selling a cleaning, it's selling the year's first half.
  2. The 6-month rebook automation. The single most important automation on this page. April service → October email: "Your fall cleaning is due — same crew, same windows, one click to rebook." October service → April email: the mirror. At $250–$450 a visit (2026 national figures), one automated rebook email per customer per year is worth hundreds — it's the engine that turns a one-time clean into a $500–$900/year household.   4. The upsell engine. The base clean is the ticket in; the campaign is the add-on menu. The honest catches

  • The route is local — the list must be too. This business dies on windshield time: a $220 average job (operator-published figure) with an hour of driving is a $0 job. The email list's #1 segmentation field is the ZIP code, and the campaigns only go to the route. The operator who emails a 50-mile radius is marketing to people they'll never profitably serve. Segment by neighborhood, send by route density, and never let the list outrun the truck.
  • Winter is the hole — email can't fill January. Nobody is excited about window cleaning in freezing rain. The winter program isn't selling cleans; it's selling the spring calendar in February and the spring-launch waitlist in January. Price the business against the seasonality: the spring and fall rushes carry the year, summer fills the gaps, winter is for maintenance, training, and the "book now, pay March" early-bird. An email program that pretends winter is a selling season will just teach the list to ignore you.
  • The per-window vs per-pane confusion is where quotes die. Per-window ($10–$18, 2026 national figures) and per-pane ($4–$8) are different pricing models, and the customer doesn't know which one you're quoting. The quote-follow-up email should name the model in plain words: "20 windows at $14 each, both sides, screens included — $280 total." Confusion is the #1 reason quotes go unanswered; clarity is the close.
  • Reviews are the real funnel — email's job is harvesting them. The homeowner picks the 4.9-star company with 200 reviews over the 5.0-star company with 6. The email program doesn't replace Google Business Profile; it feeds it — the hour-after review SMS, the "we just passed 200 reviews" milestone blast, the photo-consent ask that stocks the before/after gallery. Spend email effort on reviews before you spend it on clever campaigns.
  • The spring-rush send spike is a send-cap event. The March launch blast to the whole list in one day is exactly the spike that trips MailerLite's 10x Comfort send cap (5,000 sends on 500 subscribers). The fix is boring: stagger the launch over three days by ZIP, or run Power during launch weeks (from $25/mo). The worst outcome is the launch email bouncing on the single best selling day of the year.

FAQ

How often should a window-cleaning company email residential customers?

Twice a year for selling, once a quarter for staying warm. The two selling emails are the spring launch (February/March) and the fall rebook (August/September) — the cadence the business runs on. The quarterly touches are the trust layer: the "pollen season tips" note, the holiday greeting, the review milestone. More than that and you're noise on a list that only buys twice a year; less than that and the fall customer forgot who you are by spring.

What's the best way to get more storefront window-cleaning contracts?

Walk the block, then email the chains. The independent storefront is won on the sidewalk in 60 seconds with a $40–$75/week pitch and an introductory first clean. The multi-location chain is won by email: the one-contract-every-location proposal, named-street social proof, the quarterly review. The route is the product — sell density ("we're already on your street Tuesday") before you sell price, because the cleaner already on the street is always cheaper to keep than to replace.

Should window cleaners charge per window or per pane?

Per-window ($10–$18 in 2026 national figures) is faster to quote and easier for the customer to understand; per-pane ($4–$8) is more precise for French panes and divided-light windows. The honest answer: quote per-window for standard homes, per-pane for complex glass — and name the model in the quote so the customer isn't comparing your per-window price to a competitor's per-pane price. The follow-up email should restate the model in one line. Confusion kills more quotes than price does.

Is email or text better for window-cleaning appointment reminders?

Text for the day, email for the season. The "we're on your street Tuesday" text, the day-before confirmation, the crew-is-15-minutes-out message, the hour-after review ask — all SMS. The spring launch, the fall rebook, the add-on menu, the quote follow-up — all email. The operator who sends the season plan by text trains customers to ignore texts; the operator who sends the 8 a.m. arrival notice by email trains customers to call.

What's the first automation a window-cleaning company should build?

The 6-month rebook sequence. The revenue model is two visits a year at $250–$450 each (2026 national figures) — the automation's job is making the second visit happen without a phone call. April service triggers the October email; October service triggers the April email. Everything else on this page is optimization; the rebook sequence is the business. Build it first, test it on ten customers, then scale the rest of the machine on top of it.
