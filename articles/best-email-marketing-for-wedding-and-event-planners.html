A wedding planning business is booked a year before it is performed — and referred by everyone around the table. The couple inquires in January, books in March, and the wedding is next spring; whoever follows up owns the booking. The photographer who shoots your weddings sends you couples; the venue coordinator who trusts you sends you more. The portfolio that closes the inquiry is a styled shoot that looked like a Vogue spread. And the couple whose wedding you just nailed knows three engaged friends — but only if you ask, while the memory is fresh. A planner's marketing is five engines: the inquiry nurture sequence, the vendor referral network, the portfolio newsletter, the post-wedding review + referral ask, and the engagement-season booking push. Scored below are the tools that survive contact with a planner whose busiest season is when she's also working weddings — and the famous name this niche should skip.

What a wedding & event planner actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five engines that book the calendar:

  1. The inquiry nurture sequence. Couples plan 12–18 months out (estimate), and the first reply wins a disproportionate share of bookings — the inquiry that matters is answered in hours, not days. But the long game is the nurture: the day-1 reply with the portfolio link and the "here's how we work" one-pager, the week-1 email with the three most relevant real weddings, the month-2 availability update ("I have three Saturdays left next October"), the quarter-2 check-in ("still deciding? here's what couples ask me at this stage"). The planner who stays in the couple's inbox for the whole decision cycle books the wedding; the planner who sent one reply does not.
  2. The vendor referral network. The planner's real sales team isn't on payroll — it's the photographers, florists, venue coordinators, and caterers who recommend each other to every couple. Email runs the network: the styled-shoot announcement ("here's the shoot we just did together — tag it, share it"), the vendor spotlight ("my florist of the month"), the mutual-referral check-in ("sent you two couples last quarter — here's who I'd love an intro to"). Reciprocal referrals are the oxygen of this business; the email list is the network's nervous system.
  3. The portfolio newsletter. The proof is visual: real weddings and styled shoots, photographed like the brand you sell. The newsletter is the recurring proof — monthly or quarterly, the last three events, one story each, the booking CTA. This is the one engine where the email's look matters as much as its logic: an ugly email from a planner sells against the portfolio.
  4. The post-wedding review + referral ask. The couple is happiest the week after the wedding — and their engaged friends are the warmest leads on earth. The sequence: day 3, the thank-you with the testimonial ask (the direct Google review link); week 3, the referral ask ("who do you know planning their wedding? I'll send them our planning checklist"); month 3, the anniversary check-in (the one planner email everyone remembers — and the one that re-fires the referral ask a year later). Reviews feed the map pack; referrals feed the calendar.
  5. The engagement-season booking push. Engagement season runs roughly November–February (estimate) — the holidays, Valentine's, the spring proposal wave. That's when the inquiry wave hits, and that's when availability announcements go out: "booking 8 weddings for next year," "three October Saturdays left." The seasonal announcement to the full list + the vendor network turns a quiet January into a booked season. One campaign a year, templated once, repeated forever.

Notice what's missing: no landing-page Olympics, no lead scoring, no AI subject-line generators. The planning suite (Aisle Planner, Planning Pod, Rock Paper Coin — the established names in the trade) already holds the clients, the contracts, the proposals, and the timelines. The email tool only has to hold the prospect and vendor list, send the campaigns, and run the nurture–network–proof–ask automations. Paying for a second operations suite is the trap.

GetResponse's edge is the automation builder on Marketer ($59/mo): branching workflows that run the long engagement-cycle nurture and the vendor network with minimal babysitting — the inquiry drip with the week-1/month-2/quarter-2 branches, the vendor check-ins on a timer, the anniversary drip that re-fires referrals a year later. Pick GetResponse over MailerLite if you want the vendor network and the long nurture running automatically with branching logic — one booked wedding pays for the tool for a year. If marketing is genuinely just the portfolio newsletter and the seasonal announcement, MailerLite's simplicity wins.

3. Flodesk — the design-first pick, with the honest trade

Flodesk is the tool built for this aesthetic: the templates look genuinely custom — a wedding planner's newsletter can look like the brand she sells, with no design skills. And the pricing changed: Flodesk abandoned its famous flat-rate pricing in December 2025 — pricing now scales with subscriber count like everyone else. 4. AWeber — the simple pick for the small practice

AWeber is the old guard: straightforward campaigns, a reputation built over decades, and an interface the solo planner can learn in an afternoon. It won't win on price at scale and it won't win on automation depth — but for a planner who sends the portfolio newsletter, the seasonal availability announcement, the post-wedding thank-you, and nothing else, "works fine and stays out of the way" is a legitimate pitch. The affiliate math is verified: AWeber pays 30% recurring on its affiliate program, the same neighborhood as MailerLite and Kit.

Pick AWeber if automations scare you and the plan is genuinely just the newsletter + announcements + review asks. The moment you want the inquiry nurture running automatically, you're back at MailerLite for less money.

5. Kit — the wrong uniform

Kit is an excellent tool for creators selling courses and newsletters. It is a bad tool for a planner. The pricing is subscriber-based and climbs steeply (the entry Creator tier starts around $39/mo and the jumps at 5k and 50k subscribers are the sharpest of the five tools here), the feature set is built around digital products, and there is no planner angle anywhere in its template library. The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a planner leaves on the table:

  1. The inquiry nurture. Trigger: inquiry form submitted. Email 1 (same day, hours if possible): the personal reply with the portfolio link and the "here's how we work" one-pager — speed is the closer. Email 2 (week 1): the three most relevant real weddings ("here's one like yours"). Email 3 (month 2): the availability update ("I have three Saturdays left next October"). Email 4 (quarter 2): the check-in ("still deciding? here's what couples ask me at this stage"). The decision cycle is 12–18 months (estimate) — stay in the inbox for all of it.
  2. The vendor network check-in. Trigger: quarterly, on a timer. The styled-shoot announcement with the shareable photos ("here's the shoot we did together — tag it, share it"), the vendor spotlight ("my florist of the month — send her your June brides"), the mutual-referral ask ("sent you two couples last quarter — here's who I'd love an intro to"). Reciprocal referrals compound; the planner who nurtures the network gets the next booking from it.
  3. The portfolio newsletter. Trigger: monthly or quarterly. The last three events, one story each, the booking CTA. This is the visual proof engine — the couple who found you in January decides in June after seeing four newsletters. If the emails don't look like the brand, this engine runs backwards — this is the one place Flodesk's design advantage is a real argument.
  4. The post-wedding review + referral ask. Trigger: wedding day. Email 1 (day 3): the thank-you with the direct Google review link — ask while the memory is fresh. Email 2 (week 3): the referral ask ("who do you know planning their wedding? I'll send them our planning checklist"). Email 3 (the 1-year anniversary): the check-in nobody else sends — and the referral ask re-fired when the couple knows more engaged couples than they did a year ago.
  5. The engagement-season booking push. Trigger: the November–February inquiry wave (estimate). The seasonal announcement to the full list + the vendor network: "booking 8 weddings for next year," "three October Saturdays left," the planning-checklist lead magnet that captures the inquiries that arrive all at once. Template it once, repeat every year — this is the campaign that makes January the month the calendar fills.

The honest catches

Your planning suite already owns the operations — don't double-buy it. Aisle Planner, Planning Pod, and Rock Paper Coin already hold the clients, the contracts, the proposals, the payments, and the day-of timelines — plus their own basic client messaging. Let the suite own operations; let the email tool handle the prospect nurture, the vendor network, and the proof. Check what your suite already sends before you pay for an email tool to do it twice.

HubSpot is the incumbent for bigger event firms — and overkill for a solo planner. HubSpot's marketing hub genuinely does email + CRM + event workflows in one place, and big event businesses use it. But the price and the learning curve are built for teams, not a planner doing marketing between client meetings. If you're a firm with staff, it's the migration comparison; if you're solo, it's not the tool this page is about.

Speed is the closer on inquiries — automation is the insurance. The day-1 reply that matters is a human reply, sent in hours. The automation's job is everything after: the week-1 portfolio email, the month-2 availability update, the quarter-2 check-in — the nurture that runs while you're working someone else's wedding. Set the nurture up once in any tool on this page with automations; never let a template reply be the first thing a couple hears from you.

The newsletter has to look like the brand. Planners sell aesthetics — an ugly newsletter argues against the portfolio. This is the one vertical where the design-first pick (Flodesk) has a genuine argument, and it's the one thing to check on MailerLite's editor before you commit: send yourself a test campaign, open it on your phone, and decide whether it looks like your brand. Brevo is the budget pick if you blast. Mailchimp is the incumbent — and the migration target. It's the name most planners already have: simple blasts, familiar editor. It's also the tool that costs more at scale and gates the automations that make this page's nurture run — the inquiry drip, the vendor network, the anniversary sequence. If you're on it, the migration math is usually simple: the same list costs less on MailerLite with better automations attached.
