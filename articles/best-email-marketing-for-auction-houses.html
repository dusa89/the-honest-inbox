An auction house doesn't sell lots — it sells liquidity. The global auction-house market is estimated at about $22.6 billion in 2026 (Cognitive Market Research, covering fine art, antiques, and luxury collectibles, up from about $17.7 billion in 2021), and the online-auction slice alone is estimated at $24.75 billion in 2026, projected toward $70.6 billion by 2035 at 12.36% annual growth (one businessresearchinsights report, updated October 2026). North America holds roughly 44% of the auction-house market (Cognitive, 2026). And the whole thing runs on one asset the catalog people talk about and the marketing people undervalue: the bidder list. An auction with no registered bidders is a room full of unsold lots. The consignment pipeline is the trust sell — estates, dealers, and collectors handing you their grandmother's jewelry or a barn full of equipment only because they believe your list will bid. The lot-preview email is the catalog for most of the trade now: hero lots, specialist previews, the "this one goes Thursday" nudge. The practitioners' playbook is documented, not folk wisdom: segment bidders by specialty (the vintage-car collector gets the auto auction, not the estate sale), send reserve-aware previews to the high-value tier, use no-bid and most-active lots as the urgency hooks, and keep a consistent cadence so the list is warm when the gavel needs it (auction email-marketing guidance, FasterCapital, 2026). Every tool below is scored against that machinery: the consignment trust sell, the lot-preview engine, the specialty segmentation, the sale-day ladder, and the underbidder second-chance machine.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The consignment engine (the trust sell). Consignments are the inventory — no estates, no dealers, no sale. Winning an estate means months of trust-building before the contract: the "we sold the Miller estate for $480K" proof, the specialist's authority, the executor's newsletter. Scored on: long-cycle nurture sequences, the price-realized proof newsletter, and referral capture from attorneys, realtors, and estate planners.
  2. The lot-preview engine (the email IS the catalog). The printed catalog is dead for most regional houses; the emailed preview is the catalog. Hero-lot emails, specialist previews by category, the "bidding closes Thursday" sequence. Scored on: visual-first templates, list segmentation by specialty, and date-based automation around the sale calendar.
  3. The bidder-segmentation engine. The estate-jewelry buyer and the farm-equipment buyer share nothing except your list. One unsegmented blast ("This week's lots: a Rolex, a tractor, and a Chippendale dresser") trains both to stop opening. Scored on: multi-list management, interest tagging, behavioral segments (bid history, watchlist), and the discipline of never blasting the whole house.
  4. The sale-day + absentee ladder. Bidding now happens on LiveAuctioneers and Invaluable as much as in the room — platforms that rent you their audience. The owned list is what registers bidders on YOUR terms: the absentee-bid signup sequence, the "registered to bid" confirmations, the lot-alert texts. Scored on: event-triggered automation, registration integrations, and SMS for the final hours.
  5. The underbidder second-chance machine. Every sale produces three things: sold lots, unsold lots, and underbidders — and underbidders are the warmest leads in the building. The passed-lot follow-up ("lot 412 didn't meet reserve — it's yours at $X"), the underbidder nurture, the price-realized newsletter that sells the NEXT consignment. Scored on: post-sale automation, quick turnaround sequences, and analytics that prove list value to consignors.

The ranking

1. MailerLite — the value pick with the visual templates auction email needs

Best for: regional houses running the lot-preview engine and the consignment newsletter on one honest tool.

Auction email is photography email — the lot-preview lives or dies on the hero image, and MailerLite's templates make a 40-lot preview email look like a Sotheby's catalog instead of a classifieds page. The automation builder handles the whole sale calendar: the 14-day lot-preview drip, the specialist-segment previews, the underbidder follow-up after the hammer falls. Pricing stays gentle at auction-house list sizes: Comfort at 500 subscribers is $12/mo (5,000 sends, the 10x send cap), 1,000 subs $19/mo, 2,500 subs $33/mo, 5,000 subs $49/mo, and the free tier (250 subs / 2,500 emails) genuinely covers a small house's consignor list while it grows the bidder file. The honest catch: the pre-sale week is a burst — three preview emails plus the sale-day reminder to 4,000 bidders can trip the 10x Comfort send cap, because auction lists get emailed in pulses, not drips. Price the tool at sale-week send volume; Power (from $25/mo, removes the cap) exists for auction season.

2. GetResponse — the automation-depth pick for the segmentation machine

Best for: houses that run multiple specialties and need the segmentation to actually work.

Starter at $19/mo (sender) or Marketer/Creator at $59–$69/mo (platform). This is the pick when the house sells estate jewelry AND farm equipment AND fine art — the three-lane segmentation where MailerLite's tagging gets clunky and GetResponse's automation builder shines. Multi-branch sequences: jewelry bidders get the jewelry preview, equipment buyers get the equipment preview, and the underbidder from the March sale gets the passed-lot offer in April, all conditional on bid history. The honest demerit: the webinar and funnel machinery you're paying platform prices for is dead weight here — you're not hosting webinars for bidders. If the segmentation is genuinely multi-lane, the automation half earns the price; if the whole program is one preview email per sale, MailerLite wins on price.

3. Brevo — the email-plus-SMS pick for sale-day logistics

Best for: houses that want the lot-alert texts and the bidder logistics in one place.

The last 24 hours before a sale are a text-message business: "Lot 88 closes in 2 hours," the outbid alert that drives the final flurry, the absentee-bidder confirmation that their bid is in. Brevo bundles email and SMS, prices email on volume (friendly for the pulsing send pattern of auction weeks), and the CRM-lite keeps the consignor pipeline visible — which estate is in conversation, which dealer is due a check-in. Honest check: SMS is the scalpel, not the channel — the lot-preview stays email, and every marketing text needs real consent. Use text for outbid alerts, sale-day reminders, and passed-lot offers; never for the catalog itself.

4. Kit — the simplest path to a preview newsletter that actually ships

Best for: small houses whose whole program is the sale preview and a price-realized recap.

Free to 10,000 subscribers at $0, then the bill jumps hard ($139/mo past 10,000) — but a regional house's bidder file is a few thousand names, not tens of thousands, so the free tier is effectively permanent. The trade: Kit's automations are thinner than MailerLite's and much thinner than GetResponse's — the multi-specialty segmentation and the underbidder-branching get clunky fast, and the templates are text-first in a photo-first business. If the business is "preview email + results recap," Kit is the cheapest way to look professional doing it. If the machinery is genuinely segmented, the ceiling arrives fast.

5. AWeber — the reliable old guard, priced like it

Best for: houses that want a known quantity and will pay for it.

Long track record, real deliverability reputation — and in a business where the sale-preview email landing in spam means an empty room on Saturday, deliverability counts. But the plan structure is built for bigger consumer lists than a regional house runs, and the automation builder lags the top two for the segmentation and underbidder work. Fine if you inherit it; not the pick if you're choosing fresh in 2026.

6. Flodesk — the design pick (editorial mention, no affiliate program)

Best for: the lot-preview — nothing on this list makes a hero-lot email prettier.

In a business sold on photographs, Flodesk's templates are the prettiest money can buy, and pretty has real revenue value when you're courting estate attorneys and high-end consignors. But it's the template trap, exactly like the one this site named in the Flodesk-vs-MailerLite piece: the automation is too shallow for the segmented preview engine, the underbidder machine, or the multi-sale calendar. If the entire email program is one gorgeous preview email per sale and the office handles everything else by hand, it's defensible. For the full bidder-list engine, it isn't a contender. The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The consignment-trust sequence. The consignor funnel is a 6-to-18-month trust build, and the price-realized newsletter is the top of it. Every sale, publish the results email: the highlights, the records, the categories that over-performed — addressed to the whole list, but written for the estate attorney and the executor reading over a bidder's shoulder. Then the consignor nurture: the "how we sold the Miller estate" case study, the specialist-intro email, the seasonal "now consigning for the spring sale" window. Consignments are won on proof of liquidity, and the email list IS the proof. Estimate: our operator-pattern estimate is that houses publishing price-realized recaps consistently get warmer consignment inquiries than houses that go silent between sales — treat it as pattern, not a study.
  2. The lot-preview drip (the email IS the catalog). Fourteen days out: the hero-lot announcement (three to five lots, big photos, estimates). Seven days out: the specialist preview by segment — jewelry bidders see jewelry, equipment buyers see equipment. Three days out: the "no-reserve and sleeper lots" email (the lots with no bids yet are the treasure-hunt hook). Sale morning: the "bidding opens / closes tonight" reminder. This is the catalog now; the printed one is a souvenir.
  3. The underbidder second-chance machine. The hammer falls and every sale produces the warmest leads in the building: the underbidders and the passed lots. Within 48 hours: the passed-lot offer email to everyone who watched or bid on the lot ("lot 412 didn't meet reserve — available at $X"). Within a week: the underbidder nurture ("you were second on the Rolex — three more watches in the June sale"). Unsold inventory that sits in the warehouse is depreciation; the same inventory in an underbidder's inbox is revenue.
  4. The sale-day ladder. The registration sequence: "absentee bidding is open" (two weeks out), the how-to-bid email for first-timers (registration is the conversion event — a confused registrant is a lost bidder), the sale-day SMS ("lot 88 closes in 2 hours") for the opted-in. Then the results email within 24 hours: sell-through rate, highlights, and — this is the loop closing — the consignor-proof that feeds campaign #1.
  5. The lapsed-bidder win-back. Bidders go quiet: the collector who bought in 2023 and hasn't registered since. The 12-month win-back: "we haven't seen you at a sale lately — here's what's coming that fits your history." The 24-month version gets the personal note from the specialist. A bidder file that only grows and never reactivates is a list of strangers; the reactivation email is cheaper than acquiring a new bidder on a platform that charges you for the privilege.

The honest catches

  • The list is the liquidity — and liquidity is the product. An auction house with a dead list has an empty room, and an empty room kills consignments: no estate attorney consigns to a house that can't fill seats. Every email decision is downstream of that. The platforms (LiveAuctioneers, Invaluable) rent you bidders for a fee; the owned list is the only audience nobody can take away or reprice.
  • Segmentation isn't optional; it's the whole game. The farm-equipment buyer and the fine-art collector share nothing but your database. One unsegmented blast trains both to stop opening — and an unopened list is a dead room. Tag by specialty at registration, segment by bid history automatically, and never send the whole house the same email.
  • Every email is perishable. A lot-preview email dies at the hammer — unlike a product catalog, the inventory is one-of-one and gone. That means the email calendar is the sale calendar: miss the preview window and there's nothing to send. Build the templates once, then run the machine on the calendar, sale after sale.
  • The consignor is on the list too. The estate family, the dealer, the executor — they're reading the same emails as the bidders. The price-realized email that celebrates a record is consignor marketing; the passed-lot email that discounts their unsold lot is a relationship event. Write every campaign knowing both sides are watching.
  • Photos are the email; the tool isn't. No email platform fixes bad lot photography. The hero-lot email with a blurry phone photo of a diamond ring underperforms the plain-text email with a great one. Spend the photography money first; the email tool second. And disclose the buyer's premium in the preview emails (the industry-standard 15–25% range at mid-market houses) — it's the trust issue that costs houses bidders when it's buried in the terms page.

FAQ

How often should an auction house email its list?

On the sale calendar, not a fixed schedule: the 14-day preview drip, the 7-day specialist preview, the 3-day sleeper-lot email, the sale-day reminder, and the results email. A house running monthly sales emails roughly weekly during the sale window and goes quieter between — the consignment-trust newsletter fills the gaps. The wrong cadence is "monthly newsletter regardless of sales"; the right one is "the sale sets the rhythm."

Should we email about unsold lots?

Yes — that's the second-chance machine, and it's pure margin. The passed-lot offer email within 48 hours goes to watchers and underbidders of that lot. Unsold lots in the warehouse cost you space and consignor patience; the same lots in a targeted email cost you one send. This is the highest-ROI email most houses never send.

LiveAuctioneers and Invaluable already bring bidders — why build our own list?

Because platforms rent you their audience on their terms and their fees. Your list is the only bidder file you own, the only channel where the preview email costs you nothing per send, and the only asset that compounds: every sale's bidders become the next sale's preview audience. The platforms are distribution; the list is the business.

How does email actually win consignments?

The price-realized newsletter is the consignor funnel. Every results email is read by estate attorneys, executors, and dealers judging whether your room is liquid enough for their inventory. Publish the sell-through, the records, the category strength — consistently, sale after sale. The consignor inquiry that arrives in March was won by the results emails of September.

Do we need SMS, or is email enough?

Email carries the program; SMS carries the final hours. Outbid alerts, sale-day lot-closing reminders, and passed-lot offers are text jobs — they're read in minutes, and minutes are what the last hour of a sale runs on. The preview and the results stay email. If the tool bundles both (Brevo does), you get the ladder in one place.
