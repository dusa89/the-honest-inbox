What a medspa actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five engines that fill chairs:

  1. The treatment-cycle rebook calendar. This is the engine a salon does not have. Botox lasts 3–4 months; hyaluronic-acid fillers last 6–18 months (per 2026 medspa and aesthetics guides). Every injectable patient therefore has a known rebook date — and the email program's job is to be there a few weeks before it. The sequence: the month-3 Botox reminder ("your results are fading — book this week's touch-up slot"), the month-10 filler check-in, the annual treatment-plan review. Each one is per-patient, date-triggered, and automated. The medspa that runs this on time never has an empty chair problem from lapsed injectables.
  2. The post-treatment aftercare sequence. Aftercare is where reviews are born. The day-after email with the care instructions (what's normal, what isn't, when to call), the week-2 check-in ("how are the results settling?"), the results-peak email with the review ask and the photo-consent request. Aesthetics runs on before-and-afters — and before-and-afters need signed photo consent, which this sequence collects. Nobody asks for the testimonial in the lobby; the automated sequence asks it at the moment the patient loves the result.
  3. The membership continuity engine. Medspa memberships ($100–$300/month is the typical band — estimate) are the revenue smoothing this industry runs on: a monthly facial or credit, member pricing on injectables, priority booking. Email's job: the welcome sequence that sells the membership to first-time patients, the monthly "your credit is waiting" reminder (unused credits are retention risk), the renewal/upgrade drip, and the anniversary offer. Members rebook themselves; the email program just makes sure they remember why they're members.
  4. The new-treatment launch. Launches are a medspa's acquisition events — the new laser, the new skin-tightening device, the celebrity-backed facial. The sequence: the teaser to the list, the early-access waitlist (members first), the launch-week booking window with the intro pricing, the post-launch social-proof wave (first results, first reviews). A launch without an email sequence is a launch that fills whoever happened to walk in that week. The email tool's launch calendar is the difference.
  5. The review + referral ask. Aesthetics is a visual industry bought on trust: Google reviews, Instagram before/afters, and word of mouth from the friend whose skin looks great. The sequence: the results-peak review ask (week 2–3 post-treatment), the before/after photo consent collection (the marketing asset that pays for years), the referral ask ("who's been asking about your skin?"), and the quarterly "bring a friend" event. Reviews feed the map pack; photos feed the feed; referrals feed the calendar.

Notice what's missing: no blast calendars, no AI subject-line generators, no lead scoring. The booking stack (Zenoti, Vagaro, Mangomint — the established names) already holds the schedule, the reminders, the intake forms, and the patient record. The email tool only has to hold the patient and prospect lists, send the calendar campaigns, and run the rebook–aftercare–membership–launch–ask automations. Paying for a second appointment system is the trap.

The HIPAA trap: using health information for marketing needs written authorization. Under HIPAA's marketing rules (45 CFR §164.508(a)(3)), a covered entity needs the individual's written authorization to use protected health information for marketing — with narrow exceptions (face-to-face communication, gifts of nominal value). Treatment, payment, and operations messages — appointment reminders, recall notices, descriptions of your own services — are not marketing under the rule and don't need that authorization. But the moment you segment by treatment or diagnosis to send a promotion — "our Botox patients get 20% off lip filler this month" — you have crossed the line: that is marketing use of PHI, and it needs the written authorization. The practical rule, verified against current compliance guides: keep diagnoses, treatments, medications, and results out of marketing email entirely; segment by non-clinical attributes — location, patient status, self-selected interest — instead. (This is a summary of public rules, not legal advice; your compliance counsel governs the send.)

Most email vendors won't sign a BAA — so keep PHI off the platform entirely. Compliance vendors are explicit about this: most email marketing vendors won't sign a business associate agreement, and some that do still forbid transmitting PHI on the platform. The working rule is simple and safe: the marketing email tool never sees health information. Names and email addresses ride the list; everything clinical stays in the EHR. If your campaigns are built that way, the vendor question is moot.
