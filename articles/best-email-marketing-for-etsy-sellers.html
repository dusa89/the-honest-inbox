The thing nobody tells Etsy sellers: you don't own your buyers

Read Etsy's own seller handbook and the rule is explicit: even when you get a buyer's email address through Etsy to fulfill an order, you must get their express consent before sending marketing messages. Consent means a clear affirmative action — the buyer agreeing in a message or email to receive marketing from you. Buying a thing from your shop is not consent to be marketed to. And sending unsolicited advertising through Etsy Messages is flat-out prohibited by Etsy's house rules.

This is the opposite of every other vertical on this site. A restaurant captures emails on its own Wi-Fi splash page — those are the restaurant's. A coach's lead magnet list is the coach's. Your Etsy order list belongs to the relationship between you, the buyer, and Etsy — and Etsy's platform, Etsy's rules. So the email tool decision for an Etsy seller is really a capture decision: your tool has to be excellent at landing pages, QR-code-ready forms, and lead magnets, because you are building the list from zero, off the platform, one package insert at a time.

What Etsy sellers actually need (and don't)

Etsy lists are small. A typical working Etsy seller's email list runs 500–2,500 contacts (our estimate — sellers building from package inserts and social, not from order data they can't use). You do not need the 25,000-contact band. Price at 1,000 and 2,500; anything else is shopping for someone else's business.

What matters: landing pages and forms that work as QR-code destinations, a generous free tier (you're validating, not scaling), automations for the three flows that pay for the subscription, digital product sales if you sell downloads, and tagging that separates buyers from lookers.

What doesn't matter much: advanced CRM scoring, webinar suites, e-commerce carts for physical goods (Etsy already checks out your physical products — your email tool sells the digital ones), and multi-user seats. One person, one shop.

The capture playbook: this is where the game is won

The tool is secondary. The capture system is the business. Four capture channels that work for Etsy sellers, in order of effort-to-return:

  1. Package inserts with QR codes. A printed card in every order: a discount on the next purchase in exchange for the email. This is the highest-converting channel you have — the buyer just trusted you with money. Put the QR code on the card, not a URL (our estimate — nobody types a URL from a packing card). One tool note: the QR destination must be a hosted landing page, which is why landing pages matter more than broadcast features for you.
  2. Lead magnets for digital products. Sell planners? The free sample month. Sell SVGs? Five free designs. Sell jewelry? The care-and-styling guide. The freebie is the list; the shop is the store.
  3. Social bio links. Instagram and TikTok bios point at a landing page, not the Etsy shop. Your social followers convert to email at a fraction of the package-insert rate (our estimate), but they compound while you sleep.
  4. Shop thank-you messages (consent only). You can ask a buyer in an Etsy Message whether they'd like marketing emails — that clear affirmative answer is exactly the "express consent" the handbook requires. Convert the yes into a signup on your landing page, not a manual add. Never add buyers without the yes.

The tool that wins this vertical is the one with the best landing pages and forms per dollar — which is why Kit's free plan (unlimited landing pages and forms) leads the table.

Kit's free Newsletter plan is the single best free tier in email marketing for an Etsy seller: 10,000 subscribers, unlimited broadcasts, unlimited landing pages and forms, tagging, segmentation — and digital product sales — for $0/month. The one-automation limit is the ceiling, but for a 1,000-contact Etsy list running one welcome sequence, the free plan is the whole product.

The digital-product angle is the real story. Kit Commerce lets you sell digital products directly — Stripe-backed checkout, weekly payouts — at 3.5% + $0.30 per transaction, all-inclusive of card processing (per Kit's own published terms), on every plan including the free one. Compare that to Etsy's published fee stack: 6.5% transaction fee plus ~3% + $0.25 payment processing on every sale. A seller moving a $12 printable off Etsy through Kit keeps roughly $11.28 instead of ~$10.86 (our math from the published fee figures — payment-processing edge cases vary). The pattern working sellers use: Etsy for discovery, Kit Commerce for the digital repeat sales, email for the whole funnel. The email tool becomes the storefront.

The honesty check: the paid jump is real — Kit Creator is $39/mo at the 1,000-subscriber band, roughly double MailerLite Comfort for comparable automation. And Kit's fee math has a gotcha at low prices: the flat $0.30 per transaction is over 4% on its own for a $7 product (per independent fee analysis). Price your products above the $0.30 line or the fee eats the margin.
Who it fits: digital product sellers (printables, planners, SVGs, presets, templates) — especially anyone who wants to sell the same digital products off Etsy without a second platform.

The three automations that pay for the subscription

Run exactly these three and the tool pays for itself:

  1. Post-purchase review sequence. Etsy runs on reviews — a review request 10–14 days after delivery (tagged from your shipping records, consent-confirmed) is the highest-leverage email an Etsy seller sends. Reviews lift conversion on every future listing, not just the one sold.
  2. Repeat-buyer win-back (60–90 days). Tag what they bought; at 60–90 days send the complementary product. Jewelry buyers get the matching piece; planner buyers get next year's edition; SVG buyers get the new bundle. Repeat buyers are the cheapest revenue you have (our estimate — reacquired buyers consistently outperform cold list segments on cost).
  3. Digital freebie nurture. Free sample → 3-email nurture → paid product pitch. For digital sellers this is the whole machine, and it's the one automation Kit's free plan gives you — which is why the free plan is enough for so many digital shops.

Every paid tool on this list runs all three natively. The differentiator is not capability — it's whether the free tier lets you start them at $0.

Billing traps, Etsy edition

  • The consent trap — adding past buyers to your list without their express consent violates Etsy's policies and anti-spam law in many countries. The package insert asks; the landing page collects; the list stays clean.
  • Etsy fees vs. Kit Commerce fees — 6.5% + ~3% + $0.25 per Etsy sale against Kit's 3.5% + $0.30 all-in. But the $0.30 flat fee bites hardest on cheap products: over 4% on a $7 sale before the percentage. Price accordingly.
  • Free plans that aren't — MailerLite's 250-subscriber cap and Mailchimp's 250-contact cap (500 emails/month) are trials, not tiers. Kit's 10,000-subscriber free plan is the only real free tier in this vertical.
  • One-automation ceilings — Kit free and GetResponse Starter both cap you at one automation workflow. That's the welcome sequence; the review flow and the win-back flow need the paid tier. Plan the upgrade into the budget from day one.
  • Counting unsubscribed contacts — several tools bill contacts, not active subscribers. A list built from package inserts decays fast; clean bounces and unsubscribes quarterly or you pay for ghosts.
