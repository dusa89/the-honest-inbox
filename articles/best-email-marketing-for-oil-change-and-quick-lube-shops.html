A quick-lube shop does not sell oil changes the way a store sells products — it sells a calendar. Every car that rolls out of the bay comes back on a date you already know: 3,000 miles later for the old schedule, 7,500 to 10,000 for most modern cars (the old 3k–5k rule is fading — J.D. Power's 2026 coverage names the new interval). The US oil change market is worth about $6.8 billion in 2025, heading toward $11.31 billion by 2030 at 5.9% CAGR — and the business model is recurrence without a contract: nobody signs up for a subscription, but every car has a due date. The honest catches

Your POS already does transactional — let it. The shop's point-of-sale already handles invoices, appointment confirmations, and pickup notices. The email tool on this page is the marketing layer: the recall calendar, the sticker twin, the upsell ladder, the fleet lane. Duplicating transactional mail in your marketing tool is how customers start ignoring both — and how you pay for two tools doing one job.

The interval is per-car, not per-customer. A two-car household has two calendars — the truck on the 5k schedule and the sedan on the 10k. If your recall system can't split by vehicle, it will remind the wrong car at the wrong time and train the customer to ignore the right ones. Capture the odometer and the VIN-level vehicle at checkout; the email tool should key on the car, not the inbox.

Don't let the reminder become the whole relationship. A pure recall machine trains customers to see you as an alarm clock. The education drip (synthetic vs conventional, the filter science, the 15-minute inspection walkthrough) is what turns the alarm into a trusted shop — and trust is what the upsell ladder and the reviews feed on.

The dealer is not the enemy; the driveway is. Ducker Carlisle's 2026 numbers show quick lubes gaining on dealers structurally — $5 to $15 cheaper per change, more predictable. The customer you're actually losing is the one doing it themselves or stretching intervals into deferral. The reactivation campaign beats the conquest campaign — market to your own dormant list before you chase the dealer's.

Capture at the work order, not after. The counter is the capture moment: "we'll email you when it's due" at checkout, with the odometer reading written down. The service ticket is the list — if you don't capture the email with the mileage, the recall calendar has nobody to remind.

FAQ

How often should I remind customers? The reminder cadence follows the car, not the calendar: 30 days before the due date, 7 days before with a booking link, the day before by text, and a rescue at +30 days overdue. Four touches per cycle — persistent but never spammy. Anything more and the reminder becomes noise.

Does email work for a walk-in business? Yes — because the reminder converts walk-in customers into planned visits. The bay fills on your schedule instead of the weather's. The shops that win are the ones that book the week ahead, and the recall machine is how a walk-in business gets a book-ahead calendar.

Should I email about the upsells? Yes, but as education, not pressure. The declined-work follow-up ("the filter you passed on in March") converts at the highest rate of any campaign here — because the advisor already made the case in person. The email just remembers it. In-person pitch plus software memory is the combination that moves the ticket.

How do I collect emails at the shop? At the work order: "we'll email you when it's due" — with the odometer reading captured next to the address. One good Saturday adds dozens of names. The service ticket is the highest-traffic capture point in the business.

Email or text for reminders? Both, split by stakes: the 30-day and 7-day reminders belong in the inbox (room for the education and the booking link), while the day-before and the missed-due nudge belong in the pocket (the text). Brevo's SMS lane exists for exactly this. Match the channel to the stakes.
