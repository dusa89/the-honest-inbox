A coworking space sells tours that become members, and members who stay — and the industry's numbers tell you exactly where the money leaks. There are roughly 41,000 coworking spaces worldwide now (up from ~20,000 in 2020), with 9,136 active locations in the US alone at the end of Q1 2026, in a market valued around $29 billion heading past $35 billion by 2028. The average space sits at about 68% occupancy (global 2025 data), a healthy one keeps 65-85% of its plan-based members year over year, and just 54% of coworking businesses were profitable over the previous 12 months. The bottleneck is brutally concrete: a representative mid-market operator converts ~25% of inquiries into tours, takes a median 6 hours to respond, gets ~40% of inquiries outside business hours, and never answers ~15% at all (OfficeRnD's lead-decay benchmarks). Email is this industry's margin machinery — the instant tour-booking nurture that catches the after-hours inquiry, the day-pass ladder that converts a $31 visitor into a $220/mo member, and the win-back that recovers a canceled desk before the empty chair costs you the month. Here is how the best tool runs it.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The tour-to-member engine. The tour is the conversion event — everything else on this page is a tributary to it. The machinery: instant tour confirmation (the inquiry that gets a 6-hour wait and the inquiry that gets a 6-minute answer are two different businesses — OfficeRnD's numbers put the no-response rate at ~15% and lead-to-tour at ~25% for a well-run shop, trending toward 30%+ with instant after-hours response), the pre-tour reminder sequence (clear "Book a tour" CTAs, a real calendar with slots, automatic confirmations — ezeep's member-journey work flags exactly this as what reduces no-shows), the tour-day preparation email (parking, where to walk in, who you'll meet), and the post-tour follow-up within 24 hours. A booked-but-no-show tour is the most expensive lead you pay for twice. Scored on the tools: trigger speed (minutes, not hours), calendar/reminder automations, and follow-up sequences that fire without a staffer remembering.
  2. The day-pass-to-desk ladder. The day pass is the top of your funnel — a person's first experience of your brand, your team, your location (and some of them are just visiting relatives who will tell the whole family). The math that drives the ladder: a monthly membership costs about the same as 6.5 day passes nationally, so the break-even is roughly 1.5 office days per week — in other words, the person buying two day passes a week is overpaying by more than half a membership. The machinery: capture every day-pass email at booking, the "you spent $62 this month — a desk is $220" nudge, the first-date hospitality the allwork conversion playbook describes (tour them mid-day-pass, introduce them to a member, make an offer before they leave, log what you learned), and the 3-pack-of-day-passes offer that creates the habit the membership then captures. Scored on the tools: contact capture at booking, purchase-triggered sequences, and offer-code delivery.
  3. The community-event calendar. Coworking's differentiator against the private-office-first players is the room where members meet each other — the events that make a $300 hot desk feel like a network. The machinery: the monthly events email (wellness, founder breakfasts, happy hours, lunch-and-learns), event reminders with RSVP, the post-event recap with photos that markets the next event, member-spotlight emails that sell community to prospects reading them. Events are also the day-pass and lapsed-member re-entry point: the free Tuesday breakfast that brings last quarter's canceled member back through the door. Scored on the tools: recurring calendar automations, RSVP integrations, and templates that make photos look good on phones (everyone reads these in the elevator).
  4. The churn win-back engine. Average occupancy is ~68%; the healthy retention benchmark is 65-85% of plan members year over year; the planning assumption one financial model uses is 4% monthly churn. When a member leaves, the cost to refill that seat includes advertising, sales time, and onboarding — a space at 90% occupancy with high monthly turnover often loses to a space at 70% with long-tenured members (optixapp's honest framing). The machinery: the cancellation-exit capture (why are you leaving — downsize, move, remote, price?), the pause offer (3 months at half to hold the community access, because paused beats gone), the 30/60/90-day win-back drip ("your desk misses you" with a genuinely good offer), the upgrade counter-offer at exit (hot-desk member leaving for price? the dedicated-desk-in-quiet-corner re-pitch). Scored on the tools: cancellation-triggered sequences, branching by exit reason, and offer-code delivery.
  5. The private-office upgrade path. Here is the revenue reality the global numbers force on you: private offices average 67.12% of coworking revenue globally (the UK runs 89.63%); coworking memberships are just 6.54%. The money in this business is not the $220/mo hot desk — it is the 3-person startup that graduates to the 6-person office, then the 9-seat team room. Small businesses drove most of WeWork's 2026 growth, expanding footprints 14.8% from 7.8 to 9 seats; 8 in 10 member companies maintained or expanded. The machinery: the team-size signals (the 2-desk member adding a third laptop), the "your team's outgrowing the open floor" nurture at month 4-6, the private-office vacancy alert list (sold-out offices go to the waitlist, not the public site), the flex-lease education drip (16.5-month average flex terms in 2025 — the longest on record; sell the term, not the desk). Scored on the tools: behavioral segmentation, long-drip automations, and a waitlist machine.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first — in this order:

  1. The instant tour-booking nurture. Trigger: tour request or day-pass booking. Sequence: instant confirmation with the practical details (parking, entrance, who you're meeting — the inquiry that waits 6 hours is a different business), the day-before reminder, the day-of morning text-eligible reminder, the post-tour "what did you think?" within 24 hours, the 7-day "the desk we showed you is still open" close. Capture team size and timeline at booking (the OfficeRnD playbook's qualification pair) so the follow-up pitches the right product. This is the highest-ROI sequence on this page — every point of lead-to-tour conversion it recovers is revenue the 15% no-response rate was donating to the competitor.
  2. The day-pass-to-desk ladder. Trigger: day-pass purchase. Sequence: the welcome email before arrival (this is where the visit gets great), the mid-visit tour offer ("want a 10-minute look at the dedicated desks while you're here?"), the same-week "you came Tuesday and Thursday — a membership is cheaper" math email (the 6.5-day-pass break-even, personalized with their actual spend), the 3-pack offer at 30 days for the repeat buyer. Log what you learned about them at each visit — the remembered detail ("last time you said you hate the morning noise") is what the allwork playbook calls the 7-to-10 move.
  3. The community-event machine. Trigger: monthly cadence + event RSVP. Sequence: the first-of-month events email, the 3-day reminder with RSVP, the day-after photo recap that markets the next one, the member-spotlight series (sold to prospects as "this is who works here"). Open every event to day-pass buyers and lapsed members on the invite list — the free breakfast is the cheapest re-acquisition channel you have.
  4. The churn win-back drip. Trigger: cancellation request. Sequence: the exit interview (exit reason branches everything), the pause counter-offer (3 months at half-rate community access beats a cold seat), the 30/60/90-day "your people are still here" win-back with escalating offers, the team-growth check for former startup members ("up to 4 people? the office is $X now"). Measure it against the 4% monthly churn planning baseline — the win-back exists to keep you under it.
  5. The private-office upgrade path. Trigger: behavioral (member adds teammates, books meeting rooms weekly). Sequence: the month-4 "your team's growing" check-in, the flex-term education drip (the 16.5-month average term story — sell commitment, not desks), the office-vacancy alert list, the tour-booking engine again for the upgraded space (the tour machinery is the same machine, selling a bigger product). With private offices averaging two-thirds of global coworking revenue, this is where the P&L actually turns.

The honest catches

Speed is the whole game on tours. The tour-inquiry that sits 6 hours is competing with the space that answers in 6 minutes. Whatever tool you pick, the confirmation must fire on the booking trigger — not on the "when the community manager checks the inbox" schedule. If your tool can't send in minutes, it can't do this job.

Members, prospects, and day-passers are three different lists. The member gets the community email; the prospect gets the tour nurture; the day-passer gets the ladder. Cross the streams and the member tunes out, the prospect gets confused, and the day-passer unsubscribes before they ever buy the 3-pack. Segment by relationship, permanently.

The email tool is the comms layer, not the booking system. Cobot, OfficeRnD, Nexudus and the rest hold the truth — bookings, desk assignments, billing. Don't fight that; wire it. The tour confirmation fires off the booking; the day-pass ladder fires off the purchase; the win-back fires off the cancellation. Email that pretends to be the database is where double-bookings and dead links are born.

Don't judge the program on a quiet month. The 6.5-day-pass break-even means summer travel season and December will always soften conversion. The ladder built in the slow months is the one that converts the September surge. And the private-office pipeline is a 16-month game — the nurture that starts when the team is 2 desks is the one that closes when they're 9.

Occupancy is a vanity metric; the win-back is the business. A space at 90% with high monthly churn is refilling its seats at full acquisition cost every month; a space at 70% with long-tenured members keeps the revenue it already won. The 68% average and the 65-85% retention benchmark tell you where to look — at who's leaving, not just who's arriving.

FAQ

At what point is a membership cheaper than day passes? Nationally, a monthly membership costs about the same as 6.5 day passes — so roughly 1.5 office days per week is the break-even. That's the honest sales math, and it should be in the ladder email, personalized with their actual spend.

Email or SMS for tour reminders? Both, with a hard line: SMS for time-critical logistics (tour confirmation, "running 10 minutes late," facility closures), email for everything else. Promotional SMS to members is off the table; operational SMS is where the channel earns its keep.

How big a list does a typical coworking space actually have? Think in four pools: active members, day-pass buyers, tour prospects (most of whom didn't book — the nurtured 75%), and event attendees. A 100-member space with healthy capture can sit on 400-800 contacts without ever buying a list (this is an estimate, not a measurement) — comfortably inside the cheap tiers.

Will email marketing actually reduce our churn? It recovers tours and re-acquires leavers — which is most of what "lower churn" means operationally. It doesn't fix a bad location or above-market pricing (the 68% average is a market, not a marketing failure). What it does is win you the prospects that are shopping and keep the members you have.

Do we need a separate tool from our booking system? No — you need a separate comms layer that talks to it. Cobot and OfficeRnD run the space; the email tool runs the nurture. The integration is the product: booking fires the tour flow, purchase fires the ladder, cancellation fires the win-back.
