A trampoline park does not sell jump time — it sells the kid's birthday. Birthday parties are commonly 30-40% of revenue at a well-run park (per Sky Zone's own franchise disclosure data, analyzed September 2026), and the industry's own KPI benchmarks target a 12% party conversion rate — 600 parties out of 50,000 general-admission visits (financialmodelslab.com). The global park market is running about $1.4 billion in 2026 on the way to $3.5 billion by 2033 (market.us), with 6,500+ parks operating worldwide. The park that owns the birthday calendar owns the business — and the machinery that runs it is all email: the birthday-party engine (the inquiry-to-booking sequence on $230-$420 ten-jumper packages), the Friday-night glow engine (the weekly teen fill), the membership engine (the regular's autopilot), the camp-week calendar (school breaks as revenue seasons), and the waiver-list funnel (every signed waiver is a contact). Here is how the best tool for the park runs them.

What this guide scores

Every park guide on this site is scored against the park's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The birthday-party engine. The core of the business. Sky Zone's Omaha location (party page crawled October 2026) shows the standard shape: three tiers — Basic $229.99, Epic $319.99, Mega VIP $419.99 — for 10 jumpers, 60 minutes of jump time plus 40 minutes in the room, additional jumpers $17.99-$23.99, a 10-jumper minimum, Monday-Thursday parties saving $20. The machinery: the party-inquiry drip (the parent who priced a party but didn't book is the warmest lead in the building — the follow-up sequence that converts the quote), the book-ahead calendar (parties get booked weeks out — the email that fills the slow weekends first), the party-day capture (every party guest signs a waiver, which means every party is a prospect list with a birthday coming — the post-party "host your party here" email to the guests' parents), the rebook-a-year-later sequence (the kid's next birthday is 12 months away and the park already knows the date — the anniversary email no other venue sends), and the tier-upgrade pitch (the parent who booked Basic last year gets the Epic upsell this year, because the photos from last year's party are doing the selling). Scored on the tools: can it run date-triggered sequences and segment by party history?
  2. The Friday-night glow engine. The weekly fill. Teen nights, glow parties, the after-8pm crowd — the recurring event that turns a quiet Friday into the week's best night. The machinery: the week-ahead broadcast (the "this Friday" email to the teen list), the bring-a-friend ladder (the regular who brings two friends is cheaper than any ad), the theme-night rotation (the event that gives regulars a reason to come back instead of assuming "it's the same as last week"), and the no-show reactivation (the Friday regular who missed two weeks gets the check-in — not a discount, a nudge). Scored on the tools: can it run a weekly broadcast to a teen segment without manual work?
  3. The membership engine. The regular's autopilot. General admission runs $20-$35 per jump session; memberships run $30-$100/month (per an older sleeklens.com roundup — labeled as such). The machinery: the break-even pitch (the parent whose kid comes twice a month is already losing money on per-session tickets — the email that shows the math), the renewal calendar (the membership that lapses in 60 days gets the re-enrollment sequence), the membership-as-gift push (the holiday-season email that turns a grandparent into a member buyer), and the lapsed-member win-back (the kid who stopped coming is a cancellation with a known address — the "we miss you" sequence with the comeback offer). Scored on the tools: can it segment members from per-session buyers and run the renewal runway?
  4. The camp-week calendar. School breaks are revenue seasons. Summer break, spring break, winter break — the camp-week engine sells the break before the break. The machinery: the pre-break launch (the "summer camp weeks are open" email, weeks before school lets out — the park that emails first fills first), the early-bird pricing push (the break price the parent locks in May vs. the price they pay in June — the discount with a deadline moves parents), the multi-week ladder (the family that booked one week gets the second-week pitch — the break is long and the pool of bored kids is deep), and the camp-to-membership bridge (the camp kid is the park's warmest membership prospect — the end-of-week email that converts the summer into the year). Scored on the tools: can it run a date-triggered seasonal campaign with early-bird segmentation?
  5. The waiver-list funnel. The warmest list in the business. Every jumper signs a waiver and gets a wristband (per a parent's October 2026 account of the process) — which means every visit produces a contact, and every party produces ten. The machinery: the post-visit welcome (the first-timer's email — the park they just visited, in their inbox, the same evening), the review ask at the door's memory (the five-star review goes out while the kids are still bouncing in the car), the guest-parent capture (the party guest's parent signed a waiver too — the "your kid had a blast at Maya's party" email is the highest-converting acquisition email in the business), and the dormant-waiver reactivation (the family that visited once, six months ago, is a visit waiting to happen — the dormant list is the cheapest revenue the park owns). Scored on the tools: can it capture and segment by visit and party history, and is it cheap enough for a park's list size?

Notice what's missing: no generic "monthly newsletter" (your product is the party calendar and the weekly events — the newsletter is the calendar, or it's noise), no discount spam on admission (the ticket is the thing that pays the rent — discounting it trains families to wait for the sale), no platform dependency (the parent list lives in your tool, not in someone's social feed). The birthday engine books the revenue, the Friday engine fills the week, the membership engine autopilots the regulars, the camp engine owns the breaks, and the waiver funnel never stops filling the list. A park whose email is "the monthly newsletter" is running a birthday business on a broadcast calendar.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a trampoline park leaves on the table:

  1. The party-inquiry rescue. Trigger: the priced-but-unbooked inquiry. The parent who priced a party but didn't book is the warmest lead in the building — the 3-send follow-up (the package recap, the available-dates nudge, the "Saturdays in [month] are filling" honest-urgency close). The inquiry that goes cold is a party at someone else's venue — and the benchmark says 12% of GA visits should convert to parties, so the rescue sequence is measured against that bar.
  2. The rebook-a-year-later. Trigger: 11 months after the party. The kid's next birthday is coming and the park already knows the date — the anniversary email no other venue sends ("Maya's party was a blast last October — want the same room this year?"). The park that sends this owns the repeat; the park that doesn't re-buys the customer it already won. This is the single highest-ROI automation on this page.
  3. The guest-parent capture. Trigger: the day after the party. Every party guest signed a waiver — and every guest has a birthday coming. The "your kid had a blast at the party" email to the guests' parents, with the party-booking link, is the highest-converting acquisition email in the business: the parent already saw the venue, the kid is already asking, and the pitch arrives while the memory is fresh.
  4. The Friday-night fill. Trigger: Wednesday on the calendar. The "this Friday" broadcast to the teen list — the glow night, the theme, the bring-a-friend pitch. The recurring event lives or dies on the weekly reminder; the park that emails Wednesday fills Friday, the park that doesn't wonders why Friday was quiet.
  5. The camp-week launch. Trigger: 6 weeks before the break. The "summer camp weeks are open" email with the early-bird deadline — the break price the parent locks early vs. the price they pay late. The school break is a revenue season with a fixed end date; the email that sells it has to arrive before the break, not during it. The family that booked one week gets the second-week pitch — the break is long.

The honest catches

The buyer is not the jumper. This is the park's version of the split-buyer trap, and it shapes everything: the parent books, the kid jumps, and the email lands in the parent's inbox. The content must speak planning and value to the parent — not fun to the child. Segment by the parent's relationship to the park (prospect, party booker, member, lapsed), not by the kid's whims. The park that emails the kid is entertaining a customer; the park that emails the parent is booking one.

The waiver is a contact, not a checkbox. Every visit produces a signed waiver — a name, an email, a kid, a date. The failure mode is treating the waiver as paperwork and the visit as anonymous. The park that funnels every waiver into the list has a contact for every customer it ever served; the park that files the waivers has a list of people it served once and forgot. The waiver-list funnel is the cheapest acquisition channel in the business — it costs nothing, because the customer already came.

The party window is 100 minutes, not the afternoon. Sky Zone's Omaha packages run 60 minutes of jump plus 40 minutes in the room — 100 minutes total — and one parent's October 2026 account puts the overtime charge at $75 per 10 minutes past the window. The operational lesson lands in the email: the party-day sequence (waivers pre-signed at the link, the room-opens time, the parking note) is what keeps the 100 minutes on schedule. The park that emails the party-day logistics runs on time; the park that doesn't pays it in overtime and one-star reviews.

The off-season list decides the season. The camp-week launches reward the park that warmed the list in the months before — and the birthday engine rewards the park that kept emailing between parties. The park that goes dark when it's quiet starts the season with a cold list and a prayer; the park that emails the Friday nights, the member wins, and the waiver-list drips year-round starts the season with an audience that already trusts it.

The discount trap is real. The temptation is to fill slow Tuesdays with coupon blasts. The parent who gets the coupon learns to wait for the coupon — and the ticket is the thing that pays the rent. Fill the slow nights with events (the theme night, the bring-a-friend ladder), not discounts. The discount with a deadline (the early-bird camp price, the Monday-Thursday party savings) moves parents; the standing discount trains them.

The middle-size trap is a business fact, not a marketing one. Sky Zone's own disclosure data shows the dip: parks under 22,500 square feet average $1.79M, the middle tier averages just $1.2M, and the largest parks average $2.93M at 30.4% EBITDA (~September 2026 analysis). The honest version for the email: the park's economics are decided by the size decision and the party program together — and the email list is what fills the party rooms that pay for the square footage, whatever size you chose.
