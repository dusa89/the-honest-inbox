What a solar installer actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five jobs that matter:

  1. The unclosed-quote drip. This is the whole pipeline in solar. A homeowner gets a quote, goes quiet for weeks, compares two competitors, asks the spouse, forgets. The quote you quoted in February gets signed in June — if anyone is still talking to them. The drip runs on quote status: day 3 (the thank-you + one-page quote recap), week 2 (the education email — how the savings math works on their roof), month 2 (the honest nudge), and a seasonal trigger (rate-hike season, heat-wave season). The consideration cycle for a purchase this size is 3–9 months (estimate — industry studies put big home-improvement decisions in the multi-month range); the drip that survives it wins the signature. Discount the system to close it and you just taught the market to wait — educate instead.
  2. The post-25D incentive campaign. The federal picture changed on July 4, 2025: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act ended the 30% residential solar credit (Section 25D) for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025 — it had been scheduled to run at 30% through 2032. Some 2026 quotes in circulation still subtract the 30% credit from homeowner-owned system prices, and that number is wrong for any system bought and installed today (verified via web this run, October 3, 2026). Your install company can still access the federal credit through third-party ownership — leases and PPAs under Section 48E — which means the honest email campaign teaches the pivot: what the homeowner can still claim, what only a lease gets them, and which state incentives fill the gap (New York's 25% state credit up to $5,000, South Carolina's 25% up to $3,500, and similar programs — verify each one live before you quote it, because funding windows move). The installer who educates about the post-credit math owns the consultative sale; the one still quoting the expired credit owns a compliance problem.
  3. The neighborhood referral machine. Solar is a visible purchase on a visible roof, and it spreads house to house. The campaign: after every install, the neighborhood gets the "another one on Elm Street" email — not the customer's name, the street. The installer's own customer becomes the referral engine: the $500-per-signed-referral email, the referral link, the production screenshot a year later ("your system made 11,400 kWh this year") that the customer forwards to the group chat. Route-density math is real in this trade — three installs on one street beats three installs in three towns, and email is how you cluster them.
  4. The battery-and-maintenance add-on. The installed base is a second business most shops ignore. Battery add-ons to existing customers (especially in markets where export compensation fell — California's NEM 3.0 slashed what utilities pay for exported solar, which is why storage is now standard in new California quotes), panel-cleaning and maintenance visits, monitoring alerts with a human reply, the inverter check-in at year five. The add-on email is cheap to send and lands on a customer who already trusts you with their roof — the highest-margin revenue in the shop.
  5. Review moat with production proof. Nobody signs a $30,000 contract from a banner ad — they sign from the installer with 200+ reviews and production screenshots. The review ask goes out when the system is permitted and producing (the moment the customer's app shows the first kilowatt-hours, not the day the crew leaves), with the direct Google review link. Two emails max. In a trade where one bad review costs you five quotes, the review sequence is a moat-builder.

Notice what's missing: no lead scoring, no landing-page builder Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. Your pipeline CRM already handles quotes, permits, and install scheduling; the email tool only has to hold the customer list, send the campaigns, and run the quote-follow-up machine. Paying for a marketing department's toolkit is the trap.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a solar installer leaves on the table:

  1. The unclosed-quote drip. Trigger: quote delivered, no signature. Email 1 (day 3): the thank-you + one-page quote recap in plain English — system size, production estimate, net cost after verified current incentives. Email 2 (week 2): the education email — how the savings math works on their roof specifically, with the EnergySage 2026 benchmarks as context (average 12 kW install ~$31,135 before incentives; average payback ~10 years) so the customer can reality-check your numbers. Email 3 (month 2): the honest nudge — "the quote is still good, and here's what changed in your market since." Then the seasonal trigger: rate-hike announcements, heat-wave season, the state-incentive deadline. Never fake urgency and never invent a production estimate — every roof is different, and the customer's neighbors will compare your number to theirs. The drip wins because nobody else stays in the mailbox for four months.
  2. The post-credit incentive-education campaign. Trigger: the policy calendar. The 30% federal residential credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025 — and many homeowners still expect it. The campaign teaches the new math honestly: owned systems get no federal credit in 2026; the third-party route (lease or PPA, where the company claims the commercial credit and prices it into the payment) still carries federal value; state incentives are a patchwork that moves (check each one live before quoting); and net-metering or export-credit rules decide the economics now that the credit is gone. The installer who runs this education sequence becomes the consultative seller in a market full of shops still subtracting an expired credit from their quotes — and avoids the compliance trap of selling a credit the customer can't claim. (One hard rule: you are explaining tax incentives, not giving tax advice. Say so, in the email.)
  3. The neighborhood referral machine. Trigger: install complete and producing. Email 1: the congratulations + the first-production milestone ("your system made its first megawatt-hour") + the referral offer — $500 per signed neighbor is the industry-standard shape (set your own number). Email 2 (to the street, months later): the neighborhood campaign — the honest version names no customer, just the street and the production milestone. Solar's referral conversion is the highest in home services because the product is on the roof; the email just asks at the right moment. Route-density wins: three installs on one street beat three installs in three towns, and the crew scheduling gets cheaper too.
  4. The battery-and-maintenance add-on. Trigger: the installed base, segmented by install year. The battery email goes to pre-2024 customers without storage: the pitch is outage protection plus self-consumption math, not savings alone — and in markets where export compensation fell, the battery is now the economics, not the accessory. The maintenance email goes on the 5-year mark: the inverter check-in, the panel-cleaning offer, the monitoring subscription. The installed base already trusts you with their roof; the add-on email is the cheapest revenue in the shop.
  5. The review sequence. Trigger: permission to operate (the system is live and producing). Email 1 (~7 days after PTO): the congratulations + the first production numbers + the direct Google review link. If no review after 14 days, email 2: the gentle nudge with the link. Keep it to two emails — more reads as begging. The review on a $30,000 purchase is worth five quotes, and in a trade where trust is the entire purchase, the review count is the moat.

The honest catches

Never fake the production math. The quote-verification world has a name for the warning signs: prices shown only after assumed incentives, no equivalent cash price, unnamed equipment, vague production estimates, verbal promises absent from the contract, pressure to sign before reviewing loan terms. Your emails must never do any of these. Quote the verified net price, name the equipment, and let the customer verify the production estimate against independent benchmarks. The shop whose emails survive the homeowner's comparison shopping is the shop that gets signed.

Get written consent — solar burned the channel. Solar is one of the most telemarketed industries in America, and customers arrive with their guard up. Marketing email needs opt-in consent; buy a lead list and blast it and you are the robocall in inbox form. The consent checkbox goes on every quote form and every site-visit signup, and the unsubscribe link actually works. Being the shop that respects the channel is a competitive advantage now.

Door-knock and canvass catch the impulse — email wins the planned. The canvasser at the door books the site visit; nobody signs $30,000 at the door. Email wins everything after the quote: the four-month consideration, the spouse conversation, the competitor comparison, the seasonal trigger. The two together are the pattern: the field team fills the top of the funnel, email closes the bottom.

Your pipeline software already sends some of this — don't double-send. Solar CRMs handle quote delivery, permit updates, and install scheduling; many run their own customer messaging. Let the pipeline software handle transactional job communication; let the email tool handle marketing campaigns, quote drips, referral sequences, and review asks. Check what your CRM already does before you pay for an email tool to do it twice.

Your list import is the real setup cost. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting the quote list and tagging it properly — quote status, quoted date, system owner vs lead, install year, battery owner, neighborhood (estimate — most CRM exports won't have all of these out of the box). Budget an afternoon, not ten minutes. A list without quote-status tags can't run the unclosed-quote drip — the tags are the product. Our deliverability audit checklist covers the list-hygiene half.

How do I talk about the expired tax credit without scaring customers off?

Teach the pivot, don't mourn the credit. The honest sequence: the 30% residential credit ended for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025 (signed into law July 2025) — so owned systems get no federal credit in 2026. The lease/PPA route still carries federal value through the commercial credit; state incentives are a live patchwork (verify each one before quoting); and the savings math now rests on net metering, export credits, and self-consumption with storage. Lead with the new math, and add the disclaimer that you explain incentives, not tax advice.

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and both are built for generic small business rather than this niche.
