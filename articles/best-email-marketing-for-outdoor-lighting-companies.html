An outdoor-lighting company sells the one thing the customer cannot see in daylight. The demo happens after dark: one tree lit, the walkway glowing, the house transformed — and the homeowner signs because they just felt the product. That makes this trade the most email-shaped business in home services: email can't light a yard, but it books the nighttime consultation where the sale closes. The economics are honest: residential installs average $3,500 nationally (Angi, April 2026, editor fact-checked), with most homeowners paying $2,000–$6,000 and $100–$500 per fixture installed. The holiday season is the trade's second engine: typical single-family Christmas installs run $800–$1,500, premium designs $1,500–$3,500, commercial and HOA work $2,500–$15,000+ (2026 trade pricing, estimate labeled). Research firms put the global landscape lighting market at ~$16.25 billion in 2026 (Coherent Market Insights, estimate, marked) — and the permanent-outdoor-lighting lane is growing 45–60% a year by trade reporting. Every tool below is scored against that machine: the nighttime-demo close, the holiday-lighting season, the permanent-lighting lane, the commercial property lane, and the service-and-maintenance annuity.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The nighttime-demo close. The consultation after dark is where residential sales close — the customer sees their own home transformed, and the design sells itself. Email's job is booking the demo, not selling the design. Scored on: the demo-booking campaign, the "see your home lit up tonight" sequence, and the post-demo follow-up that closes the quote within 72 hours.
  2. The holiday-lighting season. The trade's second engine, and the one with a brutal calendar: wholesale product ordered by August (popular SKUs sell out), bookings opened in July, installs September through November, takedowns in January. Trade wisdom says contractors doing $200K–$300K in a season are systems-ready by July (estimate, labeled). Scored on: the July pre-book campaign, the October fill-the-truck sequence, and the January takedown-to-next-year rebook.
  3. The permanent-lighting lane. Permanent outdoor lighting — year-round installed systems with holiday-programmable color — is the trade's recurring lane, and trade reporting says it's growing 45–60% annually (estimate, labeled). The pitch: never climb the ladder again. Scored on: the permanent-vs-seasonal nurture, the installed-base upsell drip, and the year-round testimonial machine.
  4. The commercial property lane. HOAs, churches, restaurants, municipalities — decision cycles run months, tickets run $2,500–$15,000+, and the nurture is the closer. The commercial landscape lighting market alone is estimated at $6.5B in 2026 (research-firm estimate). Scored on: the slow-drip proposal nurture, the budget-season timing, and the property-manager relationship campaign.
  5. The service-and-maintenance annuity. Installed systems need bulb replacements, timer changes, storm-damage fixes, and annual tune-ups — and the customer who bought the design is the customer who buys the service plan. This is what turns project revenue into an annuity. Scored on: the installed-base reactivation, the annual tune-up campaign, and the service-plan signup sequence.

The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The nighttime-demo booking campaign. The list-building and sale-booking machine: "we'll light up one tree at your home tonight, free, no pitch." The offer is the demo; the demo is the close. Run it year-round, weighted to spring and fall, and every demo request gets a 72-hour quote follow-up with their own home's photos. The campaign's job is appointments, not impressions: every empty evening is a demo that didn't happen.
  2. The July holiday pre-book. The season is won before the leaves turn: the July email goes to the full list — past holiday customers first, residential list second — with early-bird pricing that closes September 30. The October follow-up names the honest stakes: install slots fill by Thanksgiving, wholesale product sells out by August, and the takedown is included in the price. Trade wisdom says the $200K–$300K seasonal operators are ready by July (estimate, labeled) — the email calendar should be too.
  3. The permanent-lighting nurture. The year-round drip for the installed base and the holiday list: the "never climb the ladder again" pitch, the year-round curb appeal angle (4th of July red-white-blue, fall orange, game-day colors), and the financing note — permanent systems are the trade's highest-ticket residential sell. Send it to every holiday customer in February, when the ladder is fresh in memory and the seasonal takedown is done.
  4. The commercial slow-drip. The HOA-church-municipality nurture: budget cycles decide this lane, not urgency. The campaign is the portfolio email — the community entrance lit at night, the church steeple, the restaurant patio — sent quarterly to property managers and board contacts with the budget-season timing: get in the email before the budget meeting, not after. Six-month nurture cycles are normal; the patient operator wins the $15,000 ticket.
  5. The annual service-plan reactivation. The annuity campaign: the installed base gets the annual tune-up email — bulb check, timer reprogramming, storm-damage inspection — and the service-plan pitch at the point of maximum gratitude, right after the tune-up visit. Frame it as the math: the plan costs less than one emergency call-out, and the system gets inspected every year. A trade that installs beautiful systems and never touches them again is leaving the recurring lane on the table.

The honest catches

  • The customer can't buy what they can't see — the demo is the sale. Nobody signs a $3,500 design from a daytime consultation. The business model is the nighttime demo: the homeowner sees their own property transformed, and the design sells itself. Email's job is booking the demo, never closing the design. If the list isn't producing demo appointments, the email is failing — no matter what the open rates say.
  • The holiday season is won in July — never launch the pre-book in September. Wholesale SKUs sell out by August, install slots fill by Thanksgiving, and the operators who do $200K–$300K in a season (trade estimate, labeled) are systems-ready by July. An operator who "starts holiday marketing" in September is marketing to a calendar that's already full at the good shops. The pre-book email goes out in July or it doesn't go out at all.
  • Permanent and seasonal are different buyers — never pitch them in the same email. The seasonal buyer rents the season (installation + takedown, one winter); the permanent buyer buys never-climbing-the-ladder-again. Same house, two different sales, two different emails. The segmented list converts; the blast list confuses.
  • The demo after dark is the whole residential model — protect the calendar. Demos take evenings, and evenings are finite. The email machine should qualify, not just fill: the consultation request form asks the property size, the budget signal ("homes like yours typically invest $2,000–$6,000"), and the timeline. Ten qualified demos beat thirty tire-kickers — and the form does the qualifying before the night is spent.
  • Never let the holiday list go cold in March. The takedown email in January is the bridge: thank the customer, schedule the takedown, and put them on the permanent-lighting nurture for February. The customer who disappears after takedown is a customer who gets resold every October. The email list is the season you bank, not the season you chase.

FAQ

How much does landscape lighting installation cost in 2026?

Angi's April 2026 data (editor fact-checked) puts the national average at $3,500, with most homeowners paying $2,000–$6,000. Small basic systems start around $800; large custom designs run to $10,000. Costs are calculated per fixture at $100–$500 installed. A lighting design consultation alone runs $400–$500 (2026 trade pricing), and permits can add $150–$300.

How much does professional Christmas light installation cost in 2026?

2026 trade pricing puts a typical single-family install (rooflines, windows, basic landscaping) at $800–$1,500 — including installation, one maintenance visit, and end-of-season takedown. Premium designs with tree wrapping and custom color programming run $1,500–$3,500; commercial properties and HOAs run $2,500–$15,000+. Trade reporting puts basic residential margins at 35–45%, premium at 50–65%, luxury at 60–80% (estimates, labeled).

How much does permanent outdoor lighting cost?

Trade pricing guidance (2026, estimate labeled) runs roughly $30–$60 per linear foot for professionally installed permanent systems — against about $6 per foot for typical seasonal installs and $8–$12 per foot for premium seasonal homes. The premium buys year-round ownership, programmable holiday colors, and no ladder ever again. It's the trade's highest-ticket residential sell and the fastest-growing lane.

What's the difference between an outdoor-lighting company and an electrician?

The job description, not the wire: electricians do permits, panels, repairs, and code-compliant electrical work — see Best Email Marketing for Electricians for the trade email machine. Outdoor-lighting companies do design — the nighttime consultation, the fixture layout, the color temperature, the look of the house at 9pm — plus holiday installs and service plans. One is trade, one is art; different buyers, different funnels, different email machines.
