Start with the thing most "best newsletter platform" guides bury at the bottom: the question is not which tool sends the prettiest email — it is how you get paid and who gets paid with you. For authors and newsletter writers, the platform fee structure is the entire financial story, and it dwarfs every feature comparison. Substack takes 10% of your paid subscription revenue forever, with no tier that buys the fee down. Beehiiv takes 0% and charges a flat monthly plan instead. Kit takes a small per-transaction commerce fee on top of its subscriber-band plans. The differences look trivial at $200/month of revenue and catastrophic at $5,000/month. This guide does the crossover math at real list sizes, settles the ownership question (whose subscribers are they, really), and separates the paid-newsletter decision from the free-newsletter decision — because they are two different decisions and every guide that merges them is lying to you.

Two different decisions (read this first)

Decision 1: is the newsletter itself the product you sell, or is it the marketing channel for products you sell? These point at different tools:

  • The newsletter is the product (paid subscriptions, paywalled essays, member-only posts): the entire decision is fee math — Substack vs Beehiiv vs Ghost. Feature grids are nearly irrelevant because all three send email and gate content. Scroll to the fee table.
  • The newsletter is the channel (you sell books, courses, coaching, speaking; the list is your launch army): the decision is automation + deliverability + price at your list size, same as any small business. MailerLite and Kit win here; Substack's paid-subscription machinery is a cost you pay for nothing.

Most guides merge these into one list and the recommendation collapses under the weight of two different questions. Pick your decision before you pick your tool.

Read this table twice. A newsletter with 2,000 readers and 80 paying subscribers at $8/month is doing $640/mo — already past the crossover at its list tier. The fee is a growth tax with no ceiling — a $100,000/year paid newsletter pays Substack $10,000 a year for the privilege of existing there. The ownership question: whose subscribers are they?

The fee math decides money. Ownership decides leverage — and leverage is what bites authors three years into a platform they picked in an afternoon.

  • Substack: your subscribers export cleanly (emails, lists, archives), and paid subscribers can move because the Stripe customer relationship can transfer. But the payment relationship was Substack's: the checkout was theirs, the recommendation engine was theirs, and the network effects that grew you are the thing you leave behind. Migration off Substack is possible — Beehiiv and Ghost both offer free white-glove migrations including paid-subscriber Stripe handoff — but "possible" and "painless" are different words. The friction is real: DNS, custom domains, re-notifying free readers, rebuilding habits around a new dashboard. Budget 1–3 weeks and some lost momentum.
  • Beehiiv: your Stripe, your list, your domain — the platform takes 0% precisely because it never owned the money layer. Leaving Beehiiv is leaving a tool, not a business partner. Its own Boosts ad network and ad marketplace mean some writers do build Beehiiv-native revenue (paid promotions between newsletters) — revenue that is, in the honest sense, portable since it runs on your audience, not their payment rails.
  • Kit: strongest ownership posture of the three mainstream options — your Stripe, your list, and the commerce features (products, courses, paid newsletters) sit on your account. Kit's Creator Network (cross-promotion with other newsletters) takes a cut on paid cross-promos, but your core list and payments stay yours.
  • Ghost: maximum ownership — open-source software, your Stripe account, your domain, full data export, and the option to self-host for free on your own server. The trade: no built-in audience network, more setup than the others, and (on self-hosting) you are the IT department — email deliverability through your own Mailgun/SendGrid account is a job, not a checkbox.

The honest ranking on ownership: Ghost (full) > Kit/Beehiiv (your payments, their dashboard) > Substack (your list, their payment relationship and discovery). Price the ownership gap against the fee gap — sometimes Substack's free start is worth the lock-in risk, but only if you named the risk first.

The discovery illusion: is Substack's network worth 10%?

Substack's real defense of its 10% cut is not features — it is the recommendation network: other newsletters recommending yours, the Substack app and leaderboards, network-effect growth you cannot buy elsewhere. This is the one argument with teeth. The question is only whether it applies to you:

  • Worth paying for if your growth is network-native: culture, politics, media criticism, tech commentary — genres where top writers genuinely report 30%+ of new subscribers arriving via Substack recommendations. If the network is delivering more than 10% of your growth, the fee is buying growth, not rent.
  • Not worth paying for if your readers arrive from your own channels anyway: an existing audience, a podcast, a YouTube channel, book marketing, conference speaking. A business author with 40,000 LinkedIn followers driving their own traffic is paying 10% for discovery they never receive. This is the most common form of the Substack overpay — the platform collects the tax on growth it did not cause.

The test: look at where your last 100 subscribers came from. If fewer than 10 would have found you through Substack's network, the 10% is rent, not a growth investment. Most niche nonfiction authors fail this test and stay anyway because starting was free and switching feels expensive. That is the business model working exactly as designed.

Decision 1 playbook: launching paid subscriptions

For writers charging readers, the build order is the same on every platform — the tool choice only changes where the money goes:

  1. Validate free first (90 days minimum). Do not build a paywall on day one. Publish free, learn what your 5 best-performing posts have in common, and let the free list grow to a few hundred engaged readers. The paid tier converts from your most obsessed free readers — our estimate, consistent with reported newsletter benchmarks: 2–10% of a free list converts to paid. At 1,000 free readers, that's 20–100 paying subscribers, or $200–$1,000/month at $10/mo. Run the crossover math above against that number before picking the platform.
  2. Gate the right things. What converts: bonus essays, full archives, community/threads, Q&A, and anything scarce (your time, your templates, your drafts). What doesn't: "the same newsletter but you feel good about it." Price at $5–10/month or $50–100/year; annual plans are cash-flow gold — a year's revenue upfront from readers who would have paid monthly.
  3. Pick the platform by revenue expectation, not by where you are today. Starting Substack free and migrating to Beehiiv at $1,000/mo revenue is a viable strategy — but name the migration as the plan on day one, or day-one comfort becomes year-three inertia. Conversely, starting on Beehiiv Scale at $49/mo before you have any revenue is paying rent on an empty store; run the free Launch plan (to 2,500 subs) until monetization is live, then upgrade the month you charge.
  4. Set up the second revenue stream before you need it. Subscriptions are one stream; newsletter ad networks (Beehiiv's built-in ad marketplace), sponsorships, and Kit's product/course commerce are the others. A $2,000/mo newsletter at a 5,000-subscriber list can often match that in sponsorship income with a standard media kit — our estimate; sponsor rates vary wildly by niche — and that revenue has no platform fee on any tool. Beehiiv's ad network is the most mature built-in option; Kit's commerce is the best if your second stream is products; Ghost keeps 0% of all of it.

Decision 2 playbook: the newsletter as a book-launch machine

If you sell books (or courses, coaching, speaking) and the newsletter is the channel, ignore the paid-subscription machinery entirely. Your requirements are: cheap at your list size, unlimited automations for the launch funnel, and deliverability that puts the email in the inbox on launch day.

  1. The 90-day launch funnel (tool-agnostic, fits inside free plans): a reader-magnet landing page (sample chapter, bonus short story, checklist — the magnet converts at 20–40% of page visitors; our estimate from standard lead-magnet benchmarks) → a 5–7 email welcome sequence that sells the author's world, not just the book → segmentation by magnet topic so the next book launches to the right readers → a launch-week broadcast sequence (announce, social proof, cart-close). Kit's free plan (10,000 subscribers) runs this whole funnel at $0; MailerLite's automations run it at $19/mo at 1,000 contacts with more visual builder depth than Substack's email tools will ever have.
  2. Why not Substack for this job. Substack is a publishing platform with email attached, not an email marketing tool. Its automation is thin, its segmentation is thin, and if you ever turn on paid subscriptions by accident — or the platform nudges you toward it — the 10% cut applies to revenue your books earned. Authors who "just use Substack for the list" are parking their launch army on someone else's parade ground.
  3. MailerLite vs Kit for authors, honestly. MailerLite wins on price-per-contact and template quality at small lists ($19/mo at 1,000 contacts, free plan for starting out). Kit wins on commerce and creator-specific workflow — if the "next product" is a course or a paid community, Kit sells it in-platform and tags the buyers automatically, while MailerLite needs a third-party checkout wired in. Price the whole stack (email tool + checkout + membership), not the email tool alone.

Where Beehiiv and Substack fit for authors specifically

  • Beehiiv's hidden strength for authors: the website/newsletter archive is genuinely good at SEO — your essays rank and compound as evergreen traffic, which is distribution Substack's app-centric model doesn't give you. Plus the ad network turns a free list into sponsor revenue without paid subscriptions. For a nonfiction author whose newsletter is free and whose books are the product, Beehiiv Launch (free to 2,500) plus the ad network is arguably the strongest free-newsletter stack in this guide — but we don't carry an affiliate link for Beehiiv (no verified program as of October 1, 2026); this recommendation is uncompensated.
  • Substack's honest use case: you're starting from zero, you will not charge for at least a year, and you write in a genre where the recommendation network is real. It costs nothing, it has the simplest editor on this page, and the Notes/app ecosystem gives a new writer reach no email tool can match. The correct play is to use it with an exit plan: own your domain from day one (Substack supports custom domains — do this, it's the difference between migrating and restarting), export your list quarterly, and know your crossover revenue number before you turn on paid subs.
  • Ghost's honest use case: you already have an audience and the revenue to justify it, or you are technical enough to self-host. It is the only option here with a 0% fee and full membership machinery and real SEO control. The $9–15/mo Starter covers 500 members — for a paid newsletter doing even $500/mo, Ghost is the cheapest honest stack on this page by a wide margin. Billing traps, author edition

  • Kit's 10,000-subscriber cliff — the free Newsletter plan ends at 10,000 subscribers, and the jump into paid tiers at that size is steep (sources report the 10k–15k Creator tier jumping to ~$169/mo on monthly billing as of August 2026). Growing through the cliff is a good problem to have, but price the landing zone before you cross it.
  • Beehiiv's free plan excludes monetization — Launch (free to 2,500 subs) has no paid subscriptions and limited automations. It is a publishing free tier, not a business free tier. Upgrade to Scale the month you charge; don't discover the gate mid-launch.
  • Substack's fee is invisible by design — there is no line item, no invoice, no plan to downgrade. You just receive 90%. Set a calendar reminder at your crossover revenue to re-run the Beehiiv/Ghost math; the fee compounds silently.
  • Paying for sends you don't need — authors publish weekly or biweekly. Unlimited-sends plans (Beehiiv, Kit free) remove this cost entirely; subscriber-count billing is the only price that matters for a newsletter cadence. Don't buy a send-volume plan for a publication schedule that sends 4 emails a month.
  • The custom-domain decision — whichever platform you pick, put it on your own domain from day one. It costs ~$12/year for the domain and it is the single cheapest insurance policy in this guide: it makes every future migration a redirect instead of a restart.
  • Kit's disputed commerce fee — repeated from the fee table because it matters for paid newsletters on Kit: verify the current transaction fee in Kit's help docs. If it's still 3.5% + $0.30 for paid subscriptions (the older figure), Kit's fee story looks materially worse than the 0.6% figure recent reviews report. Don't build a paid product on an unverified fee.

The bottom line

Charging readers and expect to grow: Beehiiv Scale — $49/mo at 1,000 subscribers, 0% platform fee. Run the crossover math: past ~$490/mo in subscription revenue at the 1k tier, it beats Substack, and the gap only widens.
Starting from zero, not charging yet: Substack — $0 at any size, simplest start, real recommendation network. Own your domain, export quarterly, know your crossover number.
Selling books/courses with the newsletter as the channel: Kit free (to 10,000 subscribers) or MailerLite ($19/mo at 1k) — full launch funnels without a platform taking a cut of anything you sell.
Want everything and own everything: Ghost Pro (~$9–15/mo Starter) or self-hosted free — 0% fee, your Stripe, your data, your SEO.
The one sentence: percentages are cheap at $200/month and catastrophic at $5,000/month — pick the fee structure for the revenue you're building toward, not the revenue you have today.
