What a financial advisor actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The six jobs that matter, compliance-wrapped:

  1. The market-commentary newsletter. The one email clients actually open and forward: your plain-English take on the month's markets, what it means for their plan, and what you're doing about it. Monthly beats weekly — consistency beats frequency. This is the retention engine: the client who reads your take every month doesn't shop advisors, because their advisor is the person who explains the world to them.
  2. The annual-review drip. The review calendar runs itself: the "let's schedule your annual review" email 90 days before the review anniversary, the reminder at 30 days, the "here's what we'll cover" prep email a week out with the agenda and anything to bring. Reviews are where retention is won and cross-selling happens — the drip makes sure nobody slips through.
  3. The seminar/webinar engine. The "Social Security timing" dinner seminar, the "retirement income" webinar, the year-end tax-and-planning workshop — advisors have run these forever, and the email tool is the RSVP machine: the invitation, the reminder sequence, the replay email with the "book a one-on-one" call to action. Webinars double as lead magnets and cross-sell floors.
  4. The referral ask sequence. Referrals are the #1 growth channel for advisory practices, and nobody asks for them systematically. The sequence: after the annual review lands well, the thank-you email; two weeks later, the soft ask — "if you know someone who could use a second opinion on their plan, here's how introductions work here." Never incentivized (that's a compliance minefield — your compliance officer's rule, not this article's).
  5. The fiduciary onboarding trust transfer. New clients sign up and then wait — and waiting erodes trust. The onboarding sequence: the welcome and what to expect, the fiduciary explanation in plain English (what "we act in your best interest" actually means in practice), the account-transfer and beneficiary checklist, the portal invitation. Standardize it once and every client gets the same confident first month.
  6. The review moat. Clients pick advisors from referrals and Google reviews, not coupons. The review ask after the annual review — when the plan is fresh and the value is top of mind — with the direct Google review link. Two emails max, then stop. (One caveat for the compliance officer: testimonials and endorsements have their own rules under FINRA and the SEC Marketing Rule — ask your compliance officer how your firm handles review asks before you automate them. This is general information, not compliance advice.)

Notice what's missing: no lead scoring, no landing-page Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. The finance CRM (Wealthbox, Redtail — the established names) already holds the client records and runs the householding, task workflows, and document links. The archiving layer (Smarsh / Global Relay class) owns compliance record-keeping. The email tool only has to hold the marketing list, send the campaigns, and run the newsletter-review-webinar-referral automations. Paying for a marketing department's toolkit is the trap.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a practice leaves on the table:

  1. The market-commentary newsletter. Monthly, same week every month: your plain-English take on the markets, what it means for client plans, and one action item. This is the retention engine and the forward engine — clients forward good commentary to the exact people who become your referrals. Have compliance review the template once; then fill in the commentary.
  2. The annual-review drip. Trigger: review anniversary minus 90 days. Email 1: the scheduling invitation. Email 2 (30 days out): the reminder. Email 3 (one week out): the prep email — the agenda, the documents to bring, the "here's what changed in your plan since last year" teaser. Email 4 (after the review): the thank-you plus the referral soft ask. This sequence is where retention and growth actually happen.
  3. The seminar/webinar engine. Invitation (3 weeks out) → reminder (1 week) → final call (day before) → replay + booking link (day after). The replay email is the highest-converting send of the whole sequence — the people who registered but didn't attend are the warmest leads you have. Pick one topic per quarter (Social Security timing, retirement income, year-end tax planning) and repeat the calendar.
  4. The referral ask sequence. Trigger: a successful annual review or a resolved problem. Email 1 (day after): the thank-you. Email 2 (two weeks later): the soft ask — "if you know someone who could use a second opinion on their retirement plan, here's how introductions work here." Keep it to two emails; more reads as pressure. (Compliance note: never incentivize referrals without your compliance officer's explicit framework — that's their rule, not mine.)
  5. The fiduciary onboarding trust transfer. Trigger: new client signed. Email 1: the welcome and what to expect (the timeline, the process, who to call). Email 2: the fiduciary explanation in plain English — what "we act in your best interest" means in practice, and how it differs from the commission model. Email 3: the account-transfer and beneficiary checklist. Email 4: the portal invitation and orientation. Standardize it once and every client gets the same confident first month — and the front office stops improvising the same five emails a hundred times a year.

The honest catches

Your CRM already owns the client record — don't double-buy it. Wealthbox and Redtail already hold the households, the account links, the task workflows, and the document references. Let them own the client record; let the email tool handle marketing campaigns, the newsletter, the review drip, the seminar engine, and the referral sequence. Check what your CRM already automates before you pay for an email tool to do it twice. If the CRM's client-communication engine is already strong, the email tool's job gets smaller — buy accordingly.

FAQ

What's the cheapest email tool for a financial advisor?

At typical practice list sizes (300–800 households, estimate), MailerLite's Comfort plan is the cheapest of the five tools here with real automations included — from $12/mo at 500 subscribers (about $19/mo at 1,000, per October 2026 roundups). Brevo is cheaper if you blast the whole client book on a regular cadence ($9/mo for 5,000 emails). All prices checked October 5, 2026 — verify live before checkout.

Can I import my client list from my CRM?

Yes — every tool here takes a CSV export, and every finance CRM exports one. The honest workflow for most practices is a scheduled CSV export and import, with life stage, service line, and review-status tags added as custom fields. The client record, the account data, and the document links stay in the CRM; the email tool only needs the marketing subset — the clients who opted into marketing emails.

Do I need an email tool if my CRM already sends client messages?

Yes — for different jobs. The CRM (Wealthbox, Redtail) owns the client record: households, accounts, tasks, document links. The archiving layer owns compliance record-keeping. The email tool owns marketing: the newsletter, the review drip, the seminar engine, the onboarding sequence, the referral ask. Different tools, different jobs — run both layers.

When should the annual-review drip start?

Before the review anniversary, not on it: the scheduling invitation ~90 days out, the reminder at 30 days, the prep email a week out, the thank-you the day after. Pre-build the life-stage segments; writing the review invitation the week of the review means the client already booked the vacation or already met with someone else. The calendar repeats forever — template it once.

Are FMG Suite or HubSpot fine for advisory practices?

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and both solve different problems. FMG Suite (~$178/mo + setup) sells you the compliance review process — worth it when nobody on the team wants to be the reviewer. HubSpot is the all-in-one CRM-and-marketing play, but its compliance workflows are manual and the real automation costs climb fast — the math rarely favors it for a solo practice that just needs campaigns and automations.

What about the compliance review — how does that actually work?

That depends on your firm: broker-dealer reps go through their firm's principal review process, RIAs through the chief compliance officer's framework. Build the campaign templates first, get the template family approved once, and the monthly commentary becomes a fill-in-the-blanks exercise instead of a fresh review each time.
