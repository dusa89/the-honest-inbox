A dry cleaner is not a cleaning business — it is a cycle business. The US has roughly 31,000 laundry and dry-cleaning establishments doing about $12 billion a year (First Research, 2026), the market is forecast to grow from $11.82 billion in 2025 to $18.01 billion by 2034 (GlobeNewswire, June 2025), and the trade press keeps saying the same thing about where the money is moving: the residential side is outgrowing the commercial side, and online laundry is gaining ground. Because the whole business is a rhythm: the bag goes out, the bag comes back, every week, forever. The customer who visits twice a year is a transaction; the customer whose bag rides the route every Tuesday is an annuity. The machinery that runs the rhythm is all email: the garment-cycle engine (the reminder that keeps the bag moving), the wash-and-fold subscription (the machine that converts errands into recurring revenue), the wedding-gown preservation ladder (the one $400 ticket that pays like 80 shirts), the corporate-shirt route (the B2B lane — uniforms, hotels, the contracted volume), and the reactivation engine (the lapsed bag). Here is how the best tool for the cleaner runs them.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The garment-cycle engine. The core of the business. Dry cleaning is a habit: the bag goes out on pickup day and comes back clean on the route. The email machinery is the reminder cycle — the "route day is tomorrow" send, the ready-for-pickup alert, the pickup-and-delivery scheduling confirmation. This is the highest-frequency engine on the site: a cleaner touches a customer every single week, and every touch is an email or a text that keeps the cycle from breaking. Scored on the tools: can it run the weekly cadence and the ready-alerts without the cleaner hand-writing every send?
  2. The wash-and-fold subscription engine. The growth machine the trade press keeps documenting. The pitch is disarmingly simple — as one subscription operator put it: "from the customer's perspective, they never have to do laundry again"; a two-, four- or six-hour-a-week chore becomes a 10-minute bag-out. The machinery: the launch sequence (the hanger tags on outgoing orders, the text and email campaigns to existing customers — "existing customers are the first place to go because they know your work and they trust you"), the auto-billing backbone (the point-of-sale has to auto-bill monthly and charge for overages — the subscription is only a subscription if the billing is automatic), and the onboarding (the first-bag welcome that sets the route day). Scored on the tools: can it run the launch, the nurture, and the win-back of a subscription business?
  3. The wedding-gown preservation ladder. The margin machine. Most professional gown preservation runs $150–$800, with the average wedding gown landing $250–$400 (B&C Cleaners 2026 guide) — couture and heirloom work runs $600–$800+. One preservation order pays like roughly 80 shirts. The machinery: the referral lane (the bridal-shop relationship — the cleaner the salon recommends wins the order), the wedding-season timing (the brides of June need their gowns preserved by fall), the education nurture (the bride doesn't know what preservation is — acid-free archival packaging, stain treatment for the invisible stains — and the education email is what sells the $400 box instead of the $99 mail-away kit), and the post-honeymoon reactivation (the dress sits in the closet for months — the timed "your dress is waiting" send). Scored on the tools: can it run a high-ticket nurture with timed reactivations?
  4. The corporate-shirt route. The volume lane. Uniforms, hotel linens, restaurant whites, the office shirt bundle — contracted, recurring, predictable. The email machinery is different from the retail lane: the account-nurture sequence (the HR manager who chooses the vendor), the service-alert track (route changes, holiday schedules — the communication that keeps the contract), and the expansion engine (the account that started with shirts gets the uniform pitch). This lane runs on fewer, bigger relationships — the CRM-style discipline, not the broadcast calendar. Scored on the tools: can it run two tracks — retail cycle and corporate route — without the messages bleeding across?
  5. The reactivation engine. The free money. The lapsed customer is the cheapest acquisition a cleaner has: the bag that used to come every week and stopped. The machinery: the 90-day save (the "we miss your bag" winback with a reason to return), the seasonal rotation (the winter-coat surge, the comforter season, the holiday-linen run — the calendar events that pull a lapsed customer back on their own), and the anniversary nudge (the preservation customer from last wedding season is a referral engine — ask for the bridal party). Scored on the tools: can it trigger on lapse and season automatically?

Notice what's missing: no generic "monthly newsletter" (the cycle customer needs the reminder, not the content), no discounting as a strategy (the cleaner that discounts the subscription trains customers to wait for the coupon — the subscription IS the offer), no social-media dependence (the algorithm shows the gown to strangers; the email shows the reminder to the bag-owner). The cycle keeps the bag moving, the subscription converts the chore into revenue, the gown ladder lands the margin, the route lands the volume, and the reactivation catches the lapses. A cleaner whose email is "the monthly special" is running a cycle business on a coupon calendar.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a dry cleaner leaves on the table:

  1. The route-day reminder. Trigger: the day before the pickup. The "route day is tomorrow — have the bag out" send (or text). The cycle that never breaks is the cycle that never has to be rebuilt.
  2. The subscription launch. Trigger: the launch week. The hanger-tag + email + text campaign to existing customers: "never do laundry again." The trade press is blunt about where to aim it — existing customers first, because they know the work and they trust it. One week of launch beats a year of hoping.
  3. The post-wedding nurture. Trigger: wedding season + 60 days after. The education sequence that sells the $400 archival box: the invisible stains, the acid-free packaging, the heirloom framing — and the timed "your dress is waiting" reactivation when the dress sits in the closet. The bridal-shop referral lane feeds it; the nurture converts it.
  4. The lapsed-bag winback. Trigger: 90 days without an order. The "we miss your bag" send — with a reason to return (the seasonal rotation, the subscription pitch, the simple "route day hasn't changed"). The cheapest acquisition on the books.
  5. The seasonal rotation. Trigger: the calendar. The winter-coat surge, the comforter season, the holiday-linen run — the three seasons that pull lapsed customers back on their own. Each one is a campaign the list sends itself.

The honest catches

The coupon is the enemy of the cycle. The discount gets the bag in the door once — and trains the customer to wait for the next coupon before bringing the next bag. The subscription is the honest offer: the price is the price, the value is "never do laundry again." Run the subscription, not the coupon calendar.

The subscription is only a subscription if the billing is automatic. The trade press is blunt on this: the point-of-sale has to auto-bill monthly and charge for overages. The email machine can sell the subscription all day — if the billing is manual, you built a reminder service, not a subscription business.

The gown is sold on education, not on price. The bride comparing your $400 preservation to a $99 mail-away kit does not understand the difference — and the education email is what teaches her. Skip the nurture and you lose the lane on price to a box in the mail.

The commercial lane is a different business. The corporate-shirt account does not respond to the retail cycle's messages — it responds to reliability communications: route changes, holiday schedules, the account review. Run the commercial track as a separate track with separate segments, or the retail volume will drown the relationship that pays the rent.

The lapsed customer is a timing problem, not a price problem. The bag stopped coming because life changed — not because the price was wrong. The winback that leads with a discount teaches the lesson the coupon already taught; the winback that leads with the season or the subscription sells the return.

Residential is outgrowing commercial. The market data is blunt: the residential side is growing faster than the commercial side, and online laundry is gaining ground. The cleaner's email program should weight the bag-cycle and the subscription heavier than the corporate lane — the market is telling you where the growth is.
