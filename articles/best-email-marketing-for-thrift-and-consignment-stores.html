Resale is the fastest-growing corner of retail, and the numbers are not subtle: U.S. secondhand apparel grew 19% in 2025 — its strongest year since 2021 and 3.6x the broader retail clothing market — with 59% of consumers shopping secondhand (ThredUp's 14th annual 2026 Resale Report, GlobalData). The global secondhand apparel market is projected at $393 billion by 2030, roughly 10% of all global apparel spend. And the report's most important sentence for a store owner is not about demand — it's about supply: "supply is the new constraint." The industry is racing to find enough stock, with $23.3 billion in additional U.S. market value unlocked if selling gets as easy as buying. That makes the thrift or consignment store a different email animal from every other retailer on this site. A normal shop's email sells products that restock. Your email sells drops — one-of-one inventory that never repeats: the vintage Levi's rack, the mid-century chair, the bag of designer shoes that landed Tuesday. The drop announcement is not marketing for the product. The drop announcement is the product. And on the consignment side, the email has a second job no retailer has: keeping the suppliers — the consignors with store credit sitting unspent — coming back in. Five engines no other vertical has in this form: the drop-day engine — the new-drop calendar that turns the list into the store's daily paper; the treasure-hunt segment engine — the vintage-fashion hunters, the furniture hunters, the kids'-stuff hunters, each getting only their drops; the consignment-payout reactivation — the unspent store credit that must be nudged back through the door; the supply-first sourcing engine — the donation reminders and seasonal purge campaigns, because in 2026 inventory is the constraint; and the regulars loyalty loop — the Friday-morning regulars whose rhythm is the store's floor. What a thrift or consignment store actually needs from email

Forget "open rates." The five engines that decide whether the drops move and the racks stay stocked:

  1. The drop-day engine. The product. One-of-one inventory means the announcement is the inventory going public: the new-drop email (the rack that hit the floor this morning, photographed), the drop-day rhythm (the regulars who plan their week around your drop days — Tuesday vintage, Friday furniture), and the sold-out proof (the "gone in 40 minutes" follow-up that trains the list to open fast next time). Scored on the tools: can it send a same-day drop announcement with photos, fast, from a phone, without a template wrestling match?
  2. The treasure-hunt segment engine. The targeting. Nobody hunts everything — the vintage-fashion hunter doesn't care about the kids'-stuff drop, and the furniture hunter doesn't open the book-media email. The engine: the interest segments (vintage fashion, furniture & home, kids', books & media, designer bags — the store's real hunter tribes), the drop routing (each segment gets only its drops — the furniture email goes to the furniture segment, never the whole list), and the want-list capture (the "tell us what you're hunting" form — the store's standing order book). Scored on the tools: can it segment the list into hunter tribes and route the drop to the right tribe automatically?
  3. The consignment-payout reactivation. The supplier side. The consignor whose items sold has store credit (or a payout check) sitting unspent — and unspent credit is a liability that earns nothing. The engine: the payout notification (the "your items sold — $84 in credit" email, the happiest email a consignor gets), the reactivation nudge (the credit that's been sitting 60 days gets the "come find something" sequence), and the new-consignment invite (the consignor with a full closet is the store's supply line — invite them back before they take it elsewhere). Scored on the tools: can it trigger emails off a consignor's credit balance and inactivity?
  4. The supply-first sourcing engine. The 2026 engine. Supply is the industry's constraint — the store that emails only to sell is running half the business. The engine: the donation reminders (the thrift store's intake calendar — the "cleaning out the garage?" sequence), the seasonal purge campaigns (spring cleaning, January closet resets, the back-to-school kids'-closet turnover — timed to when donors purge), the donation-pickup scheduler (the email that books the pickup truck), and the consignor intake invite (the curated consignment shop's supply line — "we're accepting fall designer now"). Scored on the tools: can it run intake campaigns on a seasonal calendar, separate from the selling calendar?
  5. The regulars loyalty loop. The floor. Every thrift store has its Friday-morning regulars — the hunters who show up whether or not there's an email. The engine: the loyalty recognition (the regulars' early-access window — the "doors open to the list at 9, to everyone at 10" perk), the visit streak (the "you've been in 4 Fridays running" nudge), and the find-share loop (the regulars' finds, photographed and shared back to the list — the store's content, made by its customers). The ice cream shop guide covers the other drop business's regulars (the flavor calendar); the thrift regulars run on the rack calendar — what's on the floor, not what's on the menu.

Notice what's missing: no generic "shop our new arrivals" blasts (your arrivals don't restock), no discount spam on drop day (the drop that sells out in an hour needs no coupon), no one-list-fits-all (the hunter tribes open only their drops). The drop engine moves the one-of-one inventory, the segments route it to the right hunters, the consignor engine keeps the suppliers paid and coming back, the sourcing engine keeps the racks fed, and the regulars loop keeps the floor alive. A resale store whose only email is the monthly newsletter is running a drop business with no drop infrastructure.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a resale store leaves on the table:

  2. The treasure-hunt want-list. Trigger: the hunter, on signup. The payout notification (the "your items sold — $84 in credit" — the happiest email a consignor gets), the 60-day credit nudge (the unspent credit gets the "come find something" sequence), and the new-intake invite (the consignor with a full closet gets the "we're accepting fall designer now" before they take it elsewhere). The supplier side of the business, run like revenue.
  4. The seasonal purge campaign. Trigger: the calendar, automated. The spring-cleaning sequence (the "cleaning out the garage?" donation campaign), the January closet reset (the new-year purge — the year's biggest intake window), the back-to-school kids'-closet turnover, and the donation-pickup booking (the email that fills the pickup truck). The honest catches

Your POS already sends the transactional layer. Don't double-buy it. This is the resale store's version of the rented-audience trap. Square, Lightspeed, Clover, and the rest already send the receipt, the payment confirmation, the pickup reminder. Those are not this tool's job. The email tool owns the drop, the hunter segments, the consignors, and the supply. Audit what your stack already sends before you pay for an email tool to send it twice — and note the quieter trap: Square will happily sell you the email layer too, but a generic retail email tool doesn't know what a drop is. The owned list is the drop subscribers, the consignors, and the donors.

The one-of-one trap: don't sell every SKU by email. The instinct is to photograph every item and email the rack — and the rack has 400 items. The drop announcement is the product, not the item: the email sells the drop (the rack, the theme, the day), and the store sells the items. Email the ten hero pieces, photograph the rack, name the day. The store that tries to list every unique SKU by email builds a second job that pays nothing.

Supply is the constraint — so email sources, not just sells. ThredUp's 2026 Resale Report says it plainly: the industry is racing to find enough stock. The store that emails only to sell is running half the business — the donation reminders, the purge campaigns, and the consignor-intake invites are revenue work, not housekeeping. Every intake campaign that fills the back room is a drop campaign that hasn't happened yet.

Don't discount the drop that would have sold out anyway. The drop-day crowd needs no coupon — the "gone in 40 minutes" rack was going to move at full price. Deals belong in the slow rack (the picked-over back section, the off-season categories) and in the first-drop offer for a brand-new subscriber. If the core drops need discounting, that's a curation problem, not an email problem.

Two businesses, two lists — segment the suppliers from the shoppers. The consignor and the hunter are different customers buying different things. The consignor doesn't want the drop alert for the rack they stocked; the hunter doesn't want the intake-schedule email. The minimum viable segmentation is shoppers vs. consignors vs. donors — three segments, three voices. The store that emails the consignor a first-drop-free coupon is telling its supplier they're worth less than strangers.

How is this different from the ice cream shop guide?

The ice cream shop guide covers the other drop business — but the inventory logic is the opposite. Ice cream drops are seasonal menu items: every customer can get the flavor, and the constraint is the season. Thrift drops are one-of-one: one rack, one vintage jacket, one chair — gone when it's gone. The ice cream email announces the season; the thrift email announces the rack. Same bench of tools, opposite scarcity — which is why the engines and the sequences don't transfer.

Should a thrift store use Kit?

It depends on which business you're running. Kit is the right tool for the thrift-flipper creator — the haul content, the "how to thrift" audience, the curated finds sold to followers nationwide: landing pages, sequences, and digital-product checkout are its native shape. It is the wrong tool for the local store's drop engine: steep subscriber-based pricing and templates built for content sold to followers, not racks, drops, and consignors. Two businesses, two tools: Kit owns the creator-audience list; the store's drop business lives in MailerLite or GetResponse. The full price curves: Kit pricing explained.

Do I need email marketing if my POS already sends receipts?

Yes — that's exactly the store that needs it most. Square, Lightspeed, and Clover send the transactional layer — receipts, payment confirmations, pickup reminders — and they do it well. What they don't do is the drop engine: the drop-day announcements, the hunter segments, the consignor reactivation, the sourcing campaigns. The stack handles the customers you already rang up. Email fills the floor before the doors open.
