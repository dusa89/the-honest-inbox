The calendar is a machine gun: January estimated taxes, March S-corp returns, April individual returns, quarterly estimates all year. The firm that runs a real deadline drip and a document-chase sequence gets its tax season back. The firm that runs a year-end tax-planning newsletter (October through December — the window when planning actually works) sells advisory instead of begging for it. What an accounting firm actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The six jobs that matter:

  1. The deadline drip. The highest-ROI email a firm sends is the one that arrives before the deadline, not after: the "organizers are due" email in January, the S-corp reminder before March 15, the individual-return push before April 15, the quarterly-estimate nudges (Jan 15, Apr 15, Jun 15, Sep 15). Every client who files on time because of a drip is a March-April emergency the firm never has to absorb.
  2. The document-chase engine. The missing-1099 follow-up is the most expensive email problem in the building. The sequence: the polite organizer-chase a week after sending the organizer, the "we're still missing your 1099s" nudge two weeks later, the escalation to a phone call after that. This is the automation that pays for the tool — it's the difference between a calm February and a drowning one.
  3. Tax-season retention + new-client onboarding. Every new client should get the same onboarding sequence: the welcome and what-to-expect, the organizer and document checklist, the portal invitation, the "who to contact and when" email. Standardize it once and the front desk stops improvising it. Retention is the flip side — the "we're here, here's what changes this year" email to the whole client file before tax season, so the client you did a great return for last April doesn't drift to the cheap online option in January.
  4. The year-end planning newsletter. October through December is the window when tax planning actually works — and it's the window when nobody thinks about taxes. The firm that runs the year-end newsletter (retirement-contribution deadlines, harvest timing, entity-check reminders, new-law changes in plain English) is the firm that sells advisory engagements instead of processing forms. This sequence is the advisory cross-sell without the awkward ask.
  5. The quarterly-estimate reminders. Self-employed clients and S-corp owners forget estimated payments with perfect regularity. The drip — ten days before each quarterly deadline, with the amount and the payment link — is the simplest, most appreciated automation a firm can run, and it quietly positions the firm as the thing standing between the client and an underpayment penalty.
  6. The late-April review moat. Clients pick accountants from referrals and reviews, not coupons. The automated review ask in late April, after the return is delivered and the relief is fresh, with the direct Google review link and the partner's name in the subject line if they know it — two emails max, then stop. In a market where the map pack is the front page, this sequence is a moat-builder.

Notice what's missing: no lead scoring, no landing-page Olympics, no AI subject-line generators. The tax practice suite (Intuit ProConnect, Lacerte, ProSeries, CCH Axcess, Drake, UltraTax — the established names) already holds the client records and runs the organizer, engagement-letter, e-sign, and portal workflows. The email tool only has to hold the marketing list, send the campaigns, and run the deadline-and-document automations. Paying for a marketing department's toolkit is the trap.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a firm leaves on the table:

  1. The deadline drip calendar. Build the whole year once and it repeats forever: the January "organizers are coming" email, the S-corp reminder ~3 weeks before March 15, the individual-return push ~4 weeks before April 15, the quarterly-estimate nudge 10 days before each quarterly date (Jan 15, Apr 15, Jun 15, Sep 15) with the payment amount and link. Tag clients by entity type and engagement so S-corp clients get S-corp reminders and the self-employed get the estimate series — targeted drips get read, whole-file blasts get tuned out.
  2. The document-chase engine. Trigger: organizer sent, documents not yet received. Email 1 (one week later): the polite checklist — "here's what we're still missing," with the specific documents named. Email 2 (two weeks later): the firmer nudge with the deadline the firm's filing calendar requires. Email 3 (three weeks): the escalation — a phone call from the front desk. This is the highest-ROI automation on the page: every document that arrives in February instead of April is a March emergency that never happens.
  3. The year-end planning newsletter. October through December: the retirement-contribution-deadline reminder, the harvest-and-timing explainer, the entity-check email for new business owners, the plain-English summary of this year's law changes. This is the newsletter that sells advisory engagements without a sales call — the reader who learns something in October hires the planner in November. Start it the October you're reading this; the window opens now.
  4. The new-client onboarding sequence. Trigger: engagement letter signed. Email 1: the welcome and what to expect (the timeline, the process, who to contact). Email 2: the organizer and document checklist. Email 3: the portal invitation and the "everything lives here" orientation. Standardize it once and every client gets the same confident first week — and the front desk stops improvising the same five emails a hundred times a season.
  5. The review sequence. Trigger: return delivered, late April. Email 1: the thank-you + direct Google review link, the partner's name in the subject line if the client knows them. If no review after 14 days, email 2: the gentle nudge with the link. Keep it to two emails — more reads as begging. The relief is freshest the week the return is filed; a year later it's gone.

The honest catches

Your practice suite already runs the document layer — don't double-send. Intuit ProConnect, Lacerte, ProSeries, CCH Axcess, Drake, and UltraTax already handle organizers, engagement letters, e-sign, and client portals. Let them own the document workflow; let the email tool handle marketing campaigns, the deadline drips, the planning newsletter, onboarding, and review sequences. Check what your suite already automates before you pay for an email tool to do it twice. If the suite's client-communication engine is already strong, the email tool's job gets smaller — buy accordingly.

Client confidentiality constrains the copy. Marketing emails from a firm must never put client-specific financial details in subject lines or body copy — no amounts, no return status, no document names that identify a client's situation. The drips here are built generic on purpose: "your quarterly estimate is due" with a link to the payment portal, never "your $4,200 payment." Generic is a feature, not a compromise.

The exported client file is not the email list. Clients gave you their email for tax preparation, which is not the same as opting into marketing emails. The newsletter and the cross-sell campaigns go to the opted-in subset; the deadline reminders and document chases (messages about the engagement itself) are a different lane. Expect the marketing list to be smaller than the client roster (estimate — opt-in rates vary widely by firm); the campaigns here are built for that smaller, warmer list, which converts better anyway.

Segmentation is the tags; the tags are the work. Every tool here imports CSV; the work is exporting the client file and tagging it properly — entity type, engagement type, tax year status (estimate — most practice suites' exports won't have these tags out of the box). Budget an afternoon, not ten minutes. A list without entity and engagement tags can't run the deadline drip or the estimate series — the tags are the product. Our deliverability audit checklist covers the list-hygiene half.

The year-end window is now. If you're reading this in October, the planning newsletter should launch this month — the advisory revenue it generates is earned in Q4, not tax season. The deadline drip and document chase can be built after January with a full season's runway. Order the campaigns by the calendar, not by enthusiasm.

FAQ

What's the cheapest email tool for an accounting firm?

At typical firm list sizes (150–600 clients, estimate), MailerLite's Comfort plan is the cheapest of the five tools here with real automations included — from $12/mo at 500 subscribers (about $19/mo at 1,000, per October 2026 roundups). Brevo is cheaper if you blast the whole client file on a regular cadence ($9/mo for 5,000 emails). All prices checked October 5, 2026 — verify live before checkout.

Can I import my client list from my tax software?

Yes — every tool here takes a CSV export, and every practice suite exports one. The honest workflow for most firms is a scheduled CSV export and import, with entity type, engagement type, and tax-year tags added as custom fields. The document layer (organizers, engagement letters, portals) stays in the suite; the email tool only needs the marketing subset — the clients who opted into marketing emails.

Do I need an email tool if my practice suite already sends client messages?

Yes — for different jobs. The suite (ProConnect, Lacerte, CCH Axcess, Drake, UltraTax) owns the document workflow: organizers, engagement letters, e-sign, portals. The email tool owns marketing: the newsletter, the deadline drips, the planning series, the onboarding sequence, the review ask. Different tools, different jobs — run both layers.

When should the deadline drip start?

Weeks before the date, not days: the organizer push in January, the S-corp reminder ~3 weeks before March 15, the individual push ~4 weeks before April 15, the quarterly-estimate nudge 10 days before each quarterly date. Pre-build the segments with entity and engagement tags; writing the reminder the week of the deadline means the client already filed late or already filed with someone else. The calendar repeats forever — template it once.

Are Mailchimp or Constant Contact fine for accounting firms?

Both work, both cost more than the picks above at real list sizes, and both are built for generic small business rather than this niche. Mailchimp's free tier was cut in January 2026 to 250 contacts and 500 emails/month, and Essentials starts at $13/mo for 500 contacts. See Mailchimp alternatives for the migration math.

What about bookkeeping or advisory clients?

Same engine, different campaigns — monthly bookkeeping clients need the value-reminder (the "here's what we did for you this month" email), and advisory clients are the perfect audience for the year-end planning series. Tag clients by engagement type and the whole playbook above transfers: the deadlines apply to everyone, the onboarding sequence covers every new engagement, and the review sequence works after any delivered engagement.
