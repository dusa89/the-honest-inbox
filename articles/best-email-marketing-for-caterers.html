A caterer sells a product that doesn't exist yet, for a date months in the future, to a buyer who will only ever see it once. No other trade in food has this shape: the restaurant feeds you tonight, the food truck finds you at lunch — but the caterer feeds 117 people (industry figure, labeled) on a date that was booked before the menu was written. The market math: IBISWorld puts the US catering services industry at roughly $15.7 billion in 2026 (market-research estimate, marked) — down about half a percent on the year after climbing at a 6.7% annual clip since 2021 — across some 12,000 establishments (industry profile, labeled), and the trade is brutally fragmented: the top 50 companies account for less than 15% of industry revenue (industry profile, labeled), which is the small caterer's moat. The money lane is weddings: The Knot's 2026 Real Weddings Study puts average wedding catering at $80 per head (industry figure, labeled), with couples paying $60 to $130 a head across the quartiles; 85% of couples hire a caterer, and at the study's average of 117 guests that works out to roughly $9,360 in catering per wedding (operator math on the study's figures, labeled as an estimate). And the tailwind is real: catering is growing faster than any other dining segment (industry report built on 2M+ annual orders, labeled). Every tool below is scored against the machine: the tasting-to-booking funnel, the wedding-season calendar, the corporate-holiday rush, the dietary-segment menu lane, and the venue-partner referral pipeline.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The tasting-to-booking funnel. Quote request → tasting invitation (the tasting is the close — most couples decide at the tasting, not in the quote) → the tasting-no-show rescue → the post-tasting follow-up (the menu-tweak email: "we can drop the station and add the carving board") → the attended-but-didn't-book objection sequence (price per head, date availability, food-and-beverage minimums). The operator pattern (estimate, labeled): the quote that hears nothing for seven days is dead — and almost no caterer sends a follow-up sequence, which is why the caterer who does wins the silent quotes.
  2. The wedding-season calendar. May through October is the peak; peak-season Saturday dates book 9 to 12+ months out (operator estimate, labeled); the availability nurture runs date-first ("your date, our calendar"); the off-season fill (January-March emails that move minimums, not prices) keeps the kitchen staffed through the slow months. Scored on: the date-first cadence, the lead-time honesty, and the off-season engine.
  3. The corporate-holiday rush. The recurring drop-off lunch (weekly or monthly office orders — the closest thing this trade has to a retainer), the Q4 holiday-party blast (the single biggest corporate season of the year), the office-manager nurture (the buyer is an office manager, not a couple), and the January kickoff reset (post-holiday re-engagement). Corporate is the recurring revenue; weddings are the high ticket; the list must hold both without cross-sends. Scored on: the recurring-order lane, the Q4 timing, and the buyer separation.
  4. The dietary-segment menu lane. Vegan, vegetarian, gluten-free, and allergen-conscious segments; the menu-update email per segment (the vegan couple doesn't want the carving-station blast); the allergen-statement honesty: the kitchen decides what the menu can claim, and the email never promises "allergen-free" — it links to the statement. Scored on: the segmentation depth, the menu-lane discipline, and the allergen honesty.
  5. The venue-partner referral pipeline. Wedding planners and venues send the couples: the partner newsletter (new menus, seasonal stations, capacity notes), the tasting-invites-for-planners lane (a planner who has eaten your food sells your food), the "your couple booked" feedback loop, and the co-hosted tasting events. The operator pattern (estimate, labeled): the planner who hears back sends the next couple; the venue that never gets a thank-you stops mentioning you. Scored on: the partner nurture, the feedback loop, and the event co-marketing.

2. GetResponse — the branching pick for the quote funnel

Best for: caterers with enough quote volume to need real branches on the objection sequences.

The branch arc earns this slot: GetResponse's automation branches the attended-but-didn't-book sequence by objection — the "price per head" family gets the stations-downshift variant, the "date availability" family gets the Friday-and-Sunday variant, the "minimums" family gets the off-season flexibility variant. 3. Brevo — the email-plus-SMS pick for day-of logistics

Best for: caterers whose margin leaks on logistics, not leads.

The day-of machine is SMS-shaped: the delivery-day text to the venue contact, driver coordination on the route, the last-minute headcount-change text, and the morning-of confirmation — from one list and one bill instead of a second subscription, on volume pricing. The trade-off: SMS carries logistics, not romance — the tasting invitation, the menu reveal, the tasting-follow-up all still belong in email. Buy Brevo for the logistics lane; don't ask it to do the emotional work.

4. Kit — the free-tier pick with an event-date ceiling

Kit's free tier — free to 10,000 subscribers — is effectively permanent for most single-location caterers, and its automation handles the newsletter and the basic tasting funnel fine. The ceiling is real, though: this business's money is event-date segmentation — event-month, menu package, corporate-vs-wedding lanes, tasted-didn't-book — and Kit's tagging is built for creators, not for a caterer segmenting by the month of a booked event. The bill past 10,000 subscribers ($139/mo) jumps harder than this trade's tool budget should. Honest note: if you're still on the free tier in year three, it's working. If you've outgrown it, MailerLite is the landing pad.

5. AWeber — the old-guard reliability pick

AWeber does everything this trade needs — newsletters, autoresponders, segments — and has done it for two decades without drama. It lands fifth because it's priced like the old guard while MailerLite does the same work cheaper and Brevo adds the SMS logistics lane. The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The quote-to-tasting funnel. The calendar-filling machine: the quote follow-up sequence (the three-touch nurture on day 1, day 4, and day 7 — operator estimate), the tasting invitation as the call to action (not "get a quote" — "book a tasting," because the tasting is the close), and the tasting-no-show rescue (the no-show is a lead, not a loss). The operator pattern (estimate, labeled): the quote-to-tasting funnel is the highest-ROI sequence in the business, and most caterers market the offer once and forget the tail.
  2. The public tasting event engine. The quarterly tasting as the lead magnet: the tasting-invite blast, the planner-invite lane (planners who have eaten the food sell the food), the attended-tasting nurture (the menu-tweak email within 48 hours), and the booked-couple upsell (the station upgrade, the late-night snack, the send-off brunch). The tasting is the only marketing event in this trade where the product is the pitch.
  3. The date-availability nurture. The couple booked a date with a venue; the caterer owns the calendar: the date-check sequence, the "your date, three availabilities" email, the off-season minimum-fill (January-March sends that move minimums, not prices), and the wait-list rescue when a peak date opens. The operator pattern (estimate, labeled): the date-availability email that names specific open dates converts; the generic "we're booking for 2027" email doesn't.
  4. The corporate Q4 holiday-party blast. The highest-revenue corporate quarter of the year: the October-November booking-window send (the window for December parties is September-November), the office-manager segmented lane (the buyer is the office manager — write to the person who signs the order), the repeat-office upsell (the client who ordered the summer picnic gets the holiday-party pitch), and the January kickoff reset. Run the big blast on the plan that survives the send cap.
  5. The post-event review machine. The review ask the week after the event (the couple is happiest; the office manager is relieved — never at the tasting, when everything is hypothetical), the planner-referral thank-you (the feedback loop that keeps the pipeline fed), and the testimonial capture with written consent. A review asked the week after the event carries the couple's real voice; a review asked at any other moment is a review never written.

The honest catches

  • The allergen line: never promise allergen-free in email. The allergen statement comes from the kitchen, not the newsletter — the email links to the statement, and it never promises what the line can't guarantee. A promise in a subject line is a liability in a lawsuit. The dietary-segment lane is an asset only when the kitchen owns the claims.
  • The tasting email is not a menu contract. Prices move, menus rotate, and minimums shift with the season — the quote email carries dates and expiry, and the campaign library gets refreshed when prices change. Stale per-head prices in old campaigns are how one-star reviews are born ("their email said $72").
  • Never market to the venue's guest list. The planner's referral is borrowed trust: the planner's clients can get your partner newsletter; the planner's list stays the planner's list. The partner pipeline runs on referrals and feedback loops, not on list-swaps nobody consented to.
  • Keep wedding prospects and corporate buyers on separate lanes. The couple planning a June wedding and the office manager ordering Friday lunch are different buyers at different speeds — a holiday-party blast to a bride in her wedding month is noise, and a tasting invitation to an office manager is a confused inbox. Two lists, two cadences, zero cross-sends. The operator pattern (estimate, labeled): the caterer that cross-sends teaches both audiences to ignore it.
  • The per-head price in the email must match the quote. When prices change, update the campaigns, not just the pricing page — the email is the promise the client keeps. How far in advance do couples book a caterer?
