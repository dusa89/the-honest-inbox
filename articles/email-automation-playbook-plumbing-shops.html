The prerequisite nobody mentions: the triggers live in your field software

Every automation below is a triggered email, and a triggered email needs an event. In plumbing, the events are: a job marked complete, an estimate sent, a plan member signed up, an install date recorded, a customer going quiet. All of those live in your field software — ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, Service Fusion, the established names in the trades CRM space — not in your email tool. Before you build flow #1, confirm the pipe: job-complete in the field software must tag the contact in the email tool (with the service type and the crew name), and estimate-sent must tag with the estimate date and amount. The guide names the integrations per tool; the wiring is your job or your field-software vendor’s. One judgment, labeled: shops that skip this and try to run the review engine by hand — the office manager remembering to add names — abandon it in six weeks. The automation has to fire from the software, or it is a chore, not a flow.

The 7 flows, wired

Build order follows plumbing economics, not the customer journey — the reviews first, the recurring revenue second, the nice-to-haves last. Each flow: the trigger, the sequence, the branching gates.

1. The job-complete review engine (trigger: job marked complete)

Trigger: the field software marks the job complete, and the contact gets tagged with the service type and the crew name. Sequence: email one at T+48 hours — the review ask: one paragraph, the direct Google review link, and the crew name in the subject line if the customer knew them (“How did [tech name] do?”). Email two at T+7 days — one reminder, different subject, same link. Then the flow STOPS — no third email, ever. Two asks is persistence; three is pestering, and a review you earned by nagging reads like one. The branching gates: an open complaint or a callback on the same ticket suppresses the flow entirely — asking for a review from a customer who is waiting for you to come back and fix it is how you manufacture one-star reviews. Review completed (the tag, if your tool tracks it, or the reply) exits the flow and enters flow #7. Contract members get the same flow — plan customers review more often because the relationship is ongoing, and the guide’s moat argument applies double to them. The honest mechanics: in a trade where the map pack is the front page, this is the highest-ROI email the shop sends — not because reviews are magic, but because the shops with 200+ reviews get picked and the shops with 12 do not, and the automated ask is the only way a busy office collects them consistently. No outcome is promised for any specific shop; reviews compound, but they compound from the base you already have.

2. The estimate-follow-up drip (trigger: estimate sent, no job booked)

Trigger: an estimate goes out and no job is booked within 24 hours. Sequence: email one at T+1 day — the objection-handler: restate the problem the tech found, the scope, and the price, in the customer’s words not the invoice’s. Most unbooked estimates are not price objections; they are “I need to think about it” and “I want a second quote” — the email answers the thinking, not the price. Email two at T+4 days — the seasonal tie-in: why this job is better done now (the heater before winter, the sewer line before the rains) than later. Email three at T+10 days — the final nudge: the estimate’s expiry date, one line, the booking link. The branching gates: a booked job exits instantly — the flow must never email someone who already said yes. A replied “not now” moves the contact to flow #6’s dormant track, not to silence. The honest mechanics: estimates die in the truck, not in the inbox — the customer asked for the number, which means the intent existed. The drip does not discount the work; it answers the hesitation. Build this second because a shop that books 2 more estimates a month out of the ones already written is buying the whole playbook’s tool bill with one flow.

3. The maintenance-contract ladder (trigger: plan signup at the job)

Trigger: the customer signs the annual plan — at the job, at the estimate, or from the website — and gets the plan-member tag. Sequence: email one at day 0 — the welcome-to-the-plan: what the plan actually covers (the inspection, the flush, the drain maintenance, the priority scheduling), in plain words, because most customers forget what they bought within a week. Email two at day 30 — the check-in: has anything come up, here is how to book priority, the number to call. Email three at month 6 — the midpoint: the mid-year reminder of the unused benefit (the inspection they have not scheduled is the most common), one booking link. Email four at month 11 — the renewal push: the plan renews next month, here is what it did for you this year (the visits, the priority calls), the renewal is automatic or one click. The branching gates: renewed exits back to the start of the ladder (the day-0 email becomes the year-2 welcome). Lapsed at renewal enters flow #6’s dormant track with the plan tag, not the general one. A service call mid-plan pauses the sequence — the check-in emails keep their schedule, but a customer with an active job does not need the month-6 email the same week. The honest mechanics: the guide calls the plan member the most profitable customer in the trade, and the ladder is why: recurring revenue in a one-and-done business. The 11-month email exists because customers do not renew what they forgot they had.

4. The water-heater age sequence (trigger: install-year tag from the job record)

Trigger: every water-heater install (yours or a recorded competitor install) gets tagged with the install year. Sequence: email one at the start of year 8 — the maintenance nudge: the flush, the anode check, the “heaters this age benefit from a look” framing — a service call, not a sales pitch. Email two at the start of year 9 — the honest pitch: “your heater is 9 years old” (the tag makes the subject line true), the signs of failure to watch for, the replacement quote offer. Email three at the start of year 10 — the final nudge: the failure-risk framing, the quote, the booking link. The branching gates: a quote requested exits to flow #2’s estimate drip immediately — this flow hands off, it does not duplicate. A replacement booked exits the whole sequence (and re-tags the install year to the new date — the 10-year clock restarts). A flush or service call booked pauses the pitch emails but keeps the age clock running. The honest mechanics: water heaters fail in waves — age plus hard water — which means the sequence is a calendar, not a guess. The guide names this as a campaign idea; the wiring is what makes it run without anyone remembering to check the spreadsheet. One line of judgment, labeled: the year-8 email must be a genuine service offer, not a disguised sales pitch, or the year-9 pitch reads as the setup it is.

5. The seasonal campaign engine (trigger: calendar segments, not behavior)

Trigger: the calendar, run against segments — not individual behavior. Three campaigns a year, each wired once and re-run: fall, the freeze-prep blast (segment: all customers in freeze zones; the pipe-insulation offer, the outdoor-spigot check, the “before the first hard freeze” framing); spring, the sump-pump check (segment: homes with sump pumps on record; the pre-storm test offer); rainy season, the drain push (segment: older neighborhoods, clay-pipe areas; the sewer-line inspection offer where the pipes are all the same age). The branching gates: booked exits each campaign instantly — nobody gets the freeze email twice after booking. Contract members are suppressed on campaigns their plan already covers (the plan includes the inspection; emailing them the paid version is how you teach plan members the plan is worthless). One campaign, one segment, one booking link — the segment is the targeting, and the guide’s point stands: tag the list by equipment and service history and the campaigns write themselves. The honest mechanics: these are broadcasts with automation discipline — the wiring is the segment, the suppression, and the exit, not the copy. Run them from the same tool as the flows so the suppressions actually suppress.

6. The dormant-customer reactivation (trigger: no job in 18–24 months)

Trigger: a past customer goes 18 months with no job and no plan activity. Sequence: email one — “we’re still your plumber”: the shop is still here, the number, the one-line reminder of the last job (“we replaced your water heater in 2023” — the tag makes it personal). Email two, two weeks later — the seasonal offer: whichever campaign from flow #5 is live, personalized to their record. Email three, two weeks after that — the last call: the honest “we’ll stop checking in” note. The branching gates: contract members never enter this flow — they have their own ladder (flow #3) and their own lapsed track. A booked job exits to flow #1’s review engine like any new job. After email three with no response, the contact moves to the annual-only cadence — the holiday card and the big seasonal blast, not the monthly sends. The honest mechanics: the list is already paid for — every past customer on it cost a truck roll to acquire — so reactivation is the cheapest revenue on the account. The guide names this as a GetResponse feature-bullet phrase; the wiring is the 18-month trigger, the three emails, and the annual-only landing.

7. The referral + neighbor track (trigger: job complete, review done)

Trigger: the customer completes the review flow (#1) — a review was left, or the two asks expired. Sequence: one email at T+7 days after the review — the neighbor angle: “we just finished a job on [street/neighborhood]” (the tag makes it true), the referral ask, the one-line incentive if you run one (the discount on the next service call is the standard; state the terms). Then it STOPS — one ask, not a drip. The branching gates: a referral recorded (the new customer names them, or the referral code) enters the thank-you track: the thank-you email, the incentive delivered, and — this is the part shops skip — the report-back (“your neighbor booked with us, thank you”), which is what makes the second referral happen. No referral after the single ask: the contact returns to the normal cadence. Never ask a customer who left a negative review — the complaint-suppression branch from flow #1 applies here too. The honest mechanics: nobody picks a plumber from an ad; they pick the shop their neighbor recommended. The referral is the highest-trust acquisition in the trade, and the automation’s job is only to make the ask happen at the moment trust is highest — right after the good review. One ask, well-timed, beats a referral program nobody remembers to mention.

The build order, with the money math

Build in this order: the review engine (flow #1), the estimate drip (flow #2), the contract ladder (flow #3), the water-heater sequence (flow #4), the seasonal engine (flow #5), reactivation (flow #6), the referral track (flow #7). The ranking is plumbing economics: reviews feed the map pack that feeds every other flow; estimates are revenue already half-written; the plan is the recurring machine; the rest compounds. One line of judgment, labeled: most shops build the seasonal blasts first because they are the easiest to understand, and leave the review engine — the flow that makes every future customer cheaper to win — for “later,” which is the most expensive procrastination in the trade. The estimate drip second for the same reason: quotes already written are the warmest revenue on the books.

Five mistakes that cost more than the tool

  • Building the blasts before the review engine. The seasonal campaigns are the easiest flow to understand, so they get built first — and the review engine, the flow that makes every future customer cheaper to win, waits for “later.” Reviews first, always.
  • The third review ask. Two asks is persistence; three is pestering. The flow stops at two, and the complaint-suppression branch is not optional — one automated review request to an angry customer costs more than the review was worth.
  • Emailing plan members the paid version of what their plan covers. The seasonal engine suppresses contract members on campaigns the plan includes. Break this rule once and the plan member learns the plan is worthless — the most expensive email a shop can send.
  • Running the flows by hand. The office manager adding names to the review list works for three weeks. The triggers must fire from the field software — job-complete, estimate-sent, install-year — or the playbook is a chore, not a system.
  • Asking for the referral before the review. The referral ask lands at peak trust — right after the good review, not before it. Sequence matters: review first (flow #1), referral second (flow #7). Reversed, both asks feel transactional.
