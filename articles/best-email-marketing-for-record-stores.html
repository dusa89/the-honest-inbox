A record store doesn't sell plastic — it sells the Friday. The industry numbers are extraordinary: U.S. vinyl sales hit 47.9 million albums in 2025, up 8.6% — the 19th consecutive year of growth (Luminate, via Oct 2026 coverage) — with 26.5 million units in just the first half of 2026, up 20.9% year over year (RIAA mid-2026 report). And here's the part that matters to a shop owner: 4 in 10 of those records were purchased at independent record stores. The audience is getting younger, not older — 21% of Gen Z music listeners bought vinyl in the previous 12 months, against 14% of Millennials and Gen X and 9% of Boomers (Luminate 2024 U.S. Music 360). The ritual is the business: "people want to take ownership of what they listen to, connect even more closely with the artists who make the music they love, spend the time to focus on the ritual aspects of engaging with physical records. And they want to do it at a locally owned store where their neighbors and friends are the algorithm" — that's Carrie Colliton, co-founder of Record Store Day, in April 2026, paraphrased. Then the holiday: Record Store Day 2026 was held April 18, 2026; in 2025 the week around the event moved 1.2 million albums, just over a million of them vinyl (Luminate) — the fifth straight year the event cleared a million. Every tool below is scored against that machinery: the Friday drop, the want-list alerts, the RSD calendar, the in-store shows, and the collector community.

What this guide scores

Every service guide on this site is scored against the service's real revenue machinery — not a generic feature grid. The five engines:

  1. The drop-day engine. New arrivals land every Friday; the email announcing what's in the bins IS the product for half the list. Scored on: fast visual sends, genre segmentation, and pre-release hype workflows.
  2. The want-list / special-order machine. The collector brings a want list; the store that finds the grail and emails first wins the sale. Scored on: per-customer profiles, triggered alerts, and special-order confirmation flows.
  3. The Record Store Day machinery. One Saturday in April that concentrates a month's attention: the pre-RSD hype drip, the exclusive-release list, the in-store-day queue capture. Scored on: multi-week campaign sequences, day-of alerts, and list capture at the door.
  4. The discovery/crate-dig engine. A fifth of Gen Z is buying vinyl but doesn't yet know the bins — they need staff picks and education, not just a stock list. Scored on: content-rich send formats, staff-pick workflows, and used-bin storytelling.
  5. The collector-community engine. In-store shows, listening nights, the regulars who buy every week — the list is the scene. Scored on: event RSVPs, loyalty segments, and re-engagement of lapsed regulars.

The ranking

1. MailerLite — the value pick with the visual sends a drop-day shop needs

Best for: shops running the Friday drop, the want-list alerts, and the in-store calendar on one honest tool.

Record drops are sold on covers, and MailerLite's templates make the Friday new-arrivals email and the used-bin treasure email look expensive. The automation builder handles the whole machine — the genre-segmented drop alerts, the special-order confirmation sequence, the post-purchase "rate your crate" follow-up. Pricing stays gentle at record-shop list sizes: Comfort at 500 subscribers is $12/mo (5,000 sends, the 10x send cap), 1,000 subs $19/mo, 2,500 subs $33/mo, 5,000 subs $49/mo, and the free tier (250 subs / 2,500 emails) genuinely covers a small shop while it builds the list. The honest catch: drop-day weeks and Record Store Day — the blasts that go to the whole list with heavy images — can trip the 10x Comfort send cap, because a record store's email rhythm is spiky by nature. Price the tool at RSD-season send volume, not average list size; Power (from $25/mo, removes the cap) exists for the spike weeks.

2. GetResponse — the automation-depth pick for the want-list machine

Best for: shops that run real collector programs and want every want-list alert and genre segment automated to the hilt.

Starter at $19/mo (sender) or Marketer/Creator at $59–$69/mo (platform). The automation builder is the strongest on this list for multi-branch sequences — different want-list alert ladders for jazz collectors vs punk kids vs the used-bin regulars, conditional on purchase history and genre tags. That branching is exactly what the want-list machine wants: the grail-find alert firing to exactly the collector who asked for it, the reissue pre-order sequence running off street date instead of a clerk's memory. The honest demerit: GetResponse's famous webinar and funnel machinery is dead weight for a record shop — you're not hosting webinars for collectors — so you're paying platform prices for the automation half. If the collector program genuinely runs itself, the half earns it; if the whole program is the Friday drop plus RSD, MailerLite wins on price.

3. Brevo — the email-plus-SMS pick for the reserved-copy text

Best for: shops that want the "your copy is in" texts and the drop-day emails in one place.

Collectors answer their phones: the "your special order arrived — holding it until Saturday" text, the RSD-morning "the line starts at 8" alert, the in-store-show reminder that actually gets opened. Brevo bundles email and SMS, prices email on volume (friendly for small collector lists), and the CRM-lite keeps the want list visible. Honest check: SMS is the scalpel here, not the channel — the Friday drop stays email, and every marketing text needs real consent. Use text for reserved copies, day-of alerts, and show reminders; never for the genre newsletter itself.

4. Kit — the simplest path to a Friday email that actually ships

Best for: shops whose whole program is the Friday drop and a want-list sequence.

Free to 10,000 subscribers at $0, then the bill jumps hard ($139/mo past 10,000) — but a record shop's list is hundreds of collectors, not tens of thousands, so the free tier is effectively permanent. The trade: Kit's automations are thinner than MailerLite's and much thinner than GetResponse's — the want-list branching gets clunky beyond a few genre tags, and the multi-week RSD campaign is where the ceiling shows. If the business is "Friday drop + special-order alerts," Kit is the cheapest way to look professional doing it. If the collector program is genuinely segmented (genre tribes, used-bin regulars, reissue pre-orders), the ceiling arrives fast.

5. AWeber — the reliable old guard, priced like it

Best for: shops that want a known quantity and will pay for it.

Long track record, real deliverability reputation — and in a business where the RSD-morning email has to land at 7am sharp or the line is already chaos, deliverability counts. But the plan structure is built for bigger consumer lists than a record shop runs, and the automation builder lags the top two for segmentation work. Fine if you inherit it; not the pick if you're choosing fresh in 2026.

6. Flodesk — the design pick (editorial mention, no affiliate program)

Best for: the Friday drop — nothing on this list makes a new-arrivals email prettier.

In a business sold on covers, Flodesk's templates are the prettiest money can buy, and pretty has real revenue value when you're competing for the Gen Z collector's attention. But it's the template trap, exactly like the one this site named in the Flodesk-vs-MailerLite piece: the automation is too shallow for the want-list machine, the RSD calendar, or the genre-segmentation work. If the entire email program is one gorgeous Friday drop and the counter handles everything else by hand, it's defensible. For the full drop-day engine, it isn't a contender. The 5 campaigns that pay

  1. The Friday new-arrival drop. Every Friday, same time: this week's arrivals, cover-forward, genre-segmented — the jazz collectors get the Blue Note reissues, the punk kids get the 7-inches. This is the ritual Colliton described: the neighbors-and-friends algorithm, delivered. Estimate: our operator-pattern estimate is that shops publishing a weekly drop on a fixed schedule see their regulars train themselves to check it — treat it as pattern, not a study.
  2. The want-list alert. The grail-find email — one record, one collector, personal. "Your copy of [record] just walked in" is the highest-converting email a record shop can send, because the want list already contains the buy decision. The automation: want-list intake form → staff tags the record on intake → alert fires to that collector only → 48-hour hold, then the general bin. Never blast the want-list alert to the whole list; scarcity that reaches the wrong buyer is just noise.
  3. The Record Store Day 6-week machinery. The 2025 event moved just over a million vinyl records in a week — your share of that depends on April, not April 18. Six weeks out: the exclusive-release preview list (what's coming, why it matters). Two weeks out: the plan email (open time, line policy, one-per-customer rules). Day-of: the door-list capture — every person in line is a contact before they buy a single record. The week after: the "what's left" restock email to the whole list. The shops that win RSD sell it for a month; the shops that don't just staff the register.
  4. The used-bin treasure digest. Used inventory is one-of-one — the thrift-store truth this site has already documented: advertise the genre, not the item, because the item may be gone by open. The staff-picks format solves it: "this week's five crate-digger finds" with photos, prices, and the honest rule that email subscribers see it Thursday night, everyone else sees it Friday. The used bins are where margin hides, and the digest is how the margin moves.
  5. The in-store show + listening-night calendar. Shows and listening nights are the moat Amazon can't copy — the indie-bookstore article on this site made the same point about author events. Monthly, to the whole list; RSVP via the email, reminders by SMS if you run Brevo. The show sells records that night; the calendar sells the shop every night.

The honest catches

  • The RSD million isn't yours. Luminate's 1.2-million-album RSD week (just over a million vinyl) measures industry sales — thousands of stores split it. Your shop's share is won by the six-week machinery above, not by the event existing. Plan the campaign, not the hope.
  • Used inventory is one-of-one: advertise the genre, not the item. A sold-by-Friday grail emailed Thursday night to the whole list is a broken promise to everyone who shows up Saturday. The want-list alert goes to the collector; the general digest advertises genres, staff picks, and what's in the bins category-wise. One-of-one items in mass email is how you train subscribers to stop believing you.
  • The door list is the asset; get it on RSD. The one morning a year when the queue is guaranteed is the one morning the list-capture must work: tablet at the door, "exclusives-first alert list" framing, five seconds per person. Everything else on this page is cheaper when the list is bigger.
  • Venue and label inboxes have real filters. The distributor-and-label lane (restock requests, exclusive allocations, show bookings) lands in inboxes with real spam filtering. Authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) is non-optional before the first industry newsletter — run the per-tool setup in our email authentication guide first, not after the first complaint. An allocation email that lands in spam is a restock that never happens.
  • Free tiers last longer here — and that's a trap of its own. A 500-person list of collectors sits inside MailerLite's free tier (250 subs) or Kit's (10,000) for a long time. The trap: building the want-list machine on a free plan's automation ceiling, then discovering the genre-branching can't run when the collector program needs it. Start free, but pick the tool whose paid automations fit the 5-year collector program, not the 2-month one.
  • The bins are the closer; email is the invitation. Nobody drives across town for an email — they drive for the ritual of the bins, the listening bar, the show. Email's job is getting people into the building and making sure the regulars never miss a Friday. The shop that tries to sell the whole crate by email loses to the shop whose email got the collector in the door.

FAQ

How often should a record store email?

Weekly minimum, genre-segmented. The Friday new-arrival drop is the heartbeat — collectors train themselves around a fixed schedule. Add the want-list alerts as they fire (transactional in spirit), the RSD six-week machinery once a year, and the in-store calendar monthly. Daily is too much unless it's RSD week; monthly is too little to hold the ritual.

Does email work for used inventory?

Yes — with the one-of-one discipline. Advertise genres, staff picks, and the Thursday-night subscriber preview; send specific grails only as want-list alerts to the collector who asked. The store that blasts "just in: original pressing" to 500 people for a single copy teaches 499 people to ignore its email.

How far ahead should Record Store Day emailing start?

Six weeks. The 2025 event's million-vinyl week was the payoff of stores that started selling the exclusive list in early March, not the ones that woke up on April 17. Preview the exclusives, send the plan email, capture the door list, then send the "what's left" restock email the week after.

SMS or email for reserved copies?

Both, different jobs. The "your copy arrived" alert goes by SMS — it's time-sensitive and personal. The Friday drop and the genre newsletters stay email — they're browseable and visual. Every marketing text needs real consent; the transactional special-order text is the safest SMS a shop sends.

What's the first automation a record shop should build?

The want-list intake → alert → hold sequence. It's the highest-converting flow in the business because the buy decision is already made; the automation just removes the clerk's memory from the chain. The Friday drop can be a manual send for a year. The want-list machine should be automated from week one.
