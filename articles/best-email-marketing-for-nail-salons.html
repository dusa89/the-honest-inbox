A nail salon's business is the most visually urgent repeat cycle in services. Gel and acrylic fills run every two to three weeks — and unlike a haircut, grow-out shows: the gap at the cuticle is a social embarrassment, not just a shaggy edge. "Clients come back every two to four weeks... retention is more important than constant new customer acquisition," per a 2026 nail-salon business-tools roundup — and that makes the email list a retention machine, not a marketing experiment. But the nail shop has two wrinkles no other vertical on this site has. First, the prepay package machine: fill packages and pedicure memberships sold at the register — revenue collected before the service is delivered, renewed and topped up by email. Nobody else on this site's verticals sells money ahead of the appointment. Second, the walk-in blind spot: a walk-in-heavy shop has no booking app at all — no client file, no visit history, no reminder engine. The email tool's first job isn't inheriting the client from the booking stack; it's capturing her at the register. This guide covers the five email engines that fill the tables — the fill-cycle engine, the prepay package machine, the gift-card wave, the trend-drop machine, and the review moat — plus the capture question to settle before you spend a dollar. Scored below are the tools that survive contact with a shop owner who does marketing between clients.

What a nail salon actually needs from email

Forget "engagement metrics." The five engines that keep the tables full:

  1. The fill-cycle engine. The engine no other vertical on this site has in this form. Gel and acrylic fills run every two to three weeks, and the visible grow-out is the trigger — the client who sees the gap at her cuticle is already looking for a reason to book; she doesn't need a coupon, she needs the nudge. The mechanics: the service date goes in (from the booking app or the front desk), and the reminder fires at week two-and-a-half for the fill client, week three for the gel-X client, week four for the pedicure regular. This is not a newsletter; it is a calendar with a send button. "Every two to four weeks" for the visit cycle, per the 2026 roundup — the most predictable return cycle in beauty, and the most urgent, because grow-out shows.
  2. The prepay package machine. The wrinkle no other vertical has. Fill packages (four fills, one price) and pedicure memberships (monthly) are sold at the register — revenue collected before the service is delivered. Email is what sells the package ("your package has one fill left — top up before it lapses"), what renews the membership (the renewal sequence starting two weeks before expiry), and what upgrades the client (the gel client nudged to the builder-gel package). The package is the highest-margin sale in the shop; the email tool is what keeps it from lapsing silently.
  3. The gift-card wave. Gift cards are the nail industry's largest single revenue season — and they are sold almost entirely through the list. The mechanics: the holiday push (the list gets the gift-card offer first, with the in-store purchase second), the birthday-season wave (the client's own birthday and every birthday she buys for), the Mother's Day / Valentine's / graduation drops. The list doesn't just market the gift card — it is the gift-card storefront. One good December email beats a year of promos.
  4. The trend-drop machine. Nails are visual — "social media drives discovery," as the roundup puts it. But Instagram is rented land: the follower who liked last month's chrome-nails post owes you nothing tomorrow. The mechanics: the seasonal drop email (fall tones, holiday sets, chrome season, summer brights) that converts the like into the booking, the "new art just landed" blast with the portfolio photos, the limited-season sets that create the book-ahead. Discovery happens on Instagram; the booking happens from the email. Convert the followers into subscribers and the trends into appointments.
  5. The review moat. Walk-in traffic lives on "nail salon near me" — and the map pack is won on Google reviews. A 2026 business-tools roundup puts it bluntly: reviews directly impact foot traffic for nail salons. The mechanics: the post-visit review ask (day of visit, while the nails still look perfect — with the direct Google link), the standing ask for first-timers, the monthly sweep of the happy segment. The shops that ask systematically pull away from the shops that hope. Pair the review ask with the fill-cycle capture (below): every new walk-in who joins the list enters the review engine on visit one.

Notice what's missing: no blast calendars, no lead scoring, no abandoned-cart flows. The booking stack (GlossGenius, Booksy, Boulevard and the class) already holds the appointment, the reminder, and increasingly the basic blast. The email tool only has to hold the client list, fire the fill cadences, sell and renew the packages, sell the gift cards, drop the trends, and run the reviews. Paying for a second booking system is the trap — and buying the email tool before settling how the client gets captured (below) is the bigger one.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a nail salon leaves on the table:

  1. The fill cadence. Trigger: the service date + the client's own cycle, on a timer. The acrylic client hears at week two-and-a-half, the gel-X client at three, the pedicure regular at four. One email: "it's week three — your nails are showing it. Your table's open Thursday." No discount needed — the visible grow-out IS the offer. This is the highest-ROI automation on the page, and it runs forever once built.
  2. The package machine. Trigger: the package count, on a timer. Email 1 (one fill remaining): "one fill left on your package — top up before it lapses." Email 2 (lapsed): the renewal offer with the math restated (four fills at the package price vs the single-fill price). Email 3 (for non-package clients): the package pitch after the third visit — by visit three the fill pattern is proven, so sell the package on the proven cycle. Revenue ahead of the service, renewed by email.
  3. The gift-card wave. Trigger: the season, manual. Email 1 (early December): the gift-card push to the full list — "the gift she actually wants." Email 2 (week before Christmas): the last-chance digital card. The birthday-season version: the client's own birthday reminder and the Mother's Day / Valentine's / graduation drops. The list is the gift-card storefront — treat December like a product launch.
  4. The trend drop. Trigger: the season, manual. Email 1: the seasonal set ("fall tones just landed — book before the trend peaks") with the portfolio photos. The Instagram-to-inbox version: every nail-art post points followers to the email list for "the drop list" — convert the rented audience into an owned one. Discovery happens on Instagram; the booking happens from the email.
  5. The walk-in capture + review ask. Trigger: the first visit, at the register. Email 0 (in person): the front-desk signup — "want the fill reminder text? drop your email" — every walk-in enters the list before she leaves the shop. Email 1 (day of visit + 2 hours): the review ask with the direct Google link — the map-pack play for "nail salon near me," asked while the nails still look perfect. The capture builds the file the walk-in shop never had; the review ask wins the next walk-in.

The walk-in blind spot: capture the client before you automate her. A walk-in-heavy shop has no booking app at all — no client file, no visit history, no reminder engine. The email tool's first job is capture, not automation: the front-desk QR at the register, the "fill reminder" pitch that trades the email for a real service (the client wants the reminder — week three matters to her too), the receipt-footer signup. The shops that email only the booked clients are emailing half their revenue. Build the capture habit first; the automations have someone to fire at afterward. The review ask is the map-pack play. SMS stops the no-show; email fills the table. Don't confuse the channels. Booksy's 2,000 free SMS marketing messages a month cover reminders and flash fills — texts get read, emails get batched. FAQ

What's the cheapest email tool for a nail salon?

At typical shop list sizes (a few hundred past clients — estimate), MailerLite's Comfort plan is the cheapest of the six tools here with real automations included — from $12/mo at 500 subscribers (about $19/mo at 1,000, per October 2026 roundups). Brevo's Starter is $9/mo for 5,000 emails, but the automations that run this page's engines start at Standard $18/mo. All prices checked October 6, 2026 — verify live before checkout.

My booking app already sends marketing — do I need a separate email tool?

Maybe not. GlossGenius ships AI-powered marketing with unlimited messages, Booksy ships text/email blasts plus 2,000 free SMS a month, and Boulevard runs campaigns without exporting anywhere. If those cover your whole program — promos, reminders, basic automations — you may be done. The separate email tool earns its seat when the stack can't do the per-client fill cadences, the package renewals and top-ups, and the gift-card waves with real branching. Audit what your stack sends before you buy a second seat.

Most of my clients walk in — how do I build an email list?

That's the walk-in blind spot, and it's the first thing to fix. The front-desk QR at the register is the whole game: pitch the fill reminder as the service ("want a reminder when week three hits? drop your email") — the client wants it too. Add the receipt-footer signup and a small incentive only if the QR stalls. Every walk-in who joins enters the fill-cycle engine and the review engine on visit one. The shops that email only their booked clients are emailing half their revenue.

How often should a nail salon email its list?

Let the cycles decide, not a calendar. The fill reminders fire per client on their own cycle (two to four weeks) — that's one timely email, not a blast. On top of that, the monthly trend drop, the gift-card waves in season, and package top-ups as they trigger. The shops that annoy their lists are the ones blasting weekly coupons to everyone; the shops that win send the right email to the right client at the right week. Start with the fill engine and the walk-in capture, add promos sparingly.
