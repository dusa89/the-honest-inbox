What a dog daycare or boarding kennel actually needs from email

Forget "open rates." The five engines that decide whether the suites and the playgroups fill:

  1. The stay-calendar engine. The holiday book-ahead. Thanksgiving and Christmas are the two enrollment events of the daycare year — they book out, and the owner who books early books with the facility that asked first. The engine: the 90-day runway before each holiday (the early-bird book-ahead), the "suites filling" scarcity updates (honest, counted), the waitlist capture when the holiday sells out (the waitlist is next year's book-ahead list), and the shoulder-season fill (the January and September slumps after the holidays). Scored on the tools: can it run a date-triggered countdown sequence without babysitting?
  2. The daycare-pack ladder. The recurring-revenue machine. A daycare pack — 10 or 20 prepaid days — is the kennel's version of a membership: the visits are sold before the dog walks in, the pack-expiry date is the built-in rebook trigger, and the pack buyer is the cheapest customer to keep. The engine: the pack-sale campaigns (the new-year resolution pack, the summer pack), the expiry winback (the pack ends in 3 days — the automated nudge), the pack-size upgrade ladder (the 10-pack buyer becomes the 20-pack buyer), and the lapsed-daycare winback (the dog that hasn't been in 60 days is the cheapest re-acquisition on the list). Once the pack ladder runs, the daycare schedule starts filling itself.
  3. The grooming + retail attach. The margin engine. The stay is the commodity; the add-ons are the profit: the bath-before-pickup upsell on every boarding stay (the dog goes home clean — the highest-converting add-on in the business), the nail-trim attach on daycare days, the retail shelf (food, toys, treats — the impulse buy at pickup), and the seasonal services (the holiday photo package, the deshed in spring). Scored on the tools: can it send the right add-on offer to the right stay type — boarders get the bath offer, daycare regulars get the retail drop?
  4. The webcam-proof machine. The trust engine. A boarding customer is leaving a family member with strangers — the sale is trust, and trust is visual. The engine: the report-card emails (the day's playgroup photo with a one-line note), the webcam invite (the live camera that lets the owner peek at lunch — the single strongest trust signal the industry has), the review-engineered aftermath (the healed-reveal moment is the photo after the bath: send the review ask with the clean-dog photo), and the new-owner nurture (the first-stay follow-up that turns a trial into a regular). The groomers guide covers the before/after photo machine for the appointment business; the kennel's proof machine runs on the stay — the webcam, the report card, the pickup photo.
  5. The trial-day pipeline. The conversion event. The nervous first-time owner does not buy a 20-pack — they buy one trial day. The engine: the trial-day inquiry follow-up (the response within the hour), the vaccine-on-file onboarding (the compliance step that is also the commitment device), the post-trial report (the photo, the playgroup note, the "she did great" — the emotional close), and the regular-schedule offer (the trial graduates to the weekly slot, the pack, the holiday book-ahead). Scored on the tools: can it run a one-week conversion sequence triggered by the trial day without manual sends?

Notice what's missing: no generic "visit our beautiful facility!" blasts, no Groupon-style discount spam that trains owners to wait for the deal. The holiday calendar does the enrollment work, the pack ladder does the recurring-revenue work, the attach engine does the margin work, the webcam does the trust work, and the trial-day pipeline does the conversion work. A kennel whose only email is the holiday greeting is running a record-demand business on a ghost-town playbook.

The 5 campaigns that pay (steal this sequence)

Whichever tool you pick, build these five first. They cover the revenue a kennel leaves on the table:

  1. The holiday book-ahead. Trigger: the holiday, 90 days out, automated. The early-bird announcement (the first ask — the owner who books early books with you), the 60-day "suites filling" update (counted, honest scarcity), the 30-day last-call for the procrastinators, and the waitlist capture when it sells out (the waitlist is next year's book-ahead list — the facility that captures the waitlist owns next year's holiday too). Run it for Thanksgiving, run it for Christmas, and watch what "the holiday is full" does to your October.
  2. The pack-expiry winback. Trigger: the pack balance, automated. The 3-days-left nudge (the friendly reminder, not the pressure), the expired-pack winback at 14 days (the "we miss her" with the playgroup photo), the 60-day lapsed-daycare rescue (the dog that hasn't been in two months is the cheapest re-acquisition on the list). The pack ladder only compounds if the winback catches the packs that fall off — the kennel whose packs quietly expire is leaking recurring revenue every month.
  3. The bath-before-pickup attach. Trigger: the boarding stay, automated. The pre-arrival offer (the bath added at booking), the mid-stay upsell (the 3-day boarding stay gets the "add a bath for pickup day?" message), and the daycare version (the nail-trim attach on the regular daycare day). One attach per stay, offered once, at the right moment — the kennel that spams every add-on on every email trains owners to ignore all of them.
  4. The trial-day pipeline. Trigger: the trial day, automated. The vaccine-on-file onboarding (the compliance step that doubles as the commitment device), the post-trial report (the photo, the playgroup note — the emotional close), the regular-schedule offer at 3 days (the trial graduates to the weekly slot or the pack), and the no-show rescue if the trial owner never books (the "what held you back?" ask at 14 days). The trial day is the conversion event — the pipeline that follows it decides whether it converts.
  5. The webcam-proof nurture. Trigger: the new-owner signup, automated. The welcome sequence (the facility tour photos, the staff intros, the playgroup philosophy), the webcam invite (the live camera — "peek at lunch" — the trust moment), the first-stay follow-up (the pickup photo, the report card, the review ask riding the clean-dog glow), and the referral ask (the owner whose dog just had a great stay knows other dog owners — the ask comes while the glow is warm). Trust content is not a newsletter — it's the first thing a nervous owner sees.

The honest catches

Your kennel software already sends the transactional layer. Don't double-buy it. This is the kennel's version of the rented-audience trap. Gingr ($114/mo), Time To Pet ($35/mo), ProPet ($47.99/mo) and the rest already send the booking confirmation, the intake and vaccine reminders, the billing, the daycare check-in. Those are not this tool's job. The email tool owns the holiday calendar, the pack ladder, the winbacks, and the proof content. Audit what your stack already sends before you pay for an email tool to send it twice — and note the quieter trap: the client data living in your kennel software's ecosystem is portable only if you keep your own list. The owned list is the register capture, the trial-day signup, and the holiday waitlist.

Thanksgiving is perishable — don't discount it. The holiday suite sells itself; the weekly "holiday deal" blast trains owners to wait for the coupon on the inventory that was going to sell anyway — and you can never un-train them. Deals belong in the shoulder-season fill (the January and September slumps) and in the first-trial offer for a brand-new owner. The Thanksgiving suite does not need a coupon; it needs a waitlist. If the holiday book needs discounting, that's a demand problem, not an email problem.

Two buyers, two lists — segment or annoy. The daycare regular and the once-a-year holiday boarder are different customers buying different things. The regular does not want the "first-trial free day" offer; the holiday boarder does not want the weekly daycare newsletter. One list, one voice, and you'll burn both. The minimum viable segmentation is daycare-regulars vs. boarders vs. trial-contacts — three segments, three voices. The kennel that emails the daycare regulars a first-trial coupon is telling its best customers they're worth less than strangers.

The franchise down the road has the ad budget. You have the list. PetSmart PetsHotel, Dogtopia, Camp Bow Wow — the chains held ~15% of the market in 2025 (GM Insights) and they outspend you on every ad channel. You cannot out-bid them and you should not try. What they cannot buy is the owner relationship: the report card with the dog's photo, the handler who knows the dog's name, the holiday book-ahead that arrives before the franchise's generic blast. The list is the independent's moat — build it like one.
