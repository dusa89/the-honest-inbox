The prerequisite nobody mentions: the triggers live in your field software

Every automation below is a triggered email, and a triggered email needs an event. In HVAC, the events are: a visit marked complete, an estimate sent, an agreement signed, an install recorded with the system’s install year, a customer going quiet. All of those live in your field software — ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, FieldEdge, the established names in the HVAC trades CRM space — not in your email tool. Before you build flow #1, confirm the pipe: job-complete in the field software must tag the contact in the email tool (with the visit type and the tech’s name), estimate-sent must tag with the estimate date and amount, and — the one that matters most in this trade — every install record must carry the install year as a tag, because the aging-system drip (flow #4) is a clock, not a guess. One more rule the guide states and this page enforces: the field software already sends appointment confirmations, on-my-way notifications, and invoice reminders. Do not double-send. The email tool owns campaigns, pre-books, review sequences, and the aging-system drip; the field software owns the transactional job emails. Shops that try to run the review engine by hand — the office manager remembering to add names — abandon it in six weeks. The triggers must fire from the software, or the playbook is a chore, not a system. And the guide’s tag triad stands for the whole file: system age/install year, maintenance-plan status, last service date — three tags run every flow below, and every tool on this page handles them.

The 7 flows, wired

Build order follows HVAC economics, not the customer journey — the reviews first, the slow-calendar filler second, the recurring revenue third, the five-figure replacements fourth, the protections last. Each flow: the trigger, the sequence, the branching gates.

1. The post-visit review engine (trigger: job marked complete)

Trigger: the field software marks the visit complete, and the contact gets tagged with the visit type and the tech’s name. Sequence: email one the next day — the thank-you plus the direct Google review link, and the tech’s photo if the visit had a visible fix (the guide’s point stands: the photo does the selling). Email two two weeks later if no review landed — one gentle nudge, different subject, same link. Then the flow STOPS — no third email, ever. Two asks is persistence; three is pestering. The branching gates: an open complaint, a callback, or a “no-heat” follow-up on the same ticket suppresses the flow entirely — asking for a review from a customer who is waiting for you to come back and fix it is how you manufacture one-star reviews. Review completed exits the flow and enters flow #7’s referral track. Plan members get the same flow — agreement customers review more often because the relationship is ongoing, and the moat applies double to them. The honest mechanics: in a trade where the buying decision is a stranger choosing between three five-star profiles, this is the highest-ROI email the shop sends. The shop with 400 reviews wins the phone call before the estimate even happens; the shop with 12 never gets the call. No outcome is promised for any specific shop; reviews compound, but they compound from the base you already have.

2. The shoulder-season tune-up pre-book (trigger: calendar, twice a year)

Trigger: the calendar, run against segments — not individual behavior. Two campaigns a year, each built once as a reusable template and re-run: spring, the AC tune-up pre-book (send early April in most climates — 3 to 4 weeks before the weather turns, not during it: one offer, the ~$89 preseason special (estimate), one booking link); fall, the furnace tune-up pre-book (send early September: same shape, “before the first cold snap” framing). Build the templates during the slow month before each season (March and August) — the guide’s timing rule stands: the spring campaign sent in June is a wasted blast. The branching gates: segment priority by system age — the oldest systems get the campaign first, because every tune-up visit is a truck-roll that inspects an aging system (the failing capacitor, the 12-year-old unit that should be on flow #4’s drip), so book the campaign, then tell your techs the goal of the visit is a thorough inspection, not just a filter change. Maintenance-plan members are suppressed on the general pre-book — their tune-ups are already included in the agreement, and emailing them the paid version teaches them the agreement is worthless. Booked exits instantly — nobody gets the fall email twice after booking. The IAQ upsell rides inside this flow as a segment, not a separate campaign: homes on record with allergy notes or older systems get the IAQ add-on block in the pre-book email — the guide names it as a campaign idea; the wiring is the segment. The honest mechanics: this is the single most important email job in HVAC — it fills the slow calendar with booked, planned work, and every booked tune-up is an inspection appointment for the replacement drip. Build it second because a shop whose techs sit idle in April is paying for the tool with zero return while the spring window closes.

3. The maintenance-plan sell-and-renewal ladder (trigger: plan signup, and every service visit)

Trigger: the customer signs the maintenance agreement — at the job, at the estimate, or from the website — and gets the plan-member tag; the sell sequence also fires after any service visit for non-members. Sequence, the sell half: email one after the visit — the priority-scheduling pitch: when the AC dies in August, members jump the line. Email two, a few days later — the plain-English explainer: the two tune-ups, the repair discount, the breakdown math (the agreement costs ~$180–$300/year, estimate; one avoided emergency visit pays for it). Email three — the member testimonial, one real customer, one sentence. Then it stops — three emails, not a siege. Sequence, the renewal half: starts 60 days before expiration — one reminder a month — with the “your plan lapses” nudge at 14 days. The plan-member tag drives the whole renewal half automatically. The branching gates: signed exits the sell sequence instantly — the flow must never pitch someone who already bought. Lapsed at renewal enters flow #7’s dormant track with the plan tag, not the general one. A service call mid-plan pauses the sell emails but keeps the renewal schedule. The honest mechanics: the guide calls the agreement base the shop’s most valuable list segment, and the ladder is why: recurring revenue in a break-fix business, and customers on agreements book more, replace with you when the time comes, and never shop competitors in a panic. The 14-day nudge exists because customers do not renew what they forgot they had.

4. The aging-system replacement drip (trigger: install-year tag hits the 10-year mark)

Trigger: the install-year tag on the customer record crosses 10 years (the guide’s trigger point — the system’s final third). Sequence: the patient sequence, one email a month, and it runs for years. Email 1 — the honest education piece: “your system is entering its final third; here’s what typically fails first.” Email 2 — the repair-vs-replace decision math: the 50% rule (when a repair costs half a new system, it’s replacement time), in the customer’s numbers not the invoice’s. Email 3 — financing and the comfort-and-efficiency angle: the monthly-payment framing that turns $10,000 into a number a budget can hold. Then the rotation repeats with seasonal angles — one email a month, never a blast. The branching gates: this is where the drip earns its keep. A failed inspection during a flow-#2 tune-up visit — the tech flags the dying compressor, the cracked heat exchanger — accelerates the contact into the active version of this drip immediately; the guide’s diagnosis moment is the trigger, not the calendar. An estimate requested exits to flow #5’s estimate drip instantly — this flow hands off, it does not duplicate. A replacement booked exits the whole sequence (and re-tags the install year to the new date — the 12-to-15-year clock restarts, and flow #6’s onboarding begins). Plan members stay in the drip but get the member version — the agreement’s repair discount inside the financing math. The honest mechanics: the highest-dollar campaign in the trade. One converted replacement ($7,000–$15,000, estimate) pays for the email tool for a decade. The drip is patient because the buying cycle is — nobody replaces a system from one email; the sequence keeps you the default when the compressor dies in July.

5. The estimate-follow-up drip (trigger: estimate sent, no job booked)

Trigger: a replacement or repair estimate goes out and no job is booked within 24 hours. Sequence: email one at T+1 day — the objection-handler: restate the problem the tech found, the scope, and the price, in the customer’s words not the invoice’s. Most unbooked HVAC estimates are not price objections; they are “I need to think about it” and “I want a second quote” — and for replacements, the real objection is almost always the number’s size, so the financing framing from flow #4 appears here for the first time as an alternative, not a discount. Email two at T+4 days — the seasonal tie-in: why this work is better done now (the furnace before the cold snap, the AC before the heat) than later. Email three at T+10 days — the final nudge: the estimate’s expiry date, one line, the booking link. The branching gates: a booked job exits instantly — the flow must never email someone who already said yes. A replied “not now” moves the contact to flow #4’s aging-system drip (if the system is 10+) or flow #7’s dormant track, not to silence. A second quote mentioned enters the honest-comparison branch: one email, the questions to ask any bidder, no trash-talk — judgment, labeled: in a trust trade, the shop that teaches the customer how to shop wins the callback. The honest mechanics: estimates die on the kitchen table, not in the inbox — the customer asked for the number, which means the intent existed. The drip does not discount the work; it answers the hesitation. Build this fifth because a shop that books 1 more replacement estimate a month out of the ones already written is buying the whole playbook’s tool bill with one flow.

6. The new-system onboarding (trigger: install recorded, new install year)

Trigger: the install is recorded and the install-year tag is set to the new date. Sequence: email one at day 3 — the congratulations and the what-you-bought recap: the system model, the efficiency rating, the warranty terms in plain words — because nobody reads the packet the installer leaves on the counter. Email two at day 14 — the warranty-registration nudge: register the parts warranty (the one customers lose money on by forgetting), one link, one screenshot of where to click. Email three at day 30 — the filter schedule and the thermostat/app setup: the filter size, the change interval, the app login the installer walked past. Email four at day 90 — the comfort check-in: how’s the system running, the one-question satisfaction check, and — only if the answer is good — the review ask and the referral ask (flow #7’s track, entered from here instead of flow #1 for installs). The branching gates: a problem reported pauses the sequence instantly — the check-in never lands on a customer waiting for a callback. Warranty registered exits email two’s branch (the tag updates; the nudge never repeats). The plan-member offer lands at day 30, not day 3 — pitch the agreement after they love the system, not while they’re still nervous about the bill. The honest mechanics: the sale is the most fragile moment in HVAC — five figures spent, buyer’s remorse peaking, the customer Googling whether they overpaid. Onboarding is not marketing; it is retention engineering. A customer who registers the warranty, changes the filter on schedule, and gets the day-90 check-in replaces their next system with you in 12 to 15 years and refers the neighbor this summer.

7. The dormant win-back and the referral track (trigger: no job in 18+ months; review completed)

Trigger: a past customer goes 18 months with no job and no plan activity — or, for the referral half, the customer completes the review flow (#1) or the onboarding check-in (#6). Sequence, the win-back half: email one — “we’re still your HVAC company”: the shop is still here, the number, the one-line reminder of the last visit (“we serviced your system in 2024” — the tag makes it personal). Email two, two weeks later — whichever flow-#2 shoulder campaign is live, personalized to their record. Email three, two weeks after that — the last call: the honest “we’ll stop checking in” note. After email three with no response, the contact moves to the annual-only cadence — the holiday card and the big seasonal blast, not the monthly sends. The system-age branch: a dormant contact whose system is 10+ enters flow #4’s aging drip instead of the win-back — the guide’s point stands, a one-time filter-change customer from four years ago belongs in a win-back campaign, not the tune-up blast, and an aging system belongs in the replacement drip, not the tune-up blast. Sequence, the referral half: one email at T+7 days after the good review — the neighbor angle: “we just serviced a system on [street/neighborhood]” (the tag makes it true), the referral ask, the one-line incentive if you run one (the discount on the next tune-up is the standard; state the terms). Then it STOPS — one ask, not a drip — and — this is the part shops skip — the report-back when a referral books (“your neighbor booked with us, thank you”), which is what makes the second referral happen. Never ask a customer who left a negative review — the complaint-suppression branch from flow #1 applies here too. The honest mechanics: the list is already paid for — every past customer on it cost a truck roll to acquire — so win-back is the cheapest revenue on the account. And nobody picks an HVAC company from an ad; they pick the shop their neighbor recommended. The referral is the highest-trust acquisition in the trade, and the automation’s job is only to make the ask happen at the moment trust is highest.

The build order, with the money math

Build in this order: the review engine (flow #1), the shoulder-season pre-book (flow #2), the plan ladder (flow #3), the aging-system drip (flow #4), the estimate drip (flow #5), the new-system onboarding (flow #6), the win-back and referral track (flow #7). The ranking is HVAC economics: reviews win the phone call that starts every job; the pre-book fills the slow calendar that feeds the replacement drip; the agreement is the recurring machine; the drip sells the five-figure replacements; the estimate drip, the onboarding, and the win-back protect the revenue the first four create. One line of judgment, labeled: most shops build the seasonal blasts first because they are the easiest to understand, and leave the review engine — the flow that makes every future customer cheaper to win — for “later,” which is the most expensive procrastination in the trade. The estimate drip second-to-fifth for the same reason: quotes already written are the warmest revenue on the books.

Five mistakes that cost more than the tool

  • Building the blasts before the review engine. The seasonal campaigns are the easiest flow to understand, so they get built first — and the review engine, the flow that makes every future customer cheaper to win, waits for “later.” Reviews first, always.
  • The third review ask. Two asks is persistence; three is pestering. The flow stops at two, and the complaint-suppression branch is not optional — one automated review request to an angry customer costs more than the review was worth.
  • Emailing plan members the paid version of what their agreement covers. The shoulder-season pre-book suppresses plan members whose tune-ups are included. Break this rule once and the member learns the agreement is worthless — the most expensive email a shop can send.
  • Double-sending what the field software already sends. ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, and FieldEdge already send confirmations, on-my-way texts, and invoice reminders. The customer who gets the email tool’s “thanks for your visit” and the field software’s “thanks for your visit” reads both as spam.
  • Asking for the referral before the review. The referral ask lands at peak trust — right after the good review or the day-90 check-in, not before it. Sequence matters: review first (flow #1), referral second (flow #7). Reversed, both asks feel transactional.
